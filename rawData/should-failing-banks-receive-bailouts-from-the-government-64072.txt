Discussion Name:
Should failing banks receive bailouts from the government?

1. Thesis:
Failing banks should receive bailouts from the government.

  1.1. Con:
  It is not the responsibility of the state to bail out private companies.

    1.1.1. Pro:
    Private banks are responsible for their own financial decisions and the situations they land in as a consequence.

      1.1.1.1. Con:
      The US government and its regulatory bodies played a role in creating the 2008 financial crisis and, therefore, shouldered part of the responsibility for its consequences.

        1.1.1.1.1. Pro:
        The US government's interpretation of, and subsequent repeal of, the Glass–Steagall Act [1] was instrumental in driving the 2008 financial crisis.

          1.1.1.1.1.1. Pro:
          The Gramm–Leach–Bliley Act [2], by repealing parts of the Glass-Steagall Act, permitted the merger of commercial banks, investment banks [3], and insurers, while not granting regulatory oversight to the SEC [4] or other agencies for major investment bank holdings.

          1.1.1.1.1.2. Pro:
          Federal regulators interpreted provisions of the Glass-Steagall Act leniently, allowing banks to engage in riskier behaviors (pp 13-14 [5]).

          1.1.1.1.1.3. Con:
          The 2008 financial crisis was not solely driven by the US government; bankers also bear a share of the responsibility due to their lobbying efforts [6] that led to the repeal of some sections of the Glass-Steagall Act.

        1.1.1.1.2. Con:
        The US government and its regulators could not have anticipated the 2008 financial crash.

          1.1.1.1.2.1. Pro:
          Mortgage-backed securities [7] and collateralized debt obligations [8], due to their complexity, masked the true risks and eluded full comprehension by regulators until after the financial crash.

            1.1.1.1.2.1.1. Con:
            Financial regulators, by virtue of their position, had a responsibility to keep themselves well-versed in any complex financial instruments that could affect the economy. Regulatory ignorance is not a defensible excuse.

          1.1.1.1.2.2. Con:
          Some economists and financial experts, such as Raghuram Rajan [9], had warned about the risks building up in the financial system.

          1.1.1.1.2.3. Con:
          Even if they could not have anticipated the specifics of the crash, regulators were to blame for allowing banks to massively reduce their liquidity [10] by reducing the amount of capital they held.

      1.1.1.2. Pro:
      Operating in largely deregulated environments [11], private banks have the freedom to make their decisions, and it is fair that they reap the rewards or consequences of that risk.

        1.1.1.2.1. Con:
        No market is entirely deregulated; decision-making by private banks is typically bound by government regulatory authorities.

          1.1.1.2.1.1. Pro:
          Regulatory bodies, including central banks and financial authorities, supervise [12] banking decisions. Therefore, assigning complete blame to banks for their actions may not be entirely warranted.

          1.1.1.2.1.2. Con:
          Regulations constrain banking behaviour, but do not determine it. Holding a regulator accountable for bank failure would be akin to holding a seat belt accountable for bad driving.

    1.1.2. Con:
    Private companies play a foundational role in the economy; therefore, the government must protect them and bail them out if necessary.

      1.1.2.1. Pro:
      Private companies contribute towards the state's revenue by paying high taxes [13], and thus deserve to be bailed out.

        1.1.2.1.1. Con:
        For several years, private companies in the US have exploited loopholes in tax regulations [14] to legally avoid paying taxes.

        1.1.2.1.2. Pro:
        As of 2023, banks in the UK are required to pay between 25% - 33% of corporation tax [15] depending on their declared taxable profits.

          1.1.2.1.2.1. Con:
          Banks in the UK have been shown to use European tax havens to avoid paying taxes [16].

        1.1.2.1.3. Con:
        These companies pay high taxes because of the incredible profits that they accrue - they do not create a moral imperative to bail them out.

          1.1.2.1.3.1. Pro:
          Companies which are struggling enough to require bailouts are likely not going to have to pay high taxes at the time of their downturn (p. 1 [17]).

        1.1.2.1.4. Pro:
        In addition to direct taxes like income, corporate and wealth taxes, private companies substantially contribute to an economy by bearing the burden of indirect taxes such as VAT, excise tax, and customs duties (p. 7 [18]).

          1.1.2.1.4.1. Con:
          Companies often shift the burden of taxes [19], especially indirect ones, onto consumers.

        1.1.2.1.5. Con:
        Private companies pay annual taxes in the tens of billions of dollars, whereas their bailouts have cost up to $700 billion, ultimately making them more trouble than they're worth (p. 158 [20])

      1.1.2.2. Pro:
      Private investment is the primary driver of economic growth and employment in most economies (p. 1 [21]).

        1.1.2.2.1. Pro:
        In developing nations, the private sector employs about 90% [22] of the workforce.

        1.1.2.2.2. Con:
        While the private sector might employ the majority of people, banks, as a subsection, are not the primary employers [23].

        1.1.2.2.3. Con:
        The biggest private companies in the US are not banks [24].

      1.1.2.3. Con:
      Not all private companies offer essential services or generate positive economic value; hence, they shouldn't be painted with the same brush.

        1.1.2.3.1. Con:
        In developing countries, private sector provision of essential services like water, energy, financial services and housing can help curb poverty [25].

      1.1.2.4. Con:
      The government does not have the ability or the finances to bail out all failing private companies. For it to select a few to bail out would be unfair.

        1.1.2.4.1. Pro:
        Many countries worldwide are dealing with budget deficits [26], leading to a shortage of resources for rescuing all struggling private enterprises.

    1.1.3. Con:
    Governments frequently step in to assist entities facing imminent default on their debts. There is no reason why this should not extend to private companies as well.

      1.1.3.1. Pro:
      The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) insures people against loss of deposits up to $250,000 [27] in the event of a bank failure.

        1.1.3.1.1. Pro:
        From 2008 to 2010, the FDIC Deposit Insurance Fund plummeted from $50 billion to a deficit of $20 billion due to its coverage of depositors during bank failures (p. 4 [28]).

        1.1.3.1.2. Con:
        FDIC insurance payments are not equivalent to bailouts as banks themselves pay [29] towards the deposit insurance coverage, rather than rely on taxpayer money.

      1.1.3.2. Pro:
      In the aftermath of a natural disaster, many governments are legally bound to aid victims by providing them with loan guarantees and other benefits [30] that facilitate their ability to settle their debts.

        1.1.3.2.1. Con:
        Following a natural disaster, most governments are already stretched thin in terms of resources [31]. Offering loan guarantees to aid victims in repaying their debts further worsens the strain.

      1.1.3.3. Con:
      Companies are motivated to take risks [32] in order to earn profits, making them fundamentally distinct from other actors that the government steps in to aid.

        1.1.3.3.1. Con:
        Depositors in the US, covered [33] by Federal Deposit Insurance Corp. (FDIC), also accrue profits in the form of interest [34].

        1.1.3.3.2. Pro:
        The guarantee of eventual government intervention in the case of bailout could skew a company's risk-management decision making and have deleterious effects on financial behaviour.

    1.1.4. Pro:
    The public's money, extracted from them through taxation, should not be spent to help protect the interests of private companies.

      1.1.4.1. Pro:
      Bailed-out private banks often face subsequent failures, resulting in substantial losses for taxpayers.

        1.1.4.1.1. Pro:
        Bailed-out banks are arguably more likely to fail because they know that the government will step in to save them if they take too large of a risk and might fail.

          1.1.4.1.1.1. Con:
          It is improbable that the government would bail out banks for a second time, given the potential unpopularity it could face with the voters.

            1.1.4.1.1.1.1. Con:
            In situations where governments anticipate widespread unemployment and an economic downturn resulting from a bank failure, they may opt to provide a second bailout, even at the potential cost of facing public discontent.

          1.1.4.1.1.2. Pro:
          Credit Suisse, which was bailed out in 2008 [35], had to be rescued again by the Swiss government in 2023 with a 260 billion franc [36] bailout due to its poor financial performance and decision-making.

        1.1.4.1.2. Con:
        Subsequent failures can be avoided by having strict government oversight to restrict the risks that these banks take in future.

          1.1.4.1.2.1. Pro:
          If banks persist in pursuing risky investments after a bailout, regulators can take stringent measures, including potentially shutting down the institution.

            1.1.4.1.2.1.1. Pro:
            In 2023, New York regulators closed the operations of Signature Bank [37], citing its activities to be a systemic risk to the stability of the financial system.

              1.1.4.1.2.1.1.1. Pro:
              As of December 2022, Signature Bank held an estimated $79.5 billion in uninsured deposits [38], potentially posing a risk of a widespread bank run [39] if it were to continue operating in this manner.

              1.1.4.1.2.1.1.2. Con:
              The FDIC's inadequate oversight [40] of Signature Bank's operations directly led to its closure.

              1.1.4.1.2.1.1.3. Pro:
              Signature Bank faced notable liquidity risk due to an asset composition that deviated heavily from industry norms [41].

            1.1.4.1.2.1.2. Con:
            The possibility of institutional closures can undermine customer confidence in the banking sector, potentially impeding its growth.

              1.1.4.1.2.1.2.1. Pro:
              Former major depositors might redirect their funds [42] to more secure options like money market funds [43] or short-dated bonds [44], constraining the bank's operational liquidity.

              1.1.4.1.2.1.2.2. Con:
              Customers may have more confidence in the banking sector when they know that the state is involved in preventing overly rash behaviour.

          1.1.4.1.2.2. Con:
          If banks face stricter local regulations, they may be motivated to relocate to jurisdictions with more lenient regulatory environments.

            1.1.4.1.2.2.1. Con:
            Strict regulation [45] isn't necessarily a compelling reason for banks to relocate, as evidenced by the continued expansion [46] of foreign private banks in China.

              1.1.4.1.2.2.1.1. Con:
              The Chinese economy is likely a unique case due to its massive size and continually high growth forecasts [47]. It is not a useful example by which to measure general financial behaviour.

              1.1.4.1.2.2.1.2. Pro:
              Between 2012 and 2021, commercial private banks in China experienced a robust increase [48] in their assets under management.

            1.1.4.1.2.2.2. Pro:
            Increased regulation can elevate a bank's operational costs [49], prompting the bank to seek ways to operate outside of such regulation in order to reduce costs and boost profits.

        1.1.4.1.3. Pro:
        The Troubled Asset Relief Program [50] (TARP) was unable to resurrect failing or underperforming businesses.

          1.1.4.1.3.1. Pro:
          Despite receiving funds [51] from the Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP), Central Pacific Financial [52] ultimately failed, resulting in a $60 million loss [53] for taxpayers.

          1.1.4.1.3.2. Con:
          As a direct result of government bailouts in 2008, Chrysler and General Motors [54] were able to remain solvent. Today, they are among the world's largest automakers, employing hundreds of thousands of people [55].

            1.1.4.1.3.2.1. Con:
            Chrysler has not been successful as an auto company. It was eventually acquired by Fiat in 2014 [56] for a valuation of $10.3 billion, down from $37 billion in the 90s.

            1.1.4.1.3.2.2. Pro:
            Both Chrysler [57] and General Motors [58] generate billions of dollars in annual revenue, making substantial contributions to the GDP of the US.

      1.1.4.2. Con:
      Since spending money on companies is often the most effective way that public money can be spent, the state has every right to spend it on them.

        1.1.4.2.1. Pro:
        Governments already do this by providing subsidies [59] to struggling companies/sectors of the economy to make them more competitive.

        1.1.4.2.2. Con:
        Governments are not necessarily the best entities to determine what is best for the economy.

          1.1.4.2.2.1. Con:
          Governments have departments and experts dedicated to economic research, financial planning, and policy development, enabling the optimal allocation of funds.

          1.1.4.2.2.2. Pro:
          Government decisions are often influenced by corporate lobbying [60] prioritizing the interests of industries over those of consumers.

      1.1.4.3. Pro:
      State funds being used for bailouts prevent resources being directed towards other important public goods.

        1.1.4.3.1. Con:
        Governments not only recoup the full amount of bailouts, but also generate a significant profit from the extended loans. These profits can be utilized to enhance the provision of public goods.

          1.1.4.3.1.1. Pro:
          The government not only imposed an annual 1% guarantee fee on a $1.2 billion loan, but also realized an additional $311 million from the 1979 bailout of American automaker Chrysler [61] (p. 500 [62]).

          1.1.4.3.1.2. Pro:
          Out of the $245 billion in taxpayer funds allocated to stabilize over 700 banks from 2008 to 2009, the US government has reclaimed $30.1 billion above its initial investment [63].

          1.1.4.3.1.3. Con:
          OneUnited Bank, which received $12 million as part of the TARP bailout investment [64], has not yet repaid the funds or made dividend payments [65] to the US government.

          1.1.4.3.1.4. Pro:
          The Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP) [66], designed to rescue America's largest banks, has generated a profit of approximately $1 billion for the government [67].

            1.1.4.3.1.4.1. Con:
            The actual profits are likely much lower, as the $1 billion figure is not adjusted for inflation [68].

            1.1.4.3.1.4.2. Con:
            Considering the return on TARP, experts contend that [69] American taxpayers were not fairly compensated by the banks that received bailouts.

          1.1.4.3.1.5. Con:
          Fannie [70] and Freddie [71], the mortgage giants who received a $191 billion bailout [72], have not repaid any of the principal amount to the US Treasury.

            1.1.4.3.1.5.1. Con:
            To date, the US Treasury has garnered $301 billion in dividends [73] from their investments in Fannie and Freddie, comprising a substantial portion of their revenue.

            1.1.4.3.1.5.2. Pro:
            Per the 2012 agreement [74], Fannie and Freddie were required to make quarterly payments to the US Treasury. However, as their net worth did not exceed the specified capital reserve, these payments have not yet been made.

        1.1.4.3.2. Pro:
        The 2008 bailout incurred a cost of $498 billion for the US government [75]. Redirecting this sum towards social welfare programs could have potentially made significant strides in alleviating poverty [76].

          1.1.4.3.2.1. Con:
          Not all costs of the bailout were directly borne by the US government; some were offset through directed mergers and liquidations.

            1.1.4.3.2.1.1. Pro:
            The financial collapse of the First Republic Bank was averted [77] through a $30 billion infusion of liquidity from 11 US banks, along with JPMorgan Chase acquiring the majority of the bank's assets.

            1.1.4.3.2.1.2. Pro:
            To bail out its depositors and shareholders, the US Federal Reserve orchestrated the sale of Bear Stearns [78], a major investment bank, to JP Morgan in 2008 (p. 514 [79]).

            1.1.4.3.2.1.3. Con:
            Mergers and liquidations represent a minority [80] of bailout activity - most of it is government spending.

          1.1.4.3.2.2. Con:
          The US government's 2008 bailout preserved approximately 1.5 million jobs [81], and can thus be viewed as an investment in the welfare of its citizens.

            1.1.4.3.2.2.1. Con:
            These jobs were saved by government intervention to prop up a failing industry. It is likely that the public funds used to save the banks could have generated even more jobs if spent more efficiently.

      1.1.4.4. Con:
      Governments are entrusted with the responsibility of gathering and allocating public funds, granting them the authority to utilize this revenue as they see fit.

        1.1.4.4.1. Pro:
        In democracies, governments are elected by the people, and thus they have the authority and responsibility to manage public funds according to the mandate provided [82] by the electorate.

          1.1.4.4.1.1. Pro:
          A government is motivated to uphold its election promises when deciding on the question of bailouts to enhance its chances of reelection [83].

          1.1.4.4.1.2. Con:
          Leaders and parties are often elected based on broad election promises or simply due to the unpopularity of their opponents, rather than on specific policy matters such as bailouts.

        1.1.4.4.2. Con:
        While governments have the mandate to use public funds, there may be inadequate mechanisms in place to ensure transparency and hold officials accountable for misappropriation.

          1.1.4.4.2.1. Pro:
          Leaders who have surpassed their term limits, where they exist, cannot realistically be held accountable via re-election.

          1.1.4.4.2.2. Con:
          Most jurisdictions worldwide have independent judicial systems [84] that can hold public officials accountable for misappropriating funds or using them against the public interest.

  1.2. Pro:
  Failing banks have the potential to negatively affect the stability of financial markets worldwide.

    1.2.1. Con:
    It is not the role of a national government to maintain the stability of international financial markets at its own expense.

      1.2.1.1. Con:
      As the volatility of international financial markets directly impacts investor confidence, national governments are better served by ensuring their stability (p. 1 [85]).

        1.2.1.1.1. Pro:
        Diminished investor confidence would reduce their inclination to take risks and expand their investment portfolios.

      1.2.1.2. Pro:
      Nation states are accountable to their own people, not to those of other nations.

        1.2.1.2.1. Pro:
        Nation states derive their authority and legitimacy [86] from the citizens they govern.

      1.2.1.3. Con:
      National governments derive benefits from economic growth facilitated by global financial markets and thus should prioritize their stability.

        1.2.1.3.1. Pro:
        Local firms tap into global financial markets to secure capital, infusing investments into the local economy (p. 26 [87]).

        1.2.1.3.2. Con:
        As national governments diligently work to mitigate asymmetric information in global financial markets [88], they are not necessarily obligated to prioritize them.

    1.2.2. Pro:
    The collapse of Lehman Brothers in the US in 2008 had global spillover effects.

      1.2.2.1. Pro:
      The collapse of Lehman Brothers directly impacted the Japanese economy and stock market [89], resulting in significant losses.

        1.2.2.1.1. Pro:
        The Sumitomo Mitsui Financial Group suffered a net loss of $4 billion [90], primarily attributed to substantial expenses incurred for writing off loans, a sharp decline in stock prices, and the global economic slowdown.

        1.2.2.1.2. Pro:
        The Japanese economy slipped into recession [91] due to low demand and a sharp decline in exports [92] to the United States, where individual purchasing power was significantly reduced [93].

        1.2.2.1.3. Con:
        The Bank of Japan helped alleviate the negative financial impact by purchasing shares of other local banks, [94] thereby reducing their exposure to global stock market volatility.

      1.2.2.2. Pro:
      After the bankruptcy of Lehman Brothers, the Korean Won experienced a 19.2% depreciation against the US dollar (p. 2 [95]).

        1.2.2.2.1. Con:
        Rigorous market intervention policies, such as those implemented in China and Hong Kong (p. 3 [96]), could have effectively averted exchange rate depreciation.

        1.2.2.2.2. Pro:
        The devaluation of the Korean Won resulted in the country's current account balance falling into a 9 billion USD deficit (p. 3 [97]).

      1.2.2.3. Con:
      The collapse of large financial institutions is less likely to have a global impact now, as governments worldwide have learned and adapted since 2008.

        1.2.2.3.1. Pro:
        The US Congress passed the Dodd-Frank Act [98] in 2010 to prevent excessive risk-taking in the financial sector.

        1.2.2.3.2. Pro:
        There's a global shift away [99] from using the US Dollar as a reserve and exchange currency to reduce the world market's sensitivity to the US economy.

        1.2.2.3.3. Con:
        The collapse of SVB in 2023 [100] negatively impacted global markets, indicating that measures to shield against events in the US have been ineffective.

          1.2.2.3.3.1. Pro:
          The GCC equity markets declined [101] after the collapse of SVB.

          1.2.2.3.3.2. Con:
          Unlike the 2008 financial crash, the collapse of SVB did not have a significant impact on global currency markets [102].

    1.2.3. Pro:
    Banks play a crucial role in facilitating international payments [103], so their failure can lead to disruptions in the flow of cross-border transactions.

      1.2.3.1. Pro:
      The 2008 financial market collapse led to a shortage of US dollar liquidity in the global economy (p. 213 [104]), causing a disruption in international trade flows.

        1.2.3.1.1. Pro:
        A shortage of US dollars prompted firms to accumulate liquidity as a precautionary measure [105] to cover potential future payment requirements.

        1.2.3.1.2. Con:
        The disruption in international trade flows was short lived as the Federal Reserve expanded US dollar liquidity swap lines [106] to support smooth market functioning and the flow of credit.

      1.2.3.2. Con:
      It is unlikely that all global banks would collapse simultaneously; therefore, necessary foreign payments can still be made through alternative banking channels.

        1.2.3.2.1. Pro:
        The presence of non-bank financial intermediaries [107] is poised to mitigate the risk of a global financial collapse, even in scenarios where a few banks may default and face challenges in processing essential foreign payments.

  1.3. Con:
  Bailing out banks establishes a bad precedent, which is likely to lead to riskier investments by banks in the future.

    1.3.1. Pro:
    Given the assurance of a safety net in case of failure, senior banking officers might be incentivized to pursue high-risk investments [108].

      1.3.1.1. Pro:
      If high-risk investments prove successful, they can be highly profitable for senior banking officials, resulting in substantial gains and corresponding bonuses [109].

        1.3.1.1.1. Pro:
        Shareholders often expect aggressive growth and substantial returns [110]. Delivering on these expectations through high-risk, high-reward investments can justify significant bonuses for senior banking officials.

        1.3.1.1.2. Con:
        Global compensation patterns are changing to reward long-term [111] growth and success rather than short-term gains.

      1.3.1.2. Con:
      After receiving bailouts, banks are less likely to engage in risky investments as they are subject to heightened regulatory scrutiny [112].

        1.3.1.2.1. Con:
        Despite regulatory oversight, banks can still employ valid justifications to postpone or refrain from divulging data [113] to regulators.

        1.3.1.2.2. Pro:
        In the aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis, regulators have shifted their focus towards incentivizing appropriate staff conduct to foster elevated ethical standards within financial institutions (p. 3 [114]).

    1.3.2. Con:
    -> See 1.1.4.1.2.1.

    1.3.3. Pro:
    A culture of excessive risk-taking exists in investment banks, which may be exacerbated if there is an expectation of a bailout.

      1.3.3.1. Pro:
      Lehman Brothers, which went bankrupt in 2008 [115], is said to have rewarded risk-taking behavior among its staff.

        1.3.3.1.1. Con:
        After witnessing that Goldman Sachs and Lehman Brothers were fined billions of dollars [116] for profiting from high-risk behavior, it is unlikely that banks will be eager to reward such risks.

        1.3.3.1.2. Pro:
        A study [117] found that bankers, on average, are more likely to act dishonestly to maximize profits compared to professionals in other industries.

      1.3.3.2. Con:
      There has been immense pressure on financial institutions to closely monitor culture and subsequently reduce the spread of such a 'risk-taking' culture (p. 27 [118]).

        1.3.3.2.1. Con:
        In light of the 2023 collapse of Credit Suisse and SVB, it is challenging to assert [119] that banking culture has genuinely improved since the lessons of 2008.

        1.3.3.2.2. Pro:
        In 2012, the UK established the Parliamentary Commission on Banking Standards [120] to investigate the prevailing culture within banks and recommend improvements, which has informed subsequent regulation.

      1.3.3.3. Con:
      Factors such as deregulation in the financial services industry also foster risk-taking behavior in banks (p. 32 [121]). Thus, this behavior is likely to persist even without the expectation of a bailout.

        1.3.3.3.1. Con:
        While multiple factors may incentivize risk-taking behavior, the absence of a safety net in the form of a bailout can, at the very least, reduce this incentive if not eliminate it.

    1.3.4. Con:
    Accountability among bank leadership is crucial in preventing poor decision-making, regardless of the presence or absence of a bailout.

      1.3.4.1. Pro:
      The lack of accountability for banking leadership in 2008 continues to encourage risky behavior in financial institutions.

        1.3.4.1.1. Pro:
        Only one financial executive was jailed [122] following the 2008 financial crash, which affected millions of people worldwide.

          1.3.4.1.1.1. Con:
          After Serageldin's imprisonment, the Justice Department shifted its strategy [123] towards reaching settlements instead of pursuing prison sentences, aiming to recover from the economic repercussions of 2008.

          1.3.4.1.1.2. Pro:
          Among the banking officers involved in financial concealment [124], Serageldin was the sole individual to receive a 30-month jail sentence.

        1.3.4.1.2. Con:
        After the 2008 financial crisis, banking leaders involved in the turmoil experienced a heightened sense of professional accountability, which is likely to act as a deterrent against risky behavior.

          1.3.4.1.2.1. Pro:
          Richard S. Fuld [125], the last CEO of Lehman Brothers, was deprived of his expected golden parachute [126] upon exit from the company.

          1.3.4.1.2.2. Pro:
          Morgan Stanley's CEO John Mack was made to step down and relinquish his position as chair of the board [127] following the investment bank's bailout.

          1.3.4.1.2.3. Pro:
          Bank of America's CEO, Ken Lewis, was required to pay $10 million [128] to settle a State of New York investigation.

          1.3.4.1.2.4. Con:
          Despite playing a significant role in the 2008 financial crisis, Lloyd Blankfein [129] remained the CEO of Goldman Sachs until 2018.

          1.3.4.1.2.5. Con:
          Jamie Dimon [130] retained his position as the CEO of JPMorgan after resolving legal issues related to purchases made during the 2008 crisis era.

      1.3.4.2. Con:
      Following the 2008 crisis, most banks faced substantial fines [131], which can be viewed as a form of accountability.

        1.3.4.2.1. Con:
        The total fines paid by American banks after 2008 are negligible [132] compared to their overall earnings.

        1.3.4.2.2. Pro:
        Since the 2008 financial crisis, financial institutions worldwide, including those in the US, have incurred fines totaling $321 billion [133].

  1.4. Pro:
  Failing banks represent a large threat to the security of the economy and must be rescued for everyone's benefit.

    1.4.1. Pro:
    Bailing out banks can help avert the threat of recession.

      1.4.1.1. Pro:
      The added liquidity from a bailout allows banks to sustain themselves while seeking credit to maintain their operations.

        1.4.1.1.1. Con:
        Banks which have required bailouts are likely to have damaged customer confidence to a point that would adversely affect their future liquidity.

          1.4.1.1.1.1. Con:
          Although a bailout can initially undermine trust, it does not imply that it cannot be restored. Bailed out banks are likely to be more proactive in rectifying the underlying issues [134], demonstrating a commitment to regaining customer confidence.

        1.4.1.1.2. Pro:
        Bailouts ensure that banks can maintain their capacity to provide loans or credit to consumers, thereby averting a potential credit crunch in the economy.

          1.4.1.1.2.1. Pro:
          Banks serve as a primary source of capital for companies across various industries [135].

          1.4.1.1.2.2. Con:
          Bailouts result in less favorable loan terms for the borrowers (p. 6 [136]).

            1.4.1.1.2.2.1. Pro:
            In a post-bailout economy, obtaining a loan could become even more costly due to elevated interest rates [137].

            1.4.1.1.2.2.2. Pro:
            Unfavorable loan terms for borrowers have a detrimental impact on their financial well-being, thereby impeding economic recovery and growth (p. 1 [138]).

            1.4.1.1.2.2.3. Con:
            Banks that receive bailouts are likely to offer borrowers more favorable loan terms, aiming to retain customers and safeguard their operations from the negative impact of a tarnished reputation (p. 6 [139]).

      1.4.1.2. Con:
      In a period of uncertainty, such as when a large bank needs a bailout, a recession may be inevitable.

        1.4.1.2.1. Pro:
        The US government bailouts in the aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis were not able to dampen the unfolding recession.

          1.4.1.2.1.1. Pro:
          In 2008, approximately 3.8 million [140] Americans lost their homes due to the financial crash.

          1.4.1.2.1.2. Pro:
          In 2008, the US unemployment rate reached a high of 10% [141].

            1.4.1.2.1.2.1. Con:
            Economists have estimated that, without the bailout, unemployment could have gone as high as 16% [142].

            1.4.1.2.1.2.2. Pro:
            The 2008 financial crisis led to an unprecedented doubling of unemployment rates [143] in the US.

          1.4.1.2.1.3. Con:
          The bailout package helped stabilize US stock markets [144] and eventually paved the way for a historic bull run [145].

            1.4.1.2.1.3.1. Con:
            The 2009-2020 bull market was a consequence of low interest rates and quantitative easing [146].

          1.4.1.2.1.4. Con:
          Absent the bailouts, economists believe that the recession would have been much worse [147] - going on for twice as long and shrinking the economy by 14%.

        1.4.1.2.2. Con:
        Bailouts, even if they don't prevent a larger recession, can be effective in minimizing harm within targeted sectors during tough times.

          1.4.1.2.2.1. Pro:
          The European Union, through bailouts, supported struggling businesses [148] during the pandemic to prevent bankruptcy and job losses.

            1.4.1.2.2.1.1. Con:
            Many governments [149] around the world announced broad stimulus packages across various sectors of the economy at the onset of the pandemic. Therefore, this cannot be compared to the targeted and exclusive bailouts for banks.

          1.4.1.2.2.2. Pro:
          -> See 1.1.4.1.3.2.

          1.4.1.2.2.3. Con:
          Bailouts can render said targeted sectors noncompetitive by favoring certain companies.

            1.4.1.2.2.3.1. Pro:
            Ryanair successfully lodged a case against the European Commission regarding its bailout to Lufthansa, citing its uncompetitive nature [150].

            1.4.1.2.2.3.2. Con:
            Without government bailouts, larger firms might acquire underperforming ones during a slump, leading to market monopolization and reduced competition.

              1.4.1.2.2.3.2.1. Con:
              Acquisitions of underperforming firms are infrequent, with only 4.5% of US firms in the lowest performance quintile being taken over each year (p. 1 [151]).

              1.4.1.2.2.3.2.2. Pro:
              When a company's performance is subpar, larger firms may be inclined to swiftly step in [152] and leverage their management expertise and value-addition capabilities.

              1.4.1.2.2.3.2.3. Con:
              The instances of market monopolization and uncompetitive acquisitions are prevented by antitrust laws [153].

      1.4.1.3. Con:
      The threat of recession can be averted by encouraging banks or their lenders to refinance debts and continue operation without a bailout.

        1.4.1.3.1. Con:
        Bailouts often occur in emergencies [154], leaving little time for the time-consuming process of refinancing debts.

        1.4.1.3.2. Pro:
        Refinancing mortgages has proven to be effective in reducing default rates, thereby directly contributing to the stability of the market (pp. 2-3 [155]).

    1.4.2. Con:
    Sustaining inefficient and poorly managed companies through artificial means in the economy can result in adverse long-term consequences.

      1.4.2.1. Con:
      Since only a select few companies receive bailout funds following careful evaluation, it is improbable that inefficient or poorly managed ones will endure the crisis.

        1.4.2.1.1. Con:
        The need for a government bailout for a bank suggests that it was, to some extent, poorly managed or insolvent.

      1.4.2.2. Pro:
      Poorly managed companies are unlikely [156] to have the capacity to repay loans [157] extended as part of a bailout package.

        1.4.2.2.1. Pro:
        Inefficient operations [158] in poorly managed companies can hinder their ability to generate sufficient revenue, thereby impeding their capacity to meet debt obligations.

        1.4.2.2.2. Con:
        Companies have repaid a total of $390 billion [159] to the US government from bailout disbursements, suggesting that they are regaining solvency through reform.

      1.4.2.3. Pro:
      In the aftermath of a bailout, it is likely that recipient banks may be forced to adopt policy reforms that are economically unsustainable [160].

        1.4.2.3.1. Con:
        The existing information on unsustainable policy reforms post-bailout primarily revolves around nation states [161], which are fundamentally different to private banks.

        1.4.2.3.2. Pro:
        Bailed-out banks can be compelled to reallocate their assets from loans to risky sovereign debt [162], leading to an increased risk of liquidity challenges within the banking sector.

      1.4.2.4. Con:
      Post-bailout regulations can be used help improve the efficiency and management of inefficient companies (p. 493 [163]).

        1.4.2.4.1. Con:
        Due to the prevailing strong recognition of rights for private companies [164], government intervention that exerts meaningful control is limited.

        1.4.2.4.2. Pro:
        The Dodd-Frank Act, enacted after the 2008 financial crash, effectively reduced risk-taking by banks [165] and incentivized profitability through fair practices.

    1.4.3. Con:
    Government intervention via bailouts can result in regulatory oversight that may not align with the interests of the private sector.

      1.4.3.1. Con:
      Bailouts are often given in the form of loans [166], with government oversight and intervention returning to normal levels after the repayment of the loan.

        1.4.3.1.1. Pro:
        The American International Group (AIG) severed its final bailout links with the US Treasury upon repaying its loans in 2013 [167].

        1.4.3.1.2. Con:
        Contemporary bailouts predominantly involve [168] at-the-market equity offerings [169], granting the government both shares and the ability to maintain its involvement in business decisions.

      1.4.3.2. Con:
      The 2002 American steel industry bailout [170] favored traders by imposing trade barriers on foreign imports, leading to increased revenues for the steel industry.

        1.4.3.2.1. Con:
        The imposition of trade barriers in 2002 created a rift in the relationship between the US and the EU [171]. In response, the EU declared its intention to enact retaliatory tariffs [172].

        1.4.3.2.2. Con:
        Over the long run, the trade barriers enacted during the 2002 American steel bailout led to increased prices and job losses [173] across various industries.

      1.4.3.3. Pro:
      When the US government bailed out American International Group (AIG) [174], it ended up wiping out most of its equity.

        1.4.3.3.1. Pro:
        In return for $85 billion in bailout assistance, AIG was required to surrender 79.9% of its equity stake [175] to the US Federal Reserve.

        1.4.3.3.2. Con:
        Although the AIG bailout led to equity dilution, it successfully released the corporation from the difficulties it was facing [176] with its investors and credit rating agencies.

      1.4.3.4. Pro:
      When crafting a bailout package, governments have the flexibility to de-prioritize stakeholders from the private sector.

        1.4.3.4.1. Con:
        If governments arbitrarily deprioritize certain stakeholders, they are likely to lose popularity among voters.

        1.4.3.4.2. Pro:
        In the 2008 General Motors bail out [177], the government ensured that employees, as pension creditors, received higher payouts as a proportion of their claims compared to other creditors (p. 493 [178]).

      1.4.3.5. Pro:
      Bailout packages in which governments acquire equity in the firm provides them with a greater degree of control, enabling them to exert influence over the firm's operations (p. 493 [179]).

        1.4.3.5.1. Con:
        Government influence can prevent monopolistic practices [180], fostering a competitive business environment that benefits consumers through better prices and product choices.

        1.4.3.5.2. Pro:
        Government intervention can make domestic firms less competitive [181] internationally if they are subject to more regulations or controls than their foreign counterparts.

      1.4.3.6. Con:
      The absence of a bailout would be considerably worse for the private sector, as it would result in the banks in question collapsing.

        1.4.3.6.1. Con:
        The collapse of one or a few banks would be preferable to long term government oversight in the banking sector at large, as the latter is likelier to stifle growth and competition.

        1.4.3.6.2. Pro:
        Banks in the private sector have faltered in information gathering and risk management [182], leaving them significantly disadvantaged in the absence of a bailout.

    1.4.4. Pro:
    Bank failures would hurt ordinary citizens, whom it is the government's priority to care for.

      1.4.4.1. Pro:
      The banking sector typically constitutes a significant portion of the workforce [183], and permitting their failure could result in substantial unemployment.

        1.4.4.1.1. Pro:
        In the US, around 2.2 million people [184] are employed in the banking industry in 2023.

        1.4.4.1.2. Con:
        This is only true in heavily financialized Western economies. The majority of the developing world's workforce is tied up in the agriculture, manufacturing, and service industries.

          1.4.4.1.2.1. Con:
          The failure of the banking sector would likely lead to run-on effects [185] in other sectors of the economy, making this harm pertinent even in the developing world.

          1.4.4.1.2.2. Pro:
          The majority of workers in many low to middle income [186] countries work in farming.

          1.4.4.1.2.3. Con:
          Financialized economies are the most relevant ones in this discussion as they have the most catastrophic and large-scale bank failures [187].

          1.4.4.1.2.4. Pro:
          Nearly 13% [188] of the global workforce is employed in manufacturing.

      1.4.4.2. Con:
      Bailouts tend to primarily benefit the wealthy [189], with limited direct advantages for ordinary citizens.

        1.4.4.2.1. Con:
        Wealthy individuals in a country contribute more to its economy; therefore, it is justified for them to benefit more.

          1.4.4.2.1.1. Pro:
          Wealthy individuals pay more in taxes [190] than the average person.

          1.4.4.2.1.2. Con:
          The wealth of the elite stems from the surplus value created by workers [191], suggesting that the working poor contribute significantly more to the economy.

          1.4.4.2.1.3. Con:
          Wealthy individuals tend to need less help than the needy. Therefore, even if they contribute more to the economy, it is unjust to take policy actions which benefit them at the expense of the poor.

          1.4.4.2.1.4. Pro:
          The top 10% of the wealthiest Americans [192] play a crucial role in driving capital flow within business operations, ultimately contributing to the continued prosperity of the economy.

        1.4.4.2.2. Pro:
        Following the 2008 financial crisis, several firms that received federal bailouts awarded bonuses of more than $1 million apiece to their traders and bankers [193].

        1.4.4.2.3. Pro:
        Bailouts protect elite bankers from the harms of their own decisions, ultimately acting as risk-absorption for the very wealthy.

        1.4.4.2.4. Con:
        Governments can attach conditions to bailouts [194] that help ensure positive changes to benefit ordinary citizens.
