Discussion Name:
Should refugees seeking asylum in Europe be distributed among EU member states according to a quota?

1. Thesis:
Refugees seeking asylum in Europe should be distributed among EU member states according to a quota.

  1.1. Con:
  A quota scheme commodifies asylum seekers, treating them as a burden to be distributed. This is ethically concerning.

    1.1.1. Con:
    In many respects, they are a burden to be distributed. Acknowledging that hosting refugees has costs is necessary to ensure that those costs can be effectively shared and mitigated.

      1.1.1.1. Pro:
      As this case [1] from Canada demonstrates, careful planning allows for more settlement at less cost. This planning can include recruiting sponsors, sharing logistical responsibilities, seeking cost-effective housing, etc.

    1.1.2. Con:
    Regardless of whether refugees are in the quota system or not, many will already have had previous experience of being treated as a commodity [2] at some point on their journey to Europe.

      1.1.2.1. Con:
      Continuing to commoditize human life because it has been commoditized in the past is fallacious thinking. What's right is right, regardless of whether it was executed in the past or not.

    1.1.3. Con:
    For refugees fleeing war and terror, being given safe haven in a country is a much more important and immediate concern than being possibly treated as a commodity.

      1.1.3.1. Pro:
      According to Amnesty International [3], most refugees fleeing to Europe are doing so protect their life and liberty from persecution.

      1.1.3.2. Con:
      Fleeing war and terror is a very traumatic experience in itself. Ending up in a country that doesn't want to take them in and only does so because they're forced to comply with the quota could make the refugees feel like there's no place where they're welcome, which would add to their emotional trauma.

        1.1.3.2.1. Pro:
        Refugees interviewed [4] have been 'shocked' to find that European countries do not 'see them as human' and that there are treated as 'criminals'.

        1.1.3.2.2. Con:
        There are success stories that show refugees being made to feel welcome by their host country regardless of the quota policy.

          1.1.3.2.2.1. Pro:
          Major European cities such as Barcelona [5] are choosing to welcome refugees over tourists. Immigrants are seen as part of the fabric of society - working, building communities and generally making a contribution to the city.

            1.1.3.2.2.1.1. Pro:
            Spain [6] is considered to be the most welcoming EU country for refugees.

    1.1.4. Pro:
    Commodifying refugees can lead to them becoming a novel source of economic rent [7] to be strategically exploited by countries for national interest.

      1.1.4.1. Pro:
      Jordan has already leveraged [8] its role in housing refugees to extract rent from Western states.

      1.1.4.2. Pro:
      Turkey used the migrant crisis [9] to improve its finances. The March 2016 E.U.–Turkey agreement slowed the flow of refugees into Europe from Turkey, and in return Turkey was promised €6 billion in economic relief [10].

      1.1.4.3. Pro:
      The suggested quota system would involve payment to host countries [11]. This could create perverse incentives which lead to negative outcomes for refugees applying for asylum.

        1.1.4.3.1. Pro:
        If a country is paid to process asylum claims, they may have an incentive [12] to process more claims in a shorter period of time. This may lead them to reject claims quickly, agree to process more, and continue rejecting applicants regardless of the merit of their application.

        1.1.4.3.2. Pro:
        Countries could have an incentive to over-report the number of refugees, in order to gain funding from the central European fund that is likely to be used to finance a quota system. Over-reporting would increase the strain on the limited resources such a fund might have, leaving other countries with less money to help real refugees.

          1.1.4.3.2.1. Pro:
          Double registration of refugees, the creation of fake names and collusion with international aid agencies are some ways that countries can defraud [13] the central fund and leverage the system.

  1.2. Pro:
  EU states have a shared moral responsibility [14] to help asylum seekers, which includes partaking in a quota system.

    1.2.1. Con:
    The quota scheme - in its currently discussed form - would only apply [15] to people with a good chance of receiving asylum. It would not apply to the many irregular arrivals who have little prospect of receiving protected status. Therefore, the scheme would fail to adequately share the responsibility for dealing with the migrant crisis amongst European states.

      1.2.1.1. Pro:
      The EU member states are fully within theirlegal rights [16] to set standards favorable to them for which refugees they would accept.

      1.2.1.2. Pro:
      The moral obligation for the EU does not just exist for refugees who fit certain criteria; it applies to all refugees, regardless of their circumstance.

        1.2.1.2.1. Pro:
        Many economic refugees do not fit criteria for political asylum, despite the intrinsic link between the political and economic [17].

        1.2.1.2.2. Pro:
        Every single person in the world has the legal right to apply [18] for asylum. As such, their circumstances may be different but their individual plea must be given equal weight.

    1.2.2. Con:
    Central and eastern European states are already saturated taking in a huge number of both refugees and migrants from Ukraine [19].

      1.2.2.1. Pro:
      Many central and eastern European countries, which tend to be small and ethnically homogeneous nations, do not have the infrastructure [20] to deal with significant numbers of refugees from non-European countries.

        1.2.2.1.1. Con:
        Providing the most basic needs for refugees such as a roof over their head, clothing, food, and safety does not require a complex and highly developed infrastructure.

    1.2.3. Pro:
    There is a moral duty [21] to allow asylum seekers in need to live in safety in the EU.

      1.2.3.1. Pro:
      European states would fail to respect the value of human lives and human rights [22] if they do not take in those people seeking asylum.

      1.2.3.2. Con:
      The duty to host asylum seekers should belong, first of all, to the neighbouring countries refugees are fleeing from because they are closer geographically and and more similar linguistically and culturally.

        1.2.3.2.1. Con:
        Countries in the Middle East have done more than their fair share when it comes to hosting refugees.

          1.2.3.2.1.1. Pro:
          More than 5 million [23] displaced Syrians have been hosted by neighbouring countries in the Middle East and North Africa. On the other hand only 1 million displaced Syrians now live in Europe.

          1.2.3.2.1.2. Pro:
          Despite already being over-stretched and not having the necessary resources to cope with the situation, Middle Eastern [24] countries still have allowed hundreds of thousands of neighbouring refugees in.

            1.2.3.2.1.2.1. Pro:
            Jordan has taken in a very large number [25] of refugees despite its limited resources. This is beginning to hurt its economy.

            1.2.3.2.1.2.2. Pro:
            Lebanon [26]’s economy has been under enormous pressure because of the influx of Syrian refugees.

        1.2.3.2.2. Con:
        Conflict-ridden countries are often surrounded by poor neighbours. Often these countries do not have good conditions even for their own citizens, let alone a mass influx of refugees.

          1.2.3.2.2.1. Pro:
          Despite unsuitable conditions and economic problems, developing countries still host 84% [27] of all refugees.

        1.2.3.2.3. Pro:
        Neighbouring countries are better able to provide for the refugees than EU countries as there are not barriers such as having a different language, culture, and norms.

      1.2.3.3. Pro:
      The refugee crisis is not confined to one part of the world. It is a global [28] issue needing a global response, with all countries sharing the burden and working together.

        1.2.3.3.1. Pro:
        No matter whether people are European or not, those in need should be helped. Regardless of boundaries, we all share this world and in essence are global citizens [29].

      1.2.3.4. Con:
      The EU's only moral duty is to help ensure the refugees are safe and live in acceptable conditions. Satisfying this duty does not require letting them live in Europe.

        1.2.3.4.1. Pro:
        Borders are a relatively abstract concept. Therefore there is no obligation to view them as an objective factor and aim to fit refugees in an arbitrary boundary.

      1.2.3.5. Con:
      A generic quota distributing refugees primarily based on countries GDP and population size is unlikely to ensure that all refugees are properly cared for.

    1.2.4. Pro:
    The EU owes a duty to refugees fleeing from crisis zones that owe much of their current troubles to a debilitating process of colonization [30] conducted by many EU member states in the 19th and 20th centuries.

      1.2.4.1. Con:
      Most EU member states did not partake in any colonisation attempt, and therefore do not have a moral duty to help these previously colonised peoples.

        1.2.4.1.1. Pro:
        Of the current 28 member states of the EU, only 8 [31] - Spain, Portugal, France, the Kingdom of England (later Great Britain), the Netherlands, Belgium, Italy, and the Kingdom of Prussia (now Germany) - engaged in colonial activities during the "Modern Colonial Era".

      1.2.4.2. Con:
      The home countries of the refugees will have benefited from imperialism at some point, and therefore the EU's colonial past does not create any special obligations to help these refugees

        1.2.4.2.1. Pro:
        Given the ubiquity of imperialism/colonialism [32] throughout history, every culture that has been around long enough has benefited from the subjugation of another.

      1.2.4.3. Con:
      The EU countries which have never taken part in the colonization of other lands should not be forced to bear the burden of something other countries have done.

      1.2.4.4. Con:
      Colonization is a thing of the past; current political leadership in the EU and its citizens should not be held to account for actions that were undertaken before they were even born.

        1.2.4.4.1. Con:
        When the wealth enjoyed by Europe is largely due to the rewards reaped through colonisation, it is morally correct to use that wealth to repay that debt.

          1.2.4.4.1.1. Pro:
          The EU should heed the Beneficiary Pays Principle (BPP (pg 4) [33], which states that ‘the involuntary receipts of benefits stemming from injustice can, in some circumstances, give rise to rectificatory obligations to the victims of the injustice in question.

    1.2.5. Con:
    The primary obligation that EU member states owe is to their own citizenry who have voted the political leadership into office to protect and advance their interests.

      1.2.5.1. Pro:
      EU member states are all democratic states in which the political leadership retains its legitimacy to rule [34] and represent the country by acting in a manner that is representative of the democratic will of the people.

        1.2.5.1.1. Con:
        EU member states' political leadership simultaneously derive their legitimacy from status as members of the European Union, and must represent their objectives and ideals equally.

          1.2.5.1.1.1. Pro:
          The laws and treaties followed by the countries in the EU require them to help asylum seekers.

            1.2.5.1.1.1.1. Pro:
            The 1951 Refugee Convention [35] outlines the rights of the displaced, as well as the legal obligations of States to protect them. All EU countries signed [36] this convention.

            1.2.5.1.1.1.2. Pro:
            The European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR) [37] - which all EU countries have signed on to [38] - creates a legal responsibility [39] for a state not to turn away refugees who face a threat to their lives in their country of origin.

            1.2.5.1.1.1.3. Pro:
            The EU's judicial bodies [40] have ruled that asylum seekers must be granted a visa under Articles 4 and 18 of the EU Charter on Fundamental Rights [41]; there is a clear legal obligation to ensure the welfare of refugees.

      1.2.5.2. Con:
      Democratic leadership is charged with not just fulfilling voter preferences but also maximizing their welfare; sometimes policies which advance the interests of the public may be unpopular.

        1.2.5.2.1. Pro:
        Data [42] suggests that despite any concerns to the contrary, accepting refugees is good for European economies.

      1.2.5.3. Con:
      European states follow liberal democratic principles [43] which focus on the protection of individual freedoms and minority protection. Therefore, they should accept more refugees.

        1.2.5.3.1. Pro:
        An important principle of liberal democracy is effectively guaranteeing individual and minority rights. Given that refugees are a marginalized minority at risk of harm, they fall under this principle.

        1.2.5.3.2. Pro:
        Liberal democratic states exist to increase the scope [44] for religious, political and intellectual freedoms; these freedoms are threatened for refugees, and the liberal democratic states must provide them with an umbrella of protection.

    -> See 1.2.5.1.1.1.

  1.3. Pro:
  A quota system benefits asylum seekers.

    1.3.1. Pro:
    An amended quota system could allow asylum candidates refugee to rank their preferred destination countries [45]. This would give asylum seekers a greater chance of ending up in a country where it would be easier for them to integrate in the long term.

      1.3.1.1. Con:
      This amended quota policy allows the host country's preferences [46] about the type of refugees they want to have an effect on how refugees are distributed. This mechanism could dilute any effect the country preferences refugees have, and lead to refugees often not being assigned to their first preference.

    1.3.2. Con:
    -> See 1.1.4.3.

    1.3.3. Con:
    If countries opposed to the quota are forced to accept refugees, it is likely that they will be treated poorly by the host countries.

      1.3.3.1. Pro:
      Hungary, which has denounced [47] the refugee quota, has treated refugees in its borders inhumanely [48], and has previously suggested detaining all asylum seekers in shipping containers near the border with Serbia.

        1.3.3.1.1. Pro:
        Prime Minister Viktor Orban reportedly said "We don't see these people as Muslim refugees. We see them as Muslim invaders". Such hostile [49] language does not bode well for the treatment of refugees in Hungary.

      1.3.3.2. Pro:
      The Czech Republic - which has only taken in [50] 12 refugees under the quota system - has enacted draconian [51] policies against refugees. These include the detention of families with children, family separation, and forcing refugees to pay for their own detention.

      1.3.3.3. Pro:
      74% [52] of Polish citizens don't want to host refugees from Africa and the Middle East; with a such a significant majority holding a hostile attitude towards refugees, it is likely that government institutions tasked with taking care of these refugees will follow the democratic will and treat these refugees poorly.

        1.3.3.3.1. Pro:
        Polish politicians have openly said the government would have to use some "repression [53]" on asylum seekers to prevent “a wave of aggression".

        1.3.3.3.2. Pro:
        Even if the Polish Government provides protections to refugees, they are still vulnerable to an agitated public; in Poland, hostile public attitudes [54] to refugees have manifested in hate crimes [55] against minorities.

    1.3.4. Con:
    The quota system in its current form might harm asylum seekers by relocating them in a country they do not want to live in.

      1.3.4.1. Pro:
      People granted refugee status in an EU country can only get the legal right to move freely between most other EU countries if they have been living in their country of asylum "legally and continuously" for five years [56].

        1.3.4.1.1. Con:
        Many refugees that were granted asylum in Portugal have since left the country [57] behind to live illegally in other EU countries.

      1.3.4.2. Pro:
      Many new refugees who have been offered asylum in Portugal have found it difficult to establish a life there, in part because “there are no family networks and no friend networks" [58] that can support their arrival.

      1.3.4.3. Pro:
      The Refugees themselves [59] might not want to be distributed to places [60] that would prevent them from easily returning home [61]. The EU is a refuge not a new place to set up home.

      1.3.4.4. Con:
      Being safe in a country where they don't want to live is a better option than the alternatives; attempting to survive in a dangerous [62] country or eking out [63] an existence where they are not officially supported.

      1.3.4.5. Pro:
      Asylum seekers being placed in Cyprus [64] complained to UNHCR about the deteriorating living conditions.

    1.3.5. Pro:
    Asylum seekers often remain in detention in frontline countries like Italy for long periods of time [65], waiting for their claims to be heard. Distributing asylum seekers equitably could reduce the backlog of asylum cases.

      1.3.5.1. Pro:
      While their applications are processed, refugees must reside [66] at their initial port of entry; sometimes this process can take years [67].

      1.3.5.2. Pro:
      It is unethical [68] for the standard policy for dealing with asylum seekers to involve placing refugees in camps for long periods of time.

    1.3.6. Pro:
    The other policy alternatives to a quota system would cause refugees more harm.

      1.3.6.1. Con:
      Turning back boats may be the most humane [69] response to the migrant crisis, as doing so helps stop an industry that smuggles human beings.

        1.3.6.1.1. Con:
        The EU crackdown [70] on human smuggling has had the opposite effect of what was intended. Now there is a greater supply of smuggling services, higher prices and higher risk.

        1.3.6.1.2. Pro:
        The EU shouldn't play any part, (directly or indirectly) in the business [71] of human trafficking. As soon as a country grants residency to those who arrive at its shores, it becomes the unwitting partner of people traffickers.

        1.3.6.1.3. Pro:
        Human trafficking is modern-day slavery [72]. Trafficking is the act of recruiting, harbouring, transporting, providing or obtaining a person for compelled labor or commercial sex acts through the use of force, fraud or coercion.

      1.3.6.2. Pro:
      The most recent compromise [73] pushed by the EU - to withdraw rescue ships from the Mediterranean in order to disincentivise refugees from coming - seriously threatens the lives of those being transported across the sea.

        1.3.6.2.1. Pro:
        2,300 [74] people lost their lives attempting to cross the Mediterranean just in 2018.

      1.3.6.3. Pro:
      A default back to the pre-2015 Dublin rules [75] - which state that asylum seekers must apply for asylum at their first port of entry - would disproportionately burden a fraction of EU member states and spread too thin any protections afforded to refugees in those countries.

      1.3.6.4. Pro:
      A compromise [76] to placate all EU member states at the 2018 migration summit included proposals to amp up external border security and move processing centers to North Africa, both of which will reduce the chances of unfettered passage to Europe for refugees.

  1.4. Con:
  A quota system is unenforceable due to the lack of border control [77] between most EU countries. Regardless of the EU country a refugee is given asylum in, they can travel to any other EU country easily.

    1.4.1. Con:
    -> See 1.3.4.1.

    1.4.2. Pro:
    -> See 1.3.4.1.1.

    1.4.3. Con:
    Not all EU countries are members of the Schengen Agreement, thus a few of them do have border control.

    1.4.4. Pro:
    Attempts to fence borders in Eastern Europe along with the closure of the Balkan corridor [78] has not reduced the migration flow. Instead, it forces migrants to seek more dangerous routes to Central Europe.

      1.4.4.1. Pro:
      Fences [79] and border surveillance are no deterrent [80] for migrants, who have often experienced far worse traumas.

      1.4.4.2. Pro:
      Blocking some routes has deepened the business of exploitative smugglers [81].

    1.4.5. Con:
    Hungary [82] has secured its borders with a €1 billion fence, thus proving arguments wrong that migration is impossible to stop at borders.

    1.4.6. Pro:
    With no permanent border controls between Schengen countries, external border [83] control is crucial for all EU countries in Europe. Schengen members rely on the quality of border checks and surveillance carried out by other EU countries.

      1.4.6.1. Con:
      In 2020 [84], the EU is planning to implement a 10,000 strong armed force to protect its borders. This will help address concerns about the quality of border control at Europe's external borders.

  1.5. Con:
  Forcing a country to take in a quota of refugees undermines its national sovereignty.

    1.5.1. Pro:
    A quota system agreed to under existing power imbalances within the EU would unfairly favour more powerful countries [85].

      1.5.1.1. Con:
      The last migration summit [86] in 2018 saw extensive negotiations and the drawing up of compromises which placated the vast majority of EU member states.

        1.5.1.1.1. Con:
        The compromises reached at the summit are extremely vague [87], with little agreement on substantive policy questions.

      1.5.1.2. Pro:
      France and Germany have signed a treaty that will strengthen their place [88] as the dominant countries in the EU. They will have an oversized influence on any decisions about quotas.

        1.5.1.2.1. Con:
        Even as the French and German states' power increases, rising domestic opposition to Europe from alt-right movements [89] in these countries threatens their leadership role in Europe.

    1.5.2. Pro:
    Part of being a sovereign [90] state means having control over one's borders. By forcing EU members to take in a certain quota of refugees, the EU is infringing on the sovereignty of those countries.

      1.5.2.1. Pro:
      Hungary's Prime Minister Viktor Orban [91] believes the EU's migration policies threaten the "sovereignty and cultural identity" of Hungary.

      1.5.2.2. Con:
      The EU's free movement policy [92] already dilutes border sovereignty significantly.

        1.5.2.2.1. Pro:
        Countries cannot simply opt out of this policy to 'reclaim' their sovereignty. As shown by the Brexit situation in the UK, it is unclear if EU member states truly have a free choice to quit the union if they please. Hence territorial sovereignty is a foregone concept in many respects within the EU.

    1.5.3. Con:
    EU member states already cede a considerable amount of sovereignty to the EU upon accession into the Union with a system of 'pooled sovereignty [93]'.

      1.5.3.1. Pro:
      The rules for trade within the single market are defined by the EU, and several environmental and trade regulations are arbitrated [94] by the European Court of Justice.

      1.5.3.2. Pro:
      Due to the cooperative nature of unions, some self-interest - and therefore the sovereignty to act in that self interest - must be put aside to make the union functional.

      1.5.3.3. Pro:
      EU laws [95] in areas for which the EU is responsible override any conflicting laws of member countries. In this way EU laws have higher precedence over national laws.

        1.5.3.3.1. Pro:
        The UK passed the European Communities Act [96] in 1972, which entailed accepting the supremacy of EU law. For some [97] such acts and membership of the EU are perceived as a loss of national sovereignty.

          1.5.3.3.1.1. Con:
          The fact that the UK is able to leave the EU indicates that it remains ultimately a sovereign state. [98]

          1.5.3.3.1.2. Pro:
          Brexit [99] from the European Union has been dominated by yearning for restoring UK sovereignty.

  1.6. Con:
  Taking in a larger number of refugees would be a security risk for member states that lack the necessary infrastructure to properly vet the refugees.

    1.6.1. Pro:
    Major attacks in European cities including Paris, Brussels, Berlin, Nice and Barcelona, were all claimed by the Islamic State (IS). It is believed that many of the perpetrators [100] of these attacks were people who initially entered as refugees.

    1.6.2. Con:
    The biggest threat by far comes from homegrown extremists. The vast majority of terror attacks in Europe are carried out by European citizens [101], not refugees.

      1.6.2.1. Pro:
      Thousands of Europeans [102] have gone to fight with the Islamic State in Iraq and Syria, and al-Qaida has long had a presence in Europe.

    1.6.3. Con:
    Branding refugees as a security risk is counterproductive, running the risk of becoming a self-fulfilling prophecy [103].

      1.6.3.1. Pro:
      Treating refugees as security [104] threats fuels intolerance and gives terrorists a potent recruitment tool to convince young Muslims that the West has no place for them.

        1.6.3.1.1. Pro:
        Western anti-refugee [105] politics can increase insecurity. Such political beliefs plays directly into the hands of the Islamic State’s propagandists. Security measures that deny refugees their dignity are more likely to foster extremism and violent tendencies.

    1.6.4. Pro:
    Jihadists [106] have been able to take advantage of the migrant crisis. Isis fighters posing as refugees have been smuggled to Europe on migrant boats.

      1.6.4.1. Pro:
      Conservative voices [107] in Europe believe that Europe is being flooded with “half a million” Islamic State fighters.

        1.6.4.1.1. Pro:
        Angela Merkel [108] acknowledges that without a doubt there will be refugees who pose a security threat to Germany.

          1.6.4.1.1.1. Con:
          Terrorism security risks exist with or without refugees. Germany [109] was already a target for Islamic terrorism even before the many refugees arrived.

        1.6.4.1.2. Con:
        Concerns about terrorism and the refugees are legitimate, [110] but the fears being voiced are usually exaggerated and the concerns raised are often the wrong ones.

          1.6.4.1.2.1. Pro:
          Refugees who are terrorists are extremely rare. The majority [111] of refugees fleeing war are not using the opportunity to embed themselves as terrorists in the West.

            1.6.4.1.2.1.1. Pro:
            Refugees [112] are not likely to be strong supporters of the most violent groups; if they were, they would have stayed in Iraq or Syria. They themselves are fleeing war and extremism.

      1.6.4.2. Pro:
      Europol [113] has warned that some refugees could be vulnerable to Isis propaganda. According to German authorities, jihadis have made at least 300 attempts to recruit refugees attempting to enter Europe.

  1.7. Con:
  Forcing a refugee quota on member countries would be unpopular.

It would be unfair to ask them to take in even more when countries with large populations like Spain has taken less than 0.2% of their population.

      1.7.1.4. Pro:

      1.7.2.1. Pro:
