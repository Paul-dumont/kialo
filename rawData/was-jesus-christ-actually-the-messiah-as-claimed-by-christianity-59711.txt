Discussion Name:
Was Jesus Christ actually the Messiah as claimed by Christianity?

1. Thesis:
Jesus Christ is the Messiah as claimed by Christianity.

  1.1. Pro:
  The attributes and activities of Jesus match up with certain descriptions and prophecies of the Old Testament Messiah.

    1.1.1. Pro:
    The prophet Isaiah made direct references to Jesus and his activities.

      1.1.1.1. Pro:
      Isaiah 7:14 [1] says that the Messiah would be born of a virgin, which Jesus supposedly was.

        1.1.1.1.1. Con:
        Isaiah 7:14 [2] says that the Messiah's name would be Immanuel, not Jesus.

          1.1.1.1.1.1. Pro:
          Outside of alleged prophecy, nobody in the Bible ever directly refers to Jesus with the name Immanuel

          1.1.1.1.1.2. Con:
          Immanuel is a symbolic [3] (literally meaning "God is with us"), rather than literal name.

            1.1.1.1.1.2.1. Pro:
            The writer of Matthew 1:21-23 [4] references the Immanuel prophecy immediately after the verse where Jesus' name is given. This shows an understanding that the name was not meant literally.

          1.1.1.1.1.3. Con:
          Isaiah 7:14 [5] is not actually a reference to the Messiah.

            1.1.1.1.1.3.1. Pro:
            The historical context of Isaiah 7:14 is some 700 years BC, in the midst of a war. [6] This verse is a prophecy about the war.

            1.1.1.1.1.3.2. Con:
            If Isaiah 7:14 [7] is not a reference to the Messiah, it was improper for the author of Matthew 1:23 [8] to quote it as such. This is a contradiction. [9]

          1.1.1.1.1.4. Con:
          Christians have adopted the belief from John 1:1 [10] & 1:14 [11] that Jesus was in fact, God, with us. That he had another name by which he is called doesn't disqualify him being called Immanuel.

        1.1.1.1.2. Con:
        -> See 1.1.1.1.1.3.

      1.1.1.2. Pro:
      Isaiah 53:3 [12] says that the Messiah would be despised and rejected by mankind. In the gospel, a great crowd choose to pardon a murderer and let Jesus be crucified. [13]

        1.1.1.2.1. Con:
        Isaiah 53 is a continuation of the prior chapter [14] which uses a literary device of referring to an entire group of people as a person.

          1.1.1.2.1.1. Pro:
          The beginning of chapter 52 [15] refers to "Zion", which has always been used as a reference to the Israelite/Jewish people, but never Jesus.

      1.1.1.3. Pro:
      Isaiah 53:7 [16] says the Messiah would remain silent in the face of oppression; Jesus supposedly stood mute before his accusers [17] and is not recorded ever defending himself.

        1.1.1.3.1. Con:
        -> See 1.1.1.2.1.

      1.1.1.4. Pro:
      Isaiah 9:6 [18] references the divinity (and the triune nature) of the Messiah, saying a child will be born, and called God, the father, and the Prince of peace. All of these are attributes of Jesus.

    1.1.2. Pro:
    The book of Psalms contains direct references to the Messiah.

      1.1.2.1. Pro:
      Psalm 49:15 [19] is a reference to the resurrection of the Messiah

      1.1.2.2. Pro:
      Psalm 2:7 [20] indicates that the Messiah would be the son of God, which Jesus claimed to be.

      1.1.2.3. Pro:
      Psalm 22:16-18 [21] references events that happened to Jesus; being pierced in the hands and feet, and lots being drawn for his clothing.

        1.1.2.3.1. Con:
        The use of the verb "pierced" in Psalm 22:16 is problematic and disputed as it does not appear [22] in the original manuscripts.

    1.1.3. Pro:
    Jesus' activities fulfill a number of Old Testament prophecies

      1.1.3.1. Pro:
      The prophet Zechariah made direct references to Jesus and his activities.

        1.1.3.1.1. Pro:
        Zechariah 12:10 [23], a prophecy regarding the restoration of Jerusalem, references "the one they have pierced". Jesus is described in multiple New Testament verses as being run through with a spear.

        1.1.3.1.2. Pro:
        Zechariah 11:12-13 [24] references a shepherd being paid 30 pieces of silver and the money being "thrown to the potter". Judas betrayed Jesus for this price and the money was used to buy the "potter's field".

        1.1.3.1.3. Pro:
        Zechariah 9:9 [25] references the "King of Zion" riding on a donkey in the midst of great celebration; this matches [26] how Jesus entered Jerusalem shortly after resurrecting Lazarus. [27]

          1.1.3.1.3.1. Con:
          Anyone wishing to claim they, or someone else was the Messiah illegitimately would have been aware of this prophecy and could have arranged these events through mundane methods.

            1.1.3.1.3.1.1. Con:
            Jesus' apostles had a sincere belief that he was the Messiah, and their activities are inconsistent with any kind of fraud or deception.

              1.1.3.1.3.1.1.1. Pro:
              It would not make sense for people perpetrating a fraudulent Messiah to hold to such a conspiracy even up to torturous death

                1.1.3.1.3.1.1.1.1. Pro:
                Of the 12 apostles after Judas, only the apostle John lived to old age. The others were believed to be martyred. [28]

                1.1.3.1.3.1.1.1.2. Pro:
                Tacitus describes the vicious persecution of the early Christians (Annals 15:44 [29]).

              1.1.3.1.3.1.1.2. Pro:
              For Jews, crucifixion was a humiliating ordeal [30] brought about by foreign invaders. It does not make sense that this degradation would be the chosen method of death for a fake Messiah.

              1.1.3.1.3.1.1.3. Con:
              For the apostles to be wrong, it is not necessary that they were being deceptive. They could have been tricked or misled.

                1.1.3.1.3.1.1.3.1. Con:
                Many purported events, if not factual, would have required active collusion between the gospel writers and Jesus, since the narrative consists of eyewitness accounts in which the writers themselves feature prominently.

      1.1.3.2. Pro:
      The book of Malachi references the "messenger of the covenant" [31], later in Luke, Jesus quotes this verse in reference to himself. [32]

      1.1.3.3. Pro:
      Hosea directly references Jesus' resurrection [33]

        1.1.3.3.1. Con:
        The context of this verse clearly references a nation (will raise "us" up, "we" will be…) being healed, not a single individual.

      1.1.3.4. Pro:
      The book of Psalms contains prophecies that Jesus' actions fulfill

    1.1.4. Pro:
    Jesus has attributes that the Old Testament describes the Messiah would have.

  1.2. Pro:
  -> See 1.1.3.1.3.1.1.

  1.3. Pro:
  Jesus' effect on the course of human history shows that he is the Messiah.

    1.3.1. Pro:
    Christianity's influence on human history is uniquely oversized in a way no other religion is.

      1.3.1.1. Pro:
      About one third of the world's population [34] professes belief in some kind of Christianity, and it is the most widespread geographically.

    1.3.2. Pro:
    Human historical timeline is divided into two eras, Before Christ (BC) and Anno Domini (AD). This shows that the entire human history centers around Christ as the reference.

      1.3.2.1. Con:
      The BC/AD split was enacted in the first couple of centuries by likely religious historians, modern historians tend to use CE/BCE (common era/before common era) as it is devoid of religious connotations. The division is ultimately arbitrary.

        1.3.2.1.1. Con:
        Regardless of the precise name of the division, it happens at the same time and for the same reason: Christ.

      1.3.2.2. Con:
      Jesus' effect on human history is attributable to his followers' belief that he was the Messiah. Using this to prove that he was the Messiah is, therefore, circular.

        1.3.2.2.1. Con:
        If a person is willing to die for some belief, there is good reason to consider that the belief is grounded in reality. The disciples were willing to die for the belief that Jesus rose from the dead, therefore it is reasonable to consider the reality that Jesus did in fact rise from the dead.

          1.3.2.2.1.1. Pro:
          -> See 1.1.3.1.3.1.1.1.

    1.3.3. Con:
    -> See 1.3.2.2.

  1.4. Con:
  The attributes and activities of Jesus described in the New Testament are inconsistent with the description of the Old Testament Messiah.

    1.4.1. Con:
    All inconsistencies between the old and New Testament description of the Messiah can be reconciled with the understanding that Christ will return (the "second coming")

      1.4.1.1. Con:
      This fails to address all inconsistencies

        1.4.1.1.1. Pro:
        -> See 1.1.1.1.1.

      1.4.1.2. Con:
      Nothing within the Old Testament points to the Messiah being killed and resurrected.

        1.4.1.2.1. Con:
        -> See 1.1.2.1.

        1.4.1.2.2. Pro:
        Jesus' resurrection is so central to all forms of Christianity, the New Testament directly says that if Jesus had not been resurrected, then faith is in vain. [35]

        1.4.1.2.3. Con:
        Isaiah chapter 53 in whole [36] is a direct reference to the Messiah and contains references to the Messiah being killed.

          1.4.1.2.3.1. Con:
          -> See 1.1.1.2.1.

    1.4.2. Pro:
    Jesus fails to fulfill prophecies made by the Old Testament.

      1.4.2.1. Con:
      Since none of the prophecies are time-bounded, all could still be fulfilled at some point in the future.

      1.4.2.2. Pro:
      In Isaiah 2:4 [37], the Messiah is prophesied to bring world peace. This never happened.

        1.4.2.2.1. Con:
        This passage could be referring to "latter days" (vs 2) that are closer to the end of all days when Jesus will establish his kingdom forever. Rev. 11:15 [38]

        1.4.2.2.2. Pro:
        Jesus outright declares he does not come to bring peace on earth in Luke 12:51 [39].

    1.4.3. Pro:
    -> See 1.4.1.2.

    1.4.4. Pro:
    Jesus lacks attributes that the Old Testament describes that the Messiah would have

      1.4.4.1. Pro:
      -> See 1.1.1.1.1.

      1.4.4.2. Pro:
      Ezekiel implies [40] the Messiah would be a human man, rather than a divine being.

        1.4.4.2.1. Con:
        Christian tradition holds [41] that Jesus was fully human.

      1.4.4.3. Pro:
      Deuteronomy 17:14-20 [42] says that any kings of Israel must be observant of the law, where Jesus changed the law significantly.

        1.4.4.3.1. Con:
        Deuteronomy 17:14 [43] makes it clear in context that this is in reference to kings of Israel in the promised land, not a Messiah.

        1.4.4.3.2. Pro:
        Jesus abridged or abolished [44] a number of tenets of the laws of the Israelites, which are described in various scriptures as unchanging and everlasting.

          1.4.4.3.2.1. Pro:
          The Mosaic law is explicitly [45] described [46] as [47] eternal [48] and everlasting [49], with explicit [50] warnings [51] given about disregarding or changing it.

          1.4.4.3.2.2. Con:
          The laws were a covenant, an agreement between 2 parties. Prophets before Christ recognized that Israel had broken their side of the covenant.

            1.4.4.3.2.2.1. Pro:
            Jeremiah calls out [52] both Israel's breaking of the covenant and the coming establishment of a new one.

              1.4.4.3.2.2.1.1. Pro:
              This verse only makes sense in light of the understanding that the Mosaic covenant was going away.

      1.4.4.4. Pro:
      Jesus was not a descendent of David by way of his father, as required in Jeremiah 23:5-6 [53], Isaiah 11:1 [54], and 2 Samuel 7:12-13 [55]

        1.4.4.4.1. Con:
        "Descendant" does not necessarily imply biological offspring. Instead, it could refer to a legal heir [56]. Thus, Jesus was a descendant of David through his adoptive father, Joseph.

          1.4.4.4.1.1. Con:
          There is no biblical basis for the idea of a father passing on his tribal line by adoption. A priest who adopts a son from another tribe never made him a priest by adoption.

      1.4.4.5. Pro:
      Joseph, Jesus' "adoptive" father is reportedly descended from Jeconiah [57], someone whose descendants are barred from kingship [58], therefore a child of his could not be the Messiah.

        1.4.4.5.1. Con:
        The priesthood/kingdom is carried on by blood, not adoption. Since Joseph is only the adoptive father of Jesus, he would not pass on the prophesied prohibition.

          1.4.4.5.1.1. Con:
          It is peculiar that the book of Matthew spends almost an entire chapter on Jesus' genealogy [59] were that genealogy not of some importance.

          1.4.4.5.1.2. Pro:
          -> See 1.4.4.4.1.1.

  1.5. Con:
  The biblical accounts in the gospel of Jesus' life and supposed death and resurrection are questionable [60].

    1.5.1. Pro:
    The gospel accounts are not consistent with each other.

      1.5.1.1. Pro:
      The fate of Judas is inconsistent. Matthew 27:5 [61] has him throwing away the blood money and hanging himself, while Acts 1:18 [62] has him purchasing a field with the blood money and dying after a fall.

        1.5.1.1.1. Pro:
        Even if we accept that the inconsistencies surrounding the death of Judas can be explained by different writers focusing on different activities [63], there is still a contradiction regarding who purchased the field. Was it Judas or was it the priests who paid him?

      1.5.1.2. Pro:
      The treatment of the old covenant is inconsistent. In Matthew 5:18 [64], Jesus speaks about the immutability of the law, but in Mark 7:19 [65], he countermands the dietary laws.

        1.5.1.2.1. Con:
        The "all being accomplished" mentioned in Matthew 5:18 [66] could be a reference to his death.

          1.5.1.2.1.1. Con:
          This is still an irreconcilable difference with the old testament. Something described as eternal and the everlasting was changed, abolished, or rendered moot.

            1.5.1.2.1.1.1. Con:
            -> See 1.4.4.3.2.2.1.

        1.5.1.2.2. Con:
        Colossians 2:16–17 [67] indicates that the laws were intended to foreshadow Christ.

          1.5.1.2.2.1. Con:
          -> See 1.5.1.2.1.1.

        1.5.1.2.3. Pro:
        -> See 1.4.4.3.2.

      1.5.1.3. Con:
      The inconsistencies are minor and do not detract from or contradict the message of the gospel.

        1.5.1.3.1. Con:
        Unresolved inconsistencies call the reliability of the accounts into question, a greater message notwithstanding.

        1.5.1.3.2. Pro:
        All the gospels are consistent with the main central story. A feature that is common with oral tradition.

      1.5.1.4. Pro:
      According to John's gospel, Jesus was crucified on the day of Preparation, which is before the Passover (John 19:14 [68]), but according to the Synoptic gospels, Jesus had the Passover feast with his disciples (Mark 14:12-16 [69]) and was therefore crucified on the day after Passover.

        1.5.1.4.1. Con:
        Feast days are also considered sabbaths (known as High Sabbaths [70]), each with their own days of preparation. The supposed differences in the Gospels may then be reconciled if one allows for the possibility of two Preparations/Sabbaths on Crucifixion Week, with Passover falling a few days before Saturday.

          1.5.1.4.1.1. Pro:
          Regarding High Sabbaths, the Jewish online presence Chabad says the following, "On these days, the Torah prohibits work. At a sacred time, work or any involvement in the mundanities of the week will subvert that sacredness and block its light. [71]" Lacking the ability to work on these days, a day of preparation before that sabbath is necessary.

        1.5.1.4.2. Con:
        Each book was written by very different people [72] and so had a different point of view, putting emphasis on different things and writing with different styles.

      1.5.1.5. Con:
      These inconsistencies suggest the gospel is not fake. Were the disciples lying and made a fake gospel, they would have made sure they were extremely similar. The fact that they disagree on events such as timing suggest that the gospels are authentic eyewitness accounts.

    1.5.2. Pro:
    The gospel accounts were written decades after the events they describe.

      1.5.2.1. Pro:
      The books of the gospel were written somewhere between 50 and 70 A.D. [73]

      1.5.2.2. Pro:
      The death of Jesus occurred either in A.D. 30 or A.D. 33 [74]

      1.5.2.3. Con:
      Three to six decades is considered very early by ancient standards. For example, our earliest account of Alexander the great comes roughly 400 years after his death, yet these documents are considered reliable [75].

      1.5.2.4. Con:
      The gospels are not our earliest source regarding Jesus. In his letter to the Corinthians, written around 53AD St Paul describes his initiation into the early church. Which took place 5 years after the crucifixion. He describes the oral tradition of the early church. Which states that Jesus was crucified, rose from the dead and appeared his followers and skeptics. This tradition can be dated to within 5 years of the crucifixion. Making it out earliest source.

    1.5.3. Pro:
    The accounts were all written in the more academic language of Greek [76], even though the supposed writers would have been native speakers of Hebrew.

      1.5.3.1. Con:
      Greek was increasing in popularity in the 1st century, with many residents of Galilee being bilingual.

    1.5.4. Con:
    Many accounts of the historical persons in the gospels are corroborated by non-biblical authors [77] such as Tacitus, Pliny the Younger, and Josephus.

      1.5.4.1. Pro:
      Tacitus Annals 15.44 [78] supports the existence of Jesus.

      1.5.4.2. Pro:
      Other Christian sources corroborate the historical details of the gospels.

        1.5.4.2.1. Pro:
        Approximately more than a dozen extra-biblical sources confirm many details from Jesus’ life and teachings, such as the Clement of Rome, Ignatius, and Polycarp.

        1.5.4.2.2. Pro:
        “Other Christian testimony confirms "a whole host of details in the Gospels. Second-century Christian writers refer back to and even quote a considerable portion of the Gospel accounts … These are no late Hellenistic legends that evolved long after the life of Jesus ... These were the revolutionary claims being made by his followers from the very beginning!” (The Historical Reliabily of the Gospels) [79]

        1.5.4.2.3. Pro:
        Even though archaeological sources do not contribute as much corroboration as they do in Old Testament studies, there are a number of indications that, when the details can be checked, the New Testament is often confirmed [80].

      1.5.4.3. Pro:
      Josephus has two references to Jesus, and one to John the baptist. [81]

        1.5.4.3.1. Con:
        The "Testimonium Flavianum" is considered at least partially a later interpolation [82] by most scholars.

        1.5.4.3.2. Pro:
        The reference to John the Baptist in Antiquities 18:5 and the second reference to Jesus in Antiquities 20:9 are considered authentic by most modern scholars [83].

      1.5.4.4. Con:
      While some non-biblical authors confirm the existence of an early Christian community and mention the possible existence of an historical Jesus, they do not corroborate the facts described in the gospels.

"A number of factors allow us to pinpoint the year of the death of Jesus. We calculate that John the Baptist commenced his ministry c. AD 28 or 29, based on the historical note in Luke 3:1 that John started preaching in the fifteenth year of Tiberius’s reign. Tiberius was named emperor in AD 14. If Jesus was baptized and began His ministry sometime in AD 29, and He ministered for approximately the next three and a half years, then the end of Jesus’ ministry would have been AD 33.

Pontius Pilate is known to have ruled Judea from AD 26–36. The crucifixion took place during a Passover (Mark 14:12), and that fact, plus the date John began his ministry, narrows down the date of the crucifixion to Friday, April 3, AD 33.
