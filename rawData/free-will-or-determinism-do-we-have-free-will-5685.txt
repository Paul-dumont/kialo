Discussion Name:
Free Will or Determinism: Do We Have Free Will?

1. Thesis:
There is no free will.

  1.1. Pro:
  Human actions are determined by uncontrollable internal and metaphysical processes.

    1.1.1. Pro:
    Human beings are biologically hardwired [1] to act and react in certain ways, leaving no room for free will.

      1.1.1.1. Pro:
      Research on the gut microbiome indicates that the bacteria in the gut can cause cravings [2] and drive their food choices.

        1.1.1.1.1. Con:
        The claim that gut microbiome can affect the decisions of individuals is not certain and remains debated (p. 38 [3]).

        1.1.1.1.2. Pro:
        There is a connection between the brain and the gut known as the gut-brain axis [4], which links our emotions with our digestive functions.

      1.1.1.2. Con:
      Living beings are highly complex and their actions cannot be anticipated [5], which means that they must have some kind of free will (p. E8678 [6]).

        1.1.1.2.1. Con:
        Unpredictability is not an indication of free will. Rather, it is the state of not having enough background information in order to make an informed estimate.

          1.1.1.2.1.1. Con:
          If the external determinant cause of an action cannot be identified, then the strength of claims that depend on external determination become much weaker.

          1.1.1.2.1.2. Pro:
          We can predict the weather but only within a certain percentage of confidence [7]. However, that does not mean that a sudden gust of wind or unexpected rainfall is proof that the weather has free will.

        1.1.1.2.2. Pro:
        Living beings exhibit spontaneity [8] through acts that are compulsive or fly in the face of their usual habits, which is evidence that their actions are not predetermined.

          1.1.1.2.2.1. Pro:
          It is unclear how to reconcile strict determinism with spontaneous behavior without drastically changing deterministic theories, making some kind of free will more parsimonious [9].

          1.1.1.2.2.2. Con:
          Actions that appear to be spontaneous to an observer, such as the playfulness of domesticated house cats [10], can often be attributed to mere instinct and thus should be considered deterministic.

      1.1.1.3. Con:
      It is possible to desensitize oneself from reflexive responses, which creates room for free will (p. 1 [11]).

        1.1.1.3.1. Con:
        Learning to subdue your instinctive responses, doesn't equate to your actioned reasoning to be free.

      1.1.1.4. Pro:
      According to cognitive automaticity [12], many human behaviors and choices are made automatically.

        1.1.1.4.1. Con:
        Cognitive automaticity may indicate that many behaviors are automatic, but it also identifies that our automatic systems are paired with a controlled system [13] that reintroduces a need for free will.

        1.1.1.4.2. Pro:
        Social automaticity [14] research indicates that even complex social impressions are automatic.

    1.1.2. Con:
    Lived experience and observation suggest that we have free will.

      1.1.2.1. Con:
      Free will exists only as an illusionary mental construct that feels real.

        1.1.2.1.1. Con:
        Given that many prevalent approaches to cognitive psychology describe all human experiences [15] as mentally constructed, it stands to reason that being mentally constructed does not entail being merely illusory.

        1.1.2.1.2. Pro:
        Optical illusions [16] are great examples of things that humans consistently experience, but do not accurately reflect reality.

        1.1.2.1.3. Pro:
        Free will is merely the label used to describe the many complex processes that contribute to decision-making, like the hormonal, digestive, and nervous systems in the body.

      1.1.2.2. Con:
      Through meditation, people have experiences in which they can directly notice their thoughts and emotions rising and falling without any self willing them to do so.

        1.1.2.2.1. Con:
        Meditative practices may not give us total power over our actions, but in helping us distance ourselves from our desires, they reduce their power over our actions.

        1.1.2.2.2. Con:
        Meditative attention is only one kind of attention among many [17], meaning that it does not provide sufficient evidence to refute the existence of free will.

        1.1.2.2.3. Pro:
        Meditative studies support the thesis that thoughts and intentions simply arise in the mind, which challenges theories that hold that thoughts are created by the mind or the self (pp. 2-3 [18]).

          1.1.2.2.3.1. Pro:
          When desires arise in the mind, we have the choice whether or not to act on them, but we cannot choose what to want [19] in the first place.

          1.1.2.2.3.2. Con:
          Introspective awareness is not necessarily definitive because it could be that it only seems like there is no will or conscious self during introspection.

      1.1.2.3. Con:
      Lived experience and human observation are highly reconstructive and flawed and therefore cannot falsify determinism.

        1.1.2.3.1. Con:
        If determinism is unfalsifiable [20] by human observation, then it fails as a theory because it lacks a set of potential conditions that could prove it wrong.

        1.1.2.3.2. Pro:
        There is psychological evidence [21] that people often misremember events even just after they happen, which could explain the illusory belief that choice precedes certain actions.

          1.1.2.3.2.1. Con:
          Neuroscientific evidence suggests that misremembering plays an important role in an optimally functioning [22] cognitive system.

      1.1.2.4. Pro:
      According to empiricism [23], having the experience of free will and acting as if one is free demonstrates that free will exists.

        1.1.2.4.1. Pro:
        Notable radical empiricist [24] William James [25] rejected hard determinism by proposing that free will occurs in two stages: first, we face our chance circumstances and, second, we determine a course of action from the available options.

        1.1.2.4.2. Pro:
        David Hume [26], one of the most famous empiricists, argued for the compatibalist position that human freedom can be reconciled with causal determinism.

          1.1.2.4.2.1. Pro:
          Hume argued that our understanding of cause and effect is not the result of some naturally occurring truth, but rather the result of our minds connecting like events [27]. As such, the existence of a deterministic chain does not imply our choices are unfree.

          1.1.2.4.2.2. Con:
          In response, Immanuel Kant described Hume's form of compatibilism as a "wretched subterfuge [28]" that does not constitute a freedom worthy of human beings.

        1.1.2.4.3. Con:
        Many empiricists [29] do not believe in the existence of free will. The question is far from settled among them.

        1.1.2.4.4. Con:
        Empiricism is a largely outdated philosophical view now, especially after the advent of pragmatism [30] and the scientific method. As such, its views on free will ought not to be taken seriously.

          1.1.2.4.4.1. Con:
          Many pragmatists still believe in free will, arguing that a belief in free will gives agents reason to act [31].

          1.1.2.4.4.2. Pro:
          Such pragmatist views stem from classical empiricism [32], but re-conceptualize experience and the importance of science.

      1.1.2.5. Pro:
      The universal human experience of having our will frustrated or compromised is a sound basis for considering free will a legitimate phenomenon.

        1.1.2.5.1. Con:
        The experience [33] of having free will is not the same thing as actually having free will.

        1.1.2.5.2. Pro:
        In response to life's many adverse conditions, William James advocated for strengthening our will [34] through mindful practice, which suggests that the will is free in some sense.

        1.1.2.5.3. Con:
        Given how commonly errors [35] in judgment occur, an experience of free will does not provide strong evidence for free will's existence.

    1.1.3. Pro:
    Modern science's understanding of the brain suggests it is deterministically linked to human action (p. 545 [36]).

      1.1.3.1. Con:
      Neuroscientific evidence and methods are comparitively and particularly crude, due to their novel nature and lack of replicability [37]. As such, whatever determines our physical brain states remains unknown.

        1.1.3.1.1. Con:
        All scientific fields are in a relatively crude state compared to future advancements. Since neuroscience is predicted to become more precise as the technology [38] and methodology become more advanced, it is reasonable to make predictions and interpretations around the current scientific consensus.

          1.1.3.1.1.1. Con:
          It is reasonable to be skeptical of future development in neuroscience because there is no clear scientific consensus on the relationship between neuroscience and free will (p. 14 [39]).

          1.1.3.1.1.2. Pro:
          Even if neuroscientific findings are preliminary, they have promising implications for mental health care [40].

        1.1.3.1.2. Pro:
        Science cannot explain or understand [41] consciousness [42]. Therefore, this question is outside the bounds of current science since we do not appear to be solely subject to physical laws.

        1.1.3.1.3. Con:
        Neuroscientific research into the dynamics and interactions of neurotransmitters [43] and various compounds has revealed many ways our brain states determine or influence our behavior.

      1.1.3.2. Con:
      The Cogito Model of free will argues that random events [44] occur within human brains and bodies, and these random events can be used by a human to choose between two or more actions.

        1.1.3.2.1. Pro:
        Since the human brain is made up of atoms and particles, and atoms and particles act in random or probabilistic ways, it is likely that the brain acts in these ways too.

          1.1.3.2.1.1. Con:
          To say that a system is probabilistic means that we do not know which will be the concrete outcome of that system at a given time. This is not to say that the system has free will.

          1.1.3.2.1.2. Pro:
          Some mathematicians have argued that particles [45] may, in fact, have free will.

        1.1.3.2.2. Con:
        There is no strong evidence that quantum-level effects have any impact on information processing done by the brain, which seems to follow classical laws of physics [46] entirely.

          1.1.3.2.2.1. Pro:
          Quantum approaches to consciousness resemble dangerous forms of post-empirical science [47], which make broad assumptions without evidence.

        1.1.3.2.3. Con:
        Actions caused randomly cannot be said to be freely chosen because choice and randomness are mutually exclusive.

          1.1.3.2.3.1. Pro:
          A random event cannot be the result of a free choice, or any choice, by definition.

          1.1.3.2.3.2. Con:
          It is possible to choose to do something and overcome whatever particular state the body is in. For example, while a soldier may feel cowardly, sleepy, or hungry, they may decide to act courageously.

        1.1.3.2.4. Pro:
        The quantum uncertainty [48], or randomness, in the brain is a self-caused cause and, thus, constitutes free will.

      1.1.3.3. Pro:
      Libet's [49] experiments show that humans make decisions before they are aware of doing so. As such, by the time we feel like we are making a choice, unbeknownst to us, the decision has already been made.

        1.1.3.3.1. Con:
        The methods and findings of the Libet studies have been challenged (p. 47 [50], p. 3 [51]).

          1.1.3.3.1.1. Pro:
          Libet's findings are based on an interpretation of brain activity that has been debunked [52], undermining conclusions that are drawn from them about free will.

            1.1.3.3.1.1.1. Pro:
            Researchers have cautioned against drawing conclusions about free will based on Libet's findings, which are based on the notion of bereitschaftspotential (BP [53]) or readiness potential.

          1.1.3.3.1.2. Con:
          Despite Libet's results remaining an unsolved mystery of the brain, his findings have been replicated [54].

            1.1.3.3.1.2.1. Pro:
            Some of Libet's findings were later replicated by Kreiman and Haynes with more sophisticated technology (p. 555 [55], p. 33 [56]).

            1.1.3.3.1.2.2. Con:
            There is no consensus [57] among researchers about the significance of different findings, their meaning, or what conclusions may be drawn from the Libet studies.

              1.1.3.3.1.2.2.1. Pro:
              Testing simple mechanical decisions may be an especially bad way to test for free will, which may be reserved for those decisions which arise in the most authentic, natural, and creative contexts (p. 63 [58]).

              1.1.3.3.1.2.2.2. Pro:
              Libet's studies are based on the assumption that decision-making is instantaneous, when in fact the brain activity of a decision could be ongoing or could build up over time (p. 53 [59]). This would explain Libet's counterintuitive results and create room for free will.

              1.1.3.3.1.2.2.3. Con:
              Even if scientific debates about the Libet studies are ongoing, the fact that some of the findings have been replicated warrants further investigation and gives the studies some credibility.

          1.1.3.3.1.3. Pro:
          Two methodological limitations of Libet's studies are the lack of standardization of how time is measured and the use of self-reporting, both of which undermine the results (p. 3 [60]).

        1.1.3.3.2. Pro:
        Libet's studies reveal that the brain seems to activate prior to the will [61] to act, rather than following a willful decision. This makes the will an effect, rather than a cause of action.

      1.1.3.4. Pro:
      It is plausible that the human brain is complex enough to generate human behaviors, thus eliminating the need to appeal to a non-physical entity like the will.

        1.1.3.4.1. Pro:
        Machines using algorithms that are modeled after the human brain can generate [62] intelligible, unpredictable, and articulate art. This does not mean that computers have free will.

          1.1.3.4.1.1. Pro:
          It is a mistake to associate the creativity of machine art to free will because machine learning algorithms are only capable of synthesizing and mimicking [63] datasets of human art.

            1.1.3.4.1.1.1. Con:
            AlphaGo makes moves in the games Go and Chess that are "creative, surprising, and beautiful [64]," indicating an ability to effectively respond to unique situations that are not anticipated by their programming.

          1.1.3.4.1.2. Con:
          The limitations of machine learning actually provide evidence that free will is difficult to generate with complex brain-like machinery. This indicates there is something distinctly human about free will.

        1.1.3.4.2. Con:
        Neuroplasticity suggests that the individual and their choices shape the physical structure of the brain (p. 1 [65]).

          1.1.3.4.2.1. Pro:
          For example, learning to play the guitar [66] physically changes the brain and improves auditory processing and hand coordination.

        1.1.3.4.3. Con:
        According to the predictive processing framework (PPF, p. 529 [67]), living things mentally simulate probable outcomes of actions and their surroundings to decide how to act based on the result. This demonstrates that the brain is hardwired to facilitate making choices and, thus, free will.

          1.1.3.4.3.1. Con:
          Simulating and evaluating possible outcomes does not preclude determinism, especially if one is hardwired to do so.

        1.1.3.4.4. Pro:
        As the most complex [68] organ in the human body, the brain is constantly making new connections and is responsible for our experiences, thoughts, and actions.

      1.1.3.5. Con:
      Humans are considered to be conscious beings, and consciousness, which refers to the self and the choices it can make, would not exist if there were no free will.

        1.1.3.5.1. Con:
        It is possible to theorize about humans as conscious living beings without positing the existence of a willful self, such as with the Buddhist rejection of the reality of the self (p. 141 [69]).

        1.1.3.5.2. Pro:
        Emotions [70] and bodily sensations that guide action would serve no evolutionary function if we could not choose how to respond to them (p. 4 [71]).

          1.1.3.5.2.1. Con:
          Emotions do not provide evidence for the existence of free will because human beings are bound by their emotions.

            1.1.3.5.2.1.1. Con:
            Psychological research indicates that human beings can willfully regulate [72] their emotions, which supports the existence of free will.

          1.1.3.5.2.2. Con:
          Since human beings evolved from simpler organisms - animals - that likely lacked free will, human beings likely also lack free will. It is much less likely that humans are, somehow, the only animals with free will.

            1.1.3.5.2.2.1. Pro:
            It is possible to interpret the evolutionary selection for cognitive capacities deterministically as an increase in complexity rather than freedom.

            1.1.3.5.2.2.2. Con:
            Animals do not behave deterministically and demonstrate agency [73] in similar ways to humans, which suggests that both humans and animals could have free will.

            1.1.3.5.2.2.3. Con:
            There are characterizations of evolutionary theory, like natural drift, which reject the idea that organisms are passive and subject to natural selection and argue that organisms play active roles in evolution (p. 116 [74]).

          1.1.3.5.2.3. Pro:
          Pain is an intrinsic neurological response that protects us [75] by informing us about potential danger or harm.

            1.1.3.5.2.3.1. Con:
            Our reactions to pain are conditioned responses of the Autonomic Nervous System (ANS [76]) that form from past experience. Thus, they are better understood as evidence for determinism than for free will.

              1.1.3.5.2.3.1.1. Con:
              The implications of conditioning for pain responses actually indicate that the aversion to pain can be unlearned [77], and thus that such responses are not predetermined.

    1.1.4. Con:
    Activities that involve choices provide evidence for the existence of free will.

      1.1.4.1. Pro:
      Expressive activities, like creating art or music, are not merely determined responses to environmental inputs, and thus should be considered evidence for the existence of free will.

        1.1.4.1.1. Con:
        -> See 1.1.3.4.

        1.1.4.1.2. Pro:
        According to libertarian free will, genuine creativity (L-creativity) can only exist if our artistic actions and ideas originate within us and are not derivative (p. 594 [78]).

          1.1.4.1.2.1. Pro:
          If we are not the originators of our ideas, then our ideas would lack novelty and lose their value to us (p. 595 [79]).

          1.1.4.1.2.2. Con:
          It is possible to preserve other forms of creativity (C-creativity) even if our actions are determined by our circumstances (p. 596 [80]).

            1.1.4.1.2.2.1. Pro:
            Paul Russel argues that there is more to creativity than being the originator of our ideas, because we often recognize the importance of works of art even if the artist is influenced by their circumstances (p. 296 [81]).

        1.1.4.1.3. Con:
        It is still possible to conceive of creativity in a deterministic world, as we can still attribute one's actions to a set of unique underlying mental states and character traits (p. 602 [82]).

        1.1.4.1.4. Pro:
        Making art and music are expressive activities that depend on the abilities of the artist to create something new, which necessitates free will.

          1.1.4.1.4.1. Con:
          Human and primate art has been linked with the evolutionary pressure to attract a mate, meaning that it could the result of a biological impulse [83] rather than a free expression.

            1.1.4.1.4.1.1. Con:
            Even if art began as a biological impulse, the article does not provide evidence that links the impulse to reproduce to modern forms of expression.

          1.1.4.1.4.2. Pro:
          If our creative actions are predetermined, it seems unlikely that we would see the depth and variety of art styles, genres, and mediums that have existed throughout human history.

      1.1.4.2. Pro:
      People consider and weigh the benefits of various courses of action countless times in their lives. They would not do that if there were no choices available to them.

        1.1.4.2.1. Pro:
        The ability to regret [84] some of our decisions suggests that free will and choice exists.

          1.1.4.2.1.1. Con:
          It is possible to interpret regret deterministically as the processes of learning about something unfavorable after the fact.

          1.1.4.2.1.2. Pro:
          Having regrets [85] is common and often helps an individual to change their behavior and make choices about the future of their lives.

        1.1.4.2.2. Pro:
        The fact that human beings are capable of making choices and taking steps to overcome their circumstances, preferences, cravings, urges, and desires is evidence that free will exists.

          1.1.4.2.2.1. Pro:
          The simple exercise of suppressing an innate desire, such as choosing not to snack when feeling hungry, supports the idea that there actually is free will.

          1.1.4.2.2.2. Con:
          The human drive to pursue pleasure generally [86] seeks immediate gratification and often promotes behaviors that are not in the person's best long-term interests.

            1.1.4.2.2.2.1. Pro:
            Instincts and intuition are associated with unconscious [87] processes and thus should not be confused with willful activity and consciousness.

            1.1.4.2.2.2.2. Con:
            Even if seeking immediate pleasure is a strong drive in humans, our ability to resist urges or temper desires [88] suggests that free will exists in some sense.

          1.1.4.2.2.3. Con:
          One's choices to overcome their circumstances are determined by the incentives that precede them, leaving no room to intervene (p. 3 [89]).

          1.1.4.2.2.4. Pro:
          The fact that people are able to overcome physical addictions by force of will - despite addictive desires remaining - indicates that humans retain the ability to choose against even the strongest determinants of behavior.

            1.1.4.2.2.4.1. Pro:
            One researcher argues that smoking does not contradict free will, because the motivation caused by smoking does not make it impossible to stop smoking (p. 67 [90]). The act of quitting smoking is an example of free will.

              1.1.4.2.2.4.1.1. Con:
              The conditioning required to quit smoking indicates the absence of free will, because if we could freely choose to not be addicted to something, no conditioning would be needed.

            1.1.4.2.2.4.2. Con:
            The significant effort it takes to overcome addiction suggests that overcoming addiction could be the result of our circumstances and support, rather than our free will.

        1.1.4.2.3. Con:
        Our decisions and perceptions are inevitably shaped by our past experiences [91].

          1.1.4.2.3.1. Con:
          Even if our experiences and choices are influenced by past experiences, the ability to break with tradition to try something new is evidence of free will.

          1.1.4.2.3.2. Pro:
          Biased [92] perceptions and judgments are associated with unconscious thinking that is tied to our past experiences and upbringing.

      1.1.4.3. Con:
      Every decision is determined by one's beliefs, preferences, and circumstances, none of which are within one's control [93].

        1.1.4.3.1. Pro:
        When presented with the right evidence, we have no choice but to believe what we believe.

          1.1.4.3.1.1. Con:
          People often hold incoherent or false beliefs [94], even when presented with sufficient evidence to the contrary.

          1.1.4.3.1.2. Pro:
          There is a general trend of people holding gradually more coherent beliefs [95] over time, which suggests that discovering evidence leads us to update our beliefs.

            1.1.4.3.1.2.1. Con:
            While there is a general trend toward coherence in psychology, the psychological concept of coherence refers to how we emotionally [96] and cognitively integrate with circumstances, rather than how raw evidence persuades us.

        1.1.4.3.2. Con:
        People often make random guesses or choices. These are not determined by their beliefs or circumstances.

          1.1.4.3.2.1. Pro:
          Humans are able to make decisions randomly through the use of tools like dice or coins.

            1.1.4.3.2.1.1. Con:
            Events like coin flips only appear random and unpredictable to observers because it is difficult to measure all of the forces that influence them (p. 0 [97]).

            1.1.4.3.2.1.2. Con:
            If the outcomes of the dice are uncontrollable, then making decisions based on those outcomes means that the basis for these decisions is not within one's control.

            1.1.4.3.2.1.3. Pro:
            Even if someone strongly believes that doing something is wrong, they can choose to leave their actions up to chance by flipping a coin.

          1.1.4.3.2.2. Con:
          Picking a random option only seems like acting freely because it makes it more difficult to identify the determinant cause of the decision.

            1.1.4.3.2.2.1. Con:
            Despite this, the ability to choose between available options indicates both that there are multiple possible outcomes and that we are free in some sense to choose between them.

            1.1.4.3.2.2.2. Pro:
            Much of what seems like random behavior will still likely be influenced by one's impulses and circumstances.

        1.1.4.3.3. Con:
        If this were true, psychologists could predict human behavior. Given the difficulties of studying the links between beliefs, preferences, circumstances, and behavior, it is unlikely that every decision is predetermined based on these alone.

          1.1.4.3.3.1. Con:
          While it is troubling that psychology struggles to predict human behavior, this could simply mean that psychological methods are primitive or unscientific.

            1.1.4.3.3.1.1. Pro:
            Many regard psychology as a soft science [98], and thus distinct from hard sciences like physics and biology.

          1.1.4.3.3.2. Pro:
          Psychologists consider human beings to be naturally unpredictable [99].

          1.1.4.3.3.3. Pro:
          Psychology is facing a replication problem [100] because its studies often assume that all human beings would act in similar ways, while human actions vary greatly.

        1.1.4.3.4. Pro:
        Introspection reveals that our preferences are not freely chosen.

          1.1.4.3.4.1. Pro:
          One's circumstances (country of birth, genetics, family, etc.) are predetermined without choice. These closely affect our preferences.

            1.1.4.3.4.1.1. Con:
            Genetics are not static and epigenetic research [101] indicates that genes are shaped by our choices, circumstances, and experiences.

          1.1.4.3.4.2. Pro:
          We tend to learn [102], rather than choose, to like the taste of certain foods.

          1.1.4.3.4.3. Con:
          Pavlovian conditioning [103] demonstrates that one can learn to like something they originally disliked through positive association.

            1.1.4.3.4.3.1. Con:
            If we could choose our preferences, then conditioning would not be necessary. Thus, the need for extensive conditioning to change a preference is evidence that we do not choose our preferences.

            1.1.4.3.4.3.2. Con:
            B.F. Skinner [104], the psychologist responsible for operant conditioning [105], argued that the discovery of behavioral conditioning eliminated the need for free will to explain human actions and choices.

            1.1.4.3.4.3.3. Pro:
            Human beings are capable of changing their long-held preferences and behaviors through positive reinforcement [106], such as rewards that follow an experience or action.

  1.2. Pro:
  Human actions and choices can be traced back to external, deterministic causes.

    1.2.1. Pro:
    The first rule [107] of causation is that an effect cannot cause itself. Given that free will depends on one's will causing itself to act, free will cannot exist.

      1.2.1.1. Pro:
      Galen Strawson's Basic Argument [108] demonstrates that if a will were responsible for its actions, it would create an endless regress (p. 15 [109]).

        1.2.1.1.1. Con:
        Physical determinism theoretically implies that it is possible to compute the states of the universe in the future with complete knowledge of the universe at an initial state. This is impossible because physical states, including the initial state, are indeterminate.

          1.2.1.1.1.1. Pro:
          The Three-Body Problem [110] in physics is problematic for physical determinism because the gravitational motion among three or more bodies is generally chaotic and thus indeterminate.

        1.2.1.1.2. Con:
        The first assumption of the apparent regress is already a denial of self-determination and free will, making the whole regress a circular argument.

        1.2.1.1.3. Pro:
        To be truly responsible for how one is at time-point (0), they must have brought it about via time-point (-1). However, to have been responsible for how one was at time-point (-1), one must have been responsible for how one was at time-point (-2). This regress is endless (p. 15 [111]).

          1.2.1.1.3.1. Con:
          Even if the time-point (-1) situation constrains the available choices, claims that free will exist actually mean that some choices remain at time-point (0), independent of time-point (-1).

      1.2.1.2. Pro:
      For a will to be truly free, it must be capable of full self-determination [112] and remain unperturbed by external influences.

        1.2.1.2.1. Con:
        The requirements of complete self-determination and freedom from influence are unreasonable and arbitrary. The capacity to overcome some external influences is sufficient for free will.

        1.2.1.2.2. Pro:
        Most libertarian philosophers adopt either an event- or agent-causal [113] account, which ground the will to act in the agent and detail how willful actions resist external influences.

          1.2.1.2.2.1. Pro:
          Event-causal argue that external factors that influence behavior contain a degree of indeterminacy and that free will emerges from that indeterminacy.

            1.2.1.2.2.1.1. Pro:
            Robert Kane famously argues that, in truly difficult or strained decisions, there are deterministic forces pulling agents in multiple directions, implying the existence of some indeterminacy [114].

            1.2.1.2.2.1.2. Con:

        1.2.2.1.4. Con:

          1.2.2.1.4.1. Con:

    1.2.3. Con:

      1.2.3.3. Con:

      1.2.3.4. Con:

          1.2.3.4.1.3. Con:

        1.2.3.4.2. Con:

        God's purported omnipotence and omnipresence would necessarily dictate that all choices would proceed according to God's will, not ours.

          1.4.3.3.1.1. Con:
          The impact of God's omnipotence on human free will depends on the content of God's will, not just its existence.

          1.4.3.3.1.2. Pro:
          Free will represents the ability to do otherwise, which is incompatible with an omnipotent God because it would limit the limitless power of God.

            1.4.3.3.1.2.1. Con:
            Omnipotence necessitates having the ability to create and bend logic by divine will to grant human beings free will.

        1.4.3.3.2. Pro:
        If an omniscient God can see the future, this implies the existence of a predetermined future, thereby contradicting free will.

          1.4.3.3.2.1. Con:
          There is an infinite number of possible outcomes out of a single action. Thus, an omniscient being could know all of them without constraining your choice.

          1.4.3.3.2.2. Pro:
          By definition, something that is predetermined cannot be determined again, which renders our choices inert.

        1.4.3.3.3. Con:
        Free will could be the result of God's omnibenevolence [232], where God's love of humanity and free will overwrites God's abilities to predict (omniscience [233]) and control (omnipotence [234]) our actions.

        1.4.3.3.4. Con:
        It is possible to posit a type of non-classical God who willingly blinds himself to our choices and thus preserves free will.

          1.4.3.3.4.1. Con:
          Most notions of God assume omniscience. For God to know everything, but not know everything that we may choose at the same time is incoherent.

          1.4.3.3.4.2. Pro:
          Most Christians hold that God is omniscient, but willingly took on a limited perspective while living as Jesus, a human being. This indicates that it is possible for God's omniscience to co-exist [235] with free will.

      1.4.3.4. Pro:
      God's reward to obedient human beings requires obedience to be freely chosen.

        1.4.3.4.1. Con:
        There are several Bible verses [236] that suggest that humans do not have free will and that God acts through them.

        1.4.3.4.2. Pro:
        Many Bible verses suggest that humans must freely chose obedience [237] to God to live morally.

    1.4.4. Con:
    Doctrines of predestination [238] proclaim that a monotheistic deity has predetermined the lives and salvation of mankind, which is incompatible with free will.

      1.4.4.1. Pro:
      Islam contains the concept of Al-Qadr [239], or the belief that all of our lives are predestined by Allah, which is incompatible with free will.

      1.4.4.2. Con:
      Human beings are capable of breaking religious rules and divine edicts, proving that humans have some form of free will.

      1.4.4.3. Con:
      Predestination in modern Christianity is distinct from determinism, affirms free will [240], and only refers to God's power to choose who will be saved.

      1.4.4.4. Con:
      Some theologians distinguish [241] between the notion of free will associated with making choices and the notion of free will associated with predestination and salvation, meaning that predestination may be compatible with free will.

      1.4.4.5. Pro:
      Some forms of Hindu Monotheism [242] hold that an omnipotent deity determines the fates of all human beings based on what pleases or interests them.

        1.4.4.5.1. Con:
        The Hindu faith teaches that, though we do not choose the circumstances we are now in and the karma that will result from our choices, we are free to choose [243] our actions.

        1.4.4.5.2. Pro:
        The Vaisnava sect of Madhva (1238–1317) school of Hinduism held that the God Visnu could predetermine which souls would be damned on a whim [244].

  1.5. Con:
  Free will and determinism are not mutually exclusive since freedom and determinism can be defined in compatible [245] ways.

    1.5.1. Pro:
    Compatiblism is a widely held [246] position within the academic community, which suggests that it is plausible in many different forms.

      1.5.1.1. Con:
      The quoted survey only offers libertarian free will, compatibilism, and hard determinism as options (p. 5-6 [247]). This is not representative of the variety of views in the debate.

      1.5.1.2. Pro:
      A consensus among academics is more credible than the opionions of the general public.

        1.5.1.2.1. Con:
        Academics are often wrong [248], even where there is consensus. Blindly believing them would is very dangerous.

        1.5.1.2.2. Con:
        The general public believes in free will [249].

      1.5.1.3. Con:
      Historically, many widely held positions have been incorrect.

    1.5.2. Pro:
    Hard determinism entails contradictions and paradoxes, so a weaker form of determinism that is compatible with free will should be accepted to address these challenges.

      1.5.2.1. Con:
      Even if it is only our rational faculties that we have control over, under compatiblism, this would require us to plan our thoughts, thinking about them before they arise in our mind, which is impossible.

        1.5.2.1.1. Con:
        Improvisation requires no planning. Thus, if thoughts are improvised, they can be free without being planned.

          1.5.2.1.1.1. Con:
          In music (p. 9 [250]), it is generally understood that improvisation is actually rapidly planned composition based on previous musical knowledge and extensively practiced expertise (p. 293 [251]).

        1.5.2.1.2. Con:
        Free will implies an ability to deliberate and choose among one's thoughts, preferences, and desires while not being beholden to them. This does not require us to preemptively produce our thoughts.

        1.5.2.1.3. Pro:
        Our thoughts control our actions; otherwise, our actions are involuntary. If we are not the author of our thoughts, and our thoughts dictate our actions, then we are not the author of our actions.

      1.5.2.2. Pro:
      While it would be possible to predict a strictly determined future with detailed knowledge of the present, we would be paradoxically unable to act upon that knowledge.

        1.5.2.2.1. Con:
        The capability for willful action requires that some predictions would have to be ambiguous, in order to statically represent interactions with those who will hear the predictions.

        1.5.2.2.2. Pro:
        If one was willing and able to defy a predicted action, then it would seem the prediction could not be made.

      1.5.2.3. Con:
      When a proposition produces contradictions and paradoxes, it may indicate a need to reconsider other propositions, rather than being proof that the proposition is unsound.

      1.5.2.4. Pro:
      Hard determinism is self-refuting [252] because it undermines the truth and falsity of our beliefs. Beliefs about the world cannot be true or false in a world where all beliefs are predetermined.

    1.5.3. Con:
    Determinism of any kind is incompatible [253] with free will because, if true, it would significantly limit the control we have over our actions and choices.

      1.5.3.1. Pro:
      Incompatibilists [254] argue that we can only have free will if we are the ultimate cause of our actions, which would be impossible under determinism.

        1.5.3.1.1. Con:
        Without compatible forms of free will and determinism, it would be impossible to explain how our constraints [255] create opportunities for action.

        1.5.3.1.2. Pro:
        According to the Consequence Argument [256], free will is incompatible with a future that is determined by the laws of nature and the facts of the past.

      1.5.3.2. Con:
      The philosopher Jean-Paul Sartre [257] argues that human freedom is unlimited, despite the forces that constrain and limit our actions.

        1.5.3.2.1. Pro:
        For Sartre [258], human freedom is guaranteed because we are spontaneous, conscious beings that cannot help but make choices.

        1.5.3.2.2. Con:
        The fact that our actions are constrained and our choices are always limited in some sense contradicts [259] the idea that human freedom is unlimited.

          1.5.3.2.2.1. Con:
          Sartre rejects this contradiction by distinguishing between the freedom to act [260], which is constrained by physical forces, and the freedom of the will, or make choices, which is a characteristic of consciousness.

          1.5.3.2.2.2. Pro:
          If humans actions are physically and socially constrained, then any so-called freedom they would have beyond that would be meaningless.

            1.5.3.2.2.2.1. Con:
            In his book 'Being and Nothingness', Sartre uses his character Pierre's difficult decision between helping his grandmother and joining the French Resistance to demonstrate that our choices are meaningful [261] to us in virtue of the effects they have on our lives.

        1.5.3.2.3. Pro:
        Sartre adopts a phenomenological [262] approach stating that, insofar as we experience having choices and must make decisions, it is inconsequential to suggest that we do not have free will (p. 40 [263]).

          1.5.3.2.3.1. Con:
          Sartre's view is based on a conception of humans as emerging as a totally free blank slate [264], with no meaningful natural instincts or desires. This overlooks the many ways human nature deprives humans of true freedom.

      1.5.3.3. Con:
      Compatibilists, like Harry Frankfurt, reject [265] arguments that free will is characterized by the ability to do otherwise by demonstrating that it is possible to be morally responsible for an act while not having that ability.

        1.5.3.3.1. Pro:
        Frankfurt gives the example [266] of a person who has a chip implanted in her brain that will make her walk her dog even if she chooses not to. Without engaging the chip, she chooses to walk her dog, making her morally responsible [267] for her decision despite being unable to act otherwise.

          1.5.3.3.1.1. Con:
          Frankfurt-Style Cases (FSC [268]) have been challenged for being outlandish works of science fiction, which undermines their credibility.

            1.5.3.3.1.1.1. Con:
            Many thought experiments rely on outlandish scenarios. The point is not to provide a realistic counterexample, but to challenge our intuitions about everyday situations.

            1.5.3.3.1.1.2. Pro:
            The philosopher Daniel Speak [269] warns that the hypothetical elements in FSC cases are so far removed from reality that they are not useful for moral circumstances on Earth.

              1.5.3.3.1.1.2.1. Con:
              Frankfurt's first formulation of such cases did not rely [270] on any especially outlandish details.

          1.5.3.3.1.2. Pro:
          Frankfurt would argue that, despite not having the freedom to act otherwise, we would still intuitively hold her responsible because her actions are in line with her second-order desires (p. 1 [271]).

        1.5.3.3.2. Con:
        While it is counterintuitive to hold someone morally responsible for an action that they could not have prevented, there is more to having free will than just being able to be held morally responsible - like being able to make independent decisions.

    1.5.4. Con:
    The concepts that compatibilists seek to preserve, such as moral responsibility, become more difficult to understand without assuming that individuals can determine their actions freely.

      1.5.4.1. Pro:
      Intuitively, most people would not hold an individual responsible for committing an act if they could not have done otherwise. This implies that freedom is necessary for moral responsibility.

      1.5.4.2. Con:
      P.F. Strawson famously argues [272] that our reactive attitudes to others' actions are independent of any conception of freedom and can easily sustain belief in moral responsibility.

    1.5.5. Pro:
    Classical compatibilists [273] define free will as the ability to act in ways that one wants, which means that even determined acts can be free if they align with one's wishes.

      1.5.5.1. Con:
      A person is not always aware of exactly what their motives are. As such, it is not presently possible to use this as an argument for or against free will (p. 13 [274]).

      1.5.5.2. Con:
      The classical compatibilist [275] notion of free will is often considered imprecise because of cases in which it would label certain actions free when it is clear that the person is not acting of their own free will, such as during hallucinations [276].

        1.5.5.2.1. Pro:
        While it is possible for someone experiencing immersive hallucinations [277] to act based on their desires at the time, they would not be considered to be acting of their own free will.

        1.5.5.2.2. Con:
        Reasons-responsiveness [278] theorists address this by arguing that an agent is only free if they are responsive to a range of rational concerns, which would exclude unresponsive individuals and those experiencing hallucinations.

          1.5.5.2.2.1. Pro:
          Reasons-responsiveness theory [279] holds that free will is linked to the ability to respond to rational concerns and that our actions and behaviors are based on our rational responses. Thus, if we are rationally responsive, then our actions are free.

      1.5.5.3. Pro:
      Many contemporary compatibilists [280] argue that freedom and determinism must be compatible because determinism does not prevent you from doing anything that you want to do and are capable of doing.

        1.5.5.3.1. Con:
        Despite our everyday experiences, the Consequence Argument [281] establishes that free will and determinism are not compatible because we lack the power to change both the past and the future.

          1.5.5.3.1.1. Con:
          Compatibilists reject the Consequence Argument [282] by differentiating between the ability to change the past, which is impossible, and the ability to consider what someone could have done differently in the past.

        1.5.5.3.2. Pro:
        Contemporary compatibilists like Fara, Vihvelin, and Smith argue that our dispositions [283] to act ensure that we are free to make free choices despite living in a deterministic world.

      1.5.5.4. Pro:
      Susan Wolf argues that if one is motivated to do the right thing [284] and act in accordance with their duties, then they are necessarily free. This is true even if the act was predetermined.

      1.5.5.5. Con:
      Desires are not chosen, therefore even if the action is aligned with ones wants, it is not compatible with free will.
