Discussion Name:
Time for a Maximum Wage? Should the US Limit CEO Pay?

1. Thesis:
US companies should be prohibited from paying any employee more than 35 times the compensation of their lowest-paid employee.

  1.1. Pro:
  A wage cap would benefit [1] the economy.

    1.1.1. Con:
    The existence of highly paid, wealthy executives is beneficial to society as a whole.

      1.1.1.1. Pro:
      High paid business leaders often use their wealth for philanthropic efforts.

        1.1.1.1.1. Con:
        Psychological studies indicate that [2] individuals of low socio-economic status are more prosocial than those of high social class as they face more risks and have more interest in egalitarian values and compassion.

          1.1.1.1.1.1. Con:
          High earners have more disposable income that they can, and often do, give to charity. This makes the amount given to charity [3] by high earners larger than what is given by lower earners.

            1.1.1.1.1.1.1. Con:
            High earners may give a larger amount to charity, but lower earners give a higher percentage of their income to charity, meaning that the proportion of charitable funding [4] given by high earners is actually lower than their lower earning counterparts.

            1.1.1.1.1.1.2. Pro:
            High earners may also use their wealth to fund research and development [5], support startups, or invest in socially responsible businesses, which can have positive impacts on society beyond traditional charitable giving.

          1.1.1.1.1.2. Pro:
          Other research [6] corroborates this by showing a link between social power and lower levels of distress as well as compassion in the face of other individuals' suffering.

        1.1.1.1.2. Con:
        The money that rich people give does not necessarily go to public causes and, especially, those in need.

          1.1.1.1.2.1. Pro:
          Of the 50 largest charitable gifts [7] made in the US in 2016, only one went to an organization primarily dedicated to social services, and almost 60% [8] went to specific colleges and universities, further benefitting existing elites and rich institutions.

          1.1.1.1.2.2. Pro:
          Business leaders may flaunt their philanthropic efforts with seemingly large donations, but the actual amount they give is often questionable.

            1.1.1.1.2.2.1. Pro:
            Generous donations often have conditions attached [9], such as media attention or donor control, which can limit the positive impact of the contribution.

            1.1.1.1.2.2.2. Pro:
            Mark Zuckerberg gave $100 million to education, but after the money spent on consultants and related expenses, very little money actually reached the intended recipients [10].

            1.1.1.1.2.2.3. Con:
            The Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation is the largest charitable organization [11] in the world.

          1.1.1.1.2.3. Con:
          This is not bad because it allows investment in things which are underappreciated. The SETI program [12], for instance, would not exist if not for massive donations by a Russian oligarch.

            1.1.1.1.2.3.1. Con:
            It is unfair for only a small portion of society to decide which underappreciated areas receive funding, while essential needs like education and healthcare remain inadequately funded.

            1.1.1.1.2.3.2. Pro:
            Only the ultra-wealthy have sufficient additional funds to invest in projects that might be deemed low-priority otherwise.

        1.1.1.1.3. Pro:
        Together, the top 50 American donors gave $5.6 billion in 2016 [13] for philanthropic purposes.

          1.1.1.1.3.1. Con:
          In the US, individuals with high income give a smaller share [14] of their incomes to charity than do those with low income. The middle class is the most generous [15]. Less income inequality might lead to more charitable contributions.

            1.1.1.1.3.1.1. Con:
            Even if this is true, the total sum of charitable contributions might be much lower without the mega-donations of rich people.

            1.1.1.1.3.1.2. Pro:
            This is likely because the ultra-rich have most of their income in non-liquid forms [16] that prevent them from donating freely.

          1.1.1.1.3.2. Pro:
          These 50 individuals alone gave about 1.4% [17] of the total charitable contributions that Americans made in 2016.

        1.1.1.1.4. Pro:
        Often, certain philanthropic efforts require enormous sums of money to get off the ground. High paid executives can provide the level of capital necessary to achieve success.

          1.1.1.1.4.1. Pro:
          Few individuals or even philanthropic organizations could finance a university on their own, as George Soros has done with the Central European University in Hungary. It has an endowment by him of around $800 million [18].

          1.1.1.1.4.2. Pro:
          Research Institutes that would otherwise be hard to establish are often propelled by donations of rich individuals, for example the Huntsman Cancer Institute [19] which has received more than $250 million by Jon Huntsman Sr..

          1.1.1.1.4.3. Con:
          Enhancing the economic situation of low-paid workers could decrease the need for philanthropic assistance.

      1.1.1.2. Con:
      Successful businesses often force their employees to endure undignified working conditions and salaries. Their success, therefore, often does not trickle down.

        1.1.1.2.1. Pro:
        Amazon has been accused of [20] destroying jobs, providing low-income and insecure employment and harsh working conditions for many of its more than 500,000 employees.

        1.1.1.2.2. Pro:
        In large companies, managers rise through the ranks by increasing the surplus value [21] extracted from the employees beneath them.

          1.1.1.2.2.1. Pro:
          Promotions and career advancements within large companies are frequently based on performance metrics and meeting financial objectives [22]. Managers who consistently demonstrate their ability to generate higher profits and extract more value from their employees are often rewarded with promotions and increased responsibilities.

        1.1.1.2.3. Con:
        The extra profit companies make from these practices allows them to charge lower prices for their goods, making them more affordable for all consumers.

      1.1.1.3. Con:
      A salary cap wouldn't necessarily do away with highly paid executives. Companies will increase salary for the lowest workers to keep current levels of executive pay.

      1.1.1.4. Pro:
      Large salaries allow the wealthy to save and invest, a crucial function in any economy.

        1.1.1.4.1. Pro:
        Highly paid business leaders create and invest in new companies. Without their high income, they would not have been able to invest.

          1.1.1.4.1.1. Con:
          Elon Musk, like most wealthy entrepreneurs, made his fortune primarily through sales of equity [23], not pay.

          1.1.1.4.1.2. Pro:
          This is exemplified by Elon Musk, who invested in companies [24] like Tesla Motors, Space X, Solar City, and safe AI research, which wouldn't have been possible if his lowest paid employee earned $285,715.

        1.1.1.4.2. Pro:
        Angel investors' [25] role in funding companies is important for innovation. Such investments are less likely with wage caps.

          1.1.1.4.2.1. Pro:
          Studies show [26] that new companies that receive funding from angel investors have higher success rates than other companies, possibly because of the mentorship and advice that angel investors often give.

          1.1.1.4.2.2. Pro:
          In 2016, almost 65,000 companies [27] in the US received funding from angel investors. The volume of these investments amounts to about a third [28] of what large venture capital firms have invested into startups during that year.

          1.1.1.4.2.3. Con:
          Angel investors demand high returns [29] on their investments which harm early startups.

          1.1.1.4.2.4. Con:
          Angel investors receive a company's equity and future earnings [30] as part of their investment, which makes their role comparable to that of bank loans.

        1.1.1.4.3. Con:
        Increased salaries for the poor and middle class would allow them to save as well, whereas right now most Americans have very little in savings. [31]

    1.1.2. Pro:
    Less inequality within a company tends to make it more efficient and productive.

      1.1.2.1. Pro:
      Salary differences in the US are excessively large [32], to the point where they no longer increase productivity.

        1.1.2.1.1. Con:
        Most executives make approximately five times [33] the median salary of US workers. Sampling only the largest companies [34] in the US results in numbers being skewed much higher.

        1.1.2.1.2. Pro:
        According to estimates, at least 85% [35] of the companies in the S&P 500 index pay their CEOs more than 35 times as much as the median worker. The difference between the CEO's pay and the lowest-paid employee is even larger.

        1.1.2.1.3. Con:
        America's historically high inequality is what allowed it to become the biggest economy [36] in the world. As such, it cannot be too high.

        1.1.2.1.4. Pro:
        Very few rich nations have a higher Gini coefficient [37] than the US.

        1.1.2.1.5. Pro:
        The wages of an average worker already outpace [38] their productivity growth.

          1.1.2.1.5.1. Con:
          The cited article [39] attributes some of the rise in wages to state-mandated increases in the minimum wage.

          1.1.2.1.5.2. Pro:
          The rate of total worker compensation growth has been outstripping [40] the rate of total corporate profit growth for decades.

          1.1.2.1.5.3. Con:
          US minimum wage employees make less than MIT's Living Wage Calculator [41] says is necessary for a single adult in all 50 states [42].

      1.1.2.2. Con:
      Surveys indicate that, of the employees who are aware of the levels of executive pay in the company they work for, about 80% [43] believe that they are at an appropriate level, suggesting a lack of frustration.

      1.1.2.3. Con:
      If less inequality creates more productivity, there is little reason for instituting a maximum wage. Firms will anyway have a market incentive to reduce internal inequality.

        1.1.2.3.1. Pro:
        Companies that overpay executives without regard for productivity cannot compete with efficient firms in the long run, which motivates them to reduce internal inequality.

        1.1.2.3.2. Con:
        Companies often have short-term incentives [44] to make as much profit in as little time as possible so as to placate their boards of directors.

      1.1.2.4. Pro:
      More equitable pay would improve company cultures [45] and increase employee morale.

        1.1.2.4.1. Con:
        Companies may aim to raise the minimum or lower the maximum wage, neglecting to improve wages for all, resulting in an unchanged median income and potentially undervalued middle-income workers.

          1.1.2.4.1.1. Con:
          Reducing the pay ceiling would make middle income people feel more valued as they would be earning more relative to their bosses.

          1.1.2.4.1.2. Pro:
          This would leave the majority of people in a company with an unchanged income, which would fail to improve company morale.

          1.1.2.4.1.3. Con:
          Middle income employees are not being systemically undervalued or denied living wages the way that low income employees are.

        1.1.2.4.2. Con:
        Studies of S&P 500 companies indicate that there is no visible correlation [46] between pay inequality and employee satisfaction.

        1.1.2.4.3. Pro:
        If a CEO can only raise their own salary when the staff have also been compensated appropriately, there will be less backlash when executive pay increases.

          1.1.2.4.3.1. Pro:
          Data suggests [47] that many businesses are not rewarding staff as much as they are rewarding executives. This decreases morale among staff.

          1.1.2.4.3.2. Con:
          Surveys indicate that more than half [48] of employees do not know how much the executives of their companies are being paid.

          1.1.2.4.3.3. Pro:
          Whole Foods caps executive salaries at 19 times the amount of the lowest-paid worker, citing [49] increased solidarity and company unity.

      1.1.2.5. Pro:
      A 28-year study [50] found that employee job satisfaction increases the value of the firm.

        1.1.2.5.1. Pro:
        Employee satisfaction is positively [51] correlated with company performance. The '100 Best Companies to Work For' in America' outperform their peers by 2-3% per year.

    1.1.3. Con:
    Exceptionally high earners disproportionally contribute to the government's tax revenue.

      1.1.3.1. Pro:
      The top 10% of earners in the USA pay over half of all federal income tax [52] even though they account for only 45% of all income.

        1.1.3.1.1. Con:
        The wealthiest 10% of Americans, which presumably overlaps a good deal with the top 10% of earners, own 76% of the wealth [53]. If anything, they are paying too little tax.

        1.1.3.1.2. Pro:
        A salary cap would primarily hit those with an income of over $250,000 per year. This group [54] currently includes only about 3% of tax payers, yet accounts for more than half of all income tax paid - which equals about a quarter [55] of all federal government revenue.

        1.1.3.1.3. Pro:
        In 2014, those earning over $250,000 per annum contributed 51.6% [56] of all individual income taxes.

        1.1.3.1.4. Pro:
        When Switzerland was debating the introduction of a 12:1 salary cap, the policy was forecast to lead to major financial losses.

          1.1.3.1.4.1. Pro:
          A study estimated that, even if the salaries cut at the top are paid to other workers, the country would lose 0.5 to 1 billion Francs in tax revenue. Additionally, up to a billion could be lost in social security contributions. Under all scenarios the bottom line would be negative (pp. 8-10 [57]).

          1.1.3.1.4.2. Con:
          Many supporters still voted for it on moral grounds [58], claiming that greater equality was more important than growth rates.

        1.1.3.1.5. Con:
        Company executives and upper management "continue, however, to deduct the majority of their executive compensation [59], with these deductions costing the US Treasury an estimated $7.5 billion per year."

      1.1.3.2. Con:
      Raising wages for people, especially at the bottom, reduces the demand for public services and, therefore, the costs of funding them.

        1.1.3.2.1. Pro:
        Spending on safety net programs for low income individuals and families currently accounts for about 10% [60] of the federal budget.

        1.1.3.2.2. Pro:
        Many companies pay their employees so little that many of them qualify for food stamps, Medicaid and other taxpayer-funded assistance programs. For Walmart alone, higher wages would save the government billions of dollars (p. 3 [61]).

        1.1.3.2.3. Con:
        If overall pay increased, inequality would also increase, making low-level workers as poor as they are currently. As such, they would likely need to remain reliant on public services.

      1.1.3.3. Con:
      If companies raise wages to keep CEO pay high, more people will pay higher taxes, maintaining similar overall levels of tax collection.

      1.1.3.4. Pro:
      The prospect of becoming a wealthy executive fuels the American dream and the aspirations of individuals. With a salary cap, there will be a feedback loop, producing even fewer high earners in the future.

        1.1.3.4.1. Con:
        The rhetoric of the American Dream leads to discrimination against the poor. Almost half [62] of Christians in the USA believe that people who live in poverty are poor because they are lazy.

        1.1.3.4.2. Con:
        America has much less social mobility than many other comparable countries [63].

        1.1.3.4.3. Pro:
        People are more motivated to work and compete harder if they know the next level up offers a big salary jump.

          1.1.3.4.3.1. Pro:
          Research indicates that 48% of appointments of managerial positions result from reorganization within a company. This demonstrates that a degree of mobility does exist with in companies (p. 7 [64]).

          1.1.3.4.3.2. Con:
          A situation where a CEO is paid 20 times - rather than 40 times - what the average worker is paid would surely provide enough of a disparity to present an incentive to workers.

        1.1.3.4.4. Pro:
        If low-skill workers are highly compensated, high-skill employees may choose to quit and work in easier jobs for the same pay.

          1.1.3.4.4.1. Con:
          Companies often leave pay gaps [65] between employee rankings to avoid devaluing the employment of more skilled or experienced workers. Under a salary cap, the lowest paid worker will still be paid less than employees on higher rungs of the corporate ladder.

          1.1.3.4.4.2. Pro:
          If the difference in pay is reduced, less people will be willing to commit to the increased hours, pressure and risk associated with working as a CEO.

          1.1.3.4.4.3. Con:
          CEOs themselves [66] claim that reaching their position was not an ambition which drove them. It is workers devotion to their jobs and the business that motivates them to become CEO.

    1.1.4. Pro:
    The US economy as a whole would benefit from a CEO maximum wage.

      1.1.4.1. Con:
      A maximum wage for CEOs would lead to fewer small businesses being founded.

        1.1.4.1.1. Con:
        The CEOs of startups typically aren't compensated exorbitantly [67].

        1.1.4.1.2. Pro:
        Income inequality incentivizes people to be creative and innovate [68] so that they can capture surplus wealth.

        1.1.4.1.3. Pro:
        The high salaries given to CEOs provide an incentive for people to start businesses.

      1.1.4.2. Pro:
      A rise in the share of income for the bottom 20% promotes growth, whereas a rise in the income of the top 20% slows it (p. 5 [69]).

        1.1.4.2.1. Con:
        Even if richer people spend less of their income, since they have much more income, more money goes back into the economy.

        1.1.4.2.2. Con:
        The increased income for the bottom 20% is more likely to go towards personal expenditure and is not significant enough for bigger, more profitable long-term investments.

        1.1.4.2.3. Pro:
        More money spent in the economy means an increase in demand for products. Each purchase of goods creates more income [70] for the supplier, which can then be invested.

        1.1.4.2.4. Pro:
        Americans who earn over $150,000 per annum save [71], on average, about 16% of their income, while those earning under 20,000 save an average of about 3%.

        1.1.4.2.5. Pro:
        The lowest-earning third of Americans spend, on average, 182% [72] of their income; the highest-earning third spend only 61% of their income. Increasing the lowest incomes will increase consumer spending.

      1.1.4.3. Con:
      Companies achieving high profits create the most significant benefits for society as a whole, by growing the economy and creating new jobs.

        1.1.4.3.1. Con:
        Reducing CEO pay can result in more profit for research and development, leading to better products and possible scientific breakthroughs.

        1.1.4.3.2. Pro:
        Government support could disincentivize people [73] from working hard and improving their economic situations.

          1.1.4.3.2.1. Pro:
          A redistribution of wealth would disrupt the work-leisure trade off [74] in society and make indolence more attractive.

          1.1.4.3.2.2. Pro:
          Higher wages for jobs that require higher education [75] qualification incentivize people to gain more education and skills.

          1.1.4.3.2.3. Con:
          Most people are driven to work by passion and a feeling of usefulness [76]. Higher wages are, therefore, unlikely to diminish people's work drive.

      1.1.4.4. Con:
      High executive pay attracts high quality [77] executives. Without such executives, companies will be less efficient and productive.

        1.1.4.4.1. Con:
        The prestige and power held by CEOs is what attracts candidates. While pay is also important, the dramatic pay disparity seen within companies at present may not be necessary.

          1.1.4.4.1.1. Pro:
          Companies have a number of ways [78] to paint CEOs in a very favourable light. This provides the person with as much notoriety and esteem as it does the company.

          1.1.4.4.1.2. Pro:
          Rich CEOs tend to have a large amount of power over the economy and politics. This would not change with a salary cap (p. 1 [79]).

          1.1.4.4.1.3. Con:
          If this were true, boards of directors would have sought to reduce executive pay to invest more money in their company or just to increase their compensation.

        1.1.4.4.2. Con:
        If executive positions at companies become less attractive, more highly-qualified individuals might choose more socially benefical jobs, for example in politics or the public sector.

        1.1.4.4.3. Pro:
        Attracting good senior level employees is critical to a company's performance.

          1.1.4.4.3.1. Pro:
          Limiting CEO pay reduces their incentive to succeed and grow the company, negatively impacting all employees.

            1.1.4.4.3.1.1. Pro:
            Over the past decades, the influence of CEOs on a company's success has been increasing (p. 825 [80]).

            1.1.4.4.3.1.2. Con:
            The elevation of CEO influence could be due to their valorization rather than their effectiveness, leading companies to seek exceptional CEOs and create environments that facilitate their success (p. 828 [81]).

          1.1.4.4.3.2. Con:
          Many foreign executives are paid far less than their American counterparts to run companies that perform just as well as their American counterparts.

            1.1.4.4.3.2.1. Pro:
            The head of Ford Motors made more than the heads of Toyota, Honda, and Nissan combined [82], despite being outperformed.

            1.1.4.4.3.2.2. Con:
            Compared to CEOs in other countries, those in the US have greater influence and responsibility and fewer restrictions on their actions, and therefore, receive higher pay (p. 767 [83]).

          1.1.4.4.3.3. Con:
          A high salary is an unnecessary and wasted investment if the executive does not stay with the company for more than a few years.

            1.1.4.4.3.3.1. Con:
            The increase in CEO turnover may be attributed to companies becoming more skilled at identifying when CEOs are not performing to sufficient standards.

            1.1.4.4.3.3.2. Con:
            Even if an executive only stays with a company for a few years, their tenure can be valuable in terms of implementing strategic initiatives and achieving short-term objectives, which can set the company up for long-term success.

            1.1.4.4.3.3.3. Pro:
            Following the departure of a CEO, the company's stock price becomes volatile [84], as the company's strategic future is uncertain.

            1.1.4.4.3.3.4. Pro:
            Companies lose [85] some sense of identity when a CEO leaves, resulting in decreased unity and cultural cohesion.

            1.1.4.4.3.3.5. Pro:
            Nearly one-fifth of CEOs [86] were replaced in 2015, a record high since 2000.

          1.1.4.4.3.4. Pro:
          The growth in pay for US CEOs has corresponded with a comparable growth in the size of US companies [87]. This indicates that the ability to attract talented executives with generous compensation packages has been crucial to growth.

          1.1.4.4.3.5. Con:
          High pay [88] often does not result in high performance [89].

            1.1.4.4.3.5.1. Con:
            In a free market, high compensation is tied to high performance. Otherwise, business owners would seek more competent alternatives who can offer equivalent value.

            1.1.4.4.3.5.2. Pro:
            The performance of executives barely determines their pay; a much better indicator for executives' pay is the size of companies (p. 329 [90]).

              1.1.4.4.3.5.2.1. Pro:
              A large proportion of CEO pay is derived form bonuses which do not fluctuate based on performance. [91] This means that it is an arbitrary allocation of money, not a reward for value created for the company by the CEO.

            1.1.4.4.3.5.3. Con:
            Research has shown that high-performing employees are often compensated more [92] than their peers, which suggests that there is a positive correlation between pay and performance.

            1.1.4.4.3.5.4. Pro:
            A study of over 2,500 CEOs found that their income, including salary and bonus, was not affected [93] by how their decisions affected the company.

        1.1.4.4.4. Pro:
        Companies who have to employ many lower level workers will be unable to offer sufficiently high salaries to attract quality management.

          1.1.4.4.4.1. Con:
          Even with a compensation cap, executive pay could be very generous. The highest paid workers in a company that employs others at the federal minimum wage [94] could still make it comfortably into the current top 1% [95] of earners.

          1.1.4.4.4.2. Pro:
          A potential executive might be more likely to choose a position at a boutique firm [96] instead of a large one, because those other companies would have a higher pay cap.

        1.1.4.4.5. Pro:
        If American CEOs were asked to take a drastic cut in pay or to increase their company's payroll cost dramatically, there would be an incentive for them to work for companies outside the United States.

          1.1.4.4.5.1. Con:
          Pay cuts to CEO salaries would likely be normalized after time, creating a new norm that is accepted by all executives.

            1.1.4.4.5.1.1. Pro:
            Similar to how sports leagues implement maximum contracts, [97] when people know there is an artificial maximum to how much they can earn, they adjust their internal evaluation of the offers they receive.

            1.1.4.4.5.1.2. Pro:
            In 1983, CEO salaries were 41 times [98] higher than regular employee salaries. However, top executives did not emigrate to other countries to a significant extent.

            1.1.4.4.5.1.3. Con:
            The time between legalizing and enforcing pay cuts to when they are normalized in society could harm businesses significantly.

            1.1.4.4.5.1.4. Con:
            When expectations and norms dictate lower salaries, people become even more attracted to higher salaries.

          1.1.4.4.5.2. Pro:
          The globalization of business [99] has made it easier for CEOs to work for companies outside their home country, and they may be more likely to do so if faced with lower pay or increased payroll costs in the US.

          1.1.4.4.5.3. Con:
          If the US institutes this policy, other western liberal democracies may follow suit. This would prevent the US from losing high level executives.

            1.1.4.4.5.3.1. Con:
            The difference [100] between European and American work culture demonstrates that Europeans do not simply follow the practices of the US.

            1.1.4.4.5.3.2. Pro:
            The enormous cultural reach [101] of the US means that foreign media devotes lots of coverage to American domestic issues. Often, this mean that political trends in America are followed by similar trends in Europe and elsewhere.

          1.1.4.4.5.4. Con:
          People don't tend to leave their country of origin, friends and family that easily. There is unlikely to be a 'mass exodus.'

          1.1.4.4.5.5. Pro:
          When Ben and Jerry's tied the pay of their highest paid executive to the average wage of the company, they faced signficant trouble [102] finding quality executives.

            1.1.4.4.5.5.1. Con:
            According to the article quoted, Ben and Jerry's had to raise the pay ratio from 5:1 up to 7:1 to attract a new executive. Eventually, the ratio was raised to 17:1 until bought out by Unilever. This is still less than 35:1.

      1.1.4.5. Pro:
      Almost 40% [103] of US employers report that domestic labor alone cannot supply the level of skilled employees needed. Increasing wages for foreign employees would attract workers [104] from poorer countries to migrate to the US.

  1.2. Pro:
  Surveys indicate that the public would support a maximum wage policy.

    1.2.1. Con:
    Basing policy decisions on a pure majority opinion is just mob rule [105] and allows the larger group to take from the smaller one, even if it's only slightly bigger.

      1.2.1.1. Con:
      In the case of CEOs, the larger group can benefit by making an incredibly smaller group [106] of people slightly worse off.

      1.2.1.2. Con:
      This majority opinion is not simply a case of 'mob rule'. The majority of the American people are morally entitled to restitution from high-earning CEOs.

        1.2.1.2.1. Pro:
        Since many CEOs have amassed their wealth by directly harming their employees [107], it is justifiable for society to decrease their compensation.

        1.2.1.2.2. Con:
        It is not the role of the government or the majority of voters to determine what individuals or groups are morally entitled to.

      1.2.1.3. Pro:
      The protection of individual rights and freedoms is important in any democratic society, even if it goes against the will of the majority.

      1.2.1.4. Pro:
      A pure majority opinion may not necessarily represent the best decision for society as a whole, as the majority can be swayed by emotion or misinformation.

    1.2.2. Con:
    If the introduction of a salary cap was of high importance to the electorate, it would feature more prominently in the public discourse and election campaigns of politicians.

      1.2.2.1. Con:
      If people were more aware of the magnitude [108] of inequality they would want the government to act to reduce it. US citizens believe that wealth distribution in the US is far more equal than it actually is.

        1.2.2.1.1. Con:
        9 of 10 Americans say they would boycott a company for 'bad corporate behaviour [109]'. This illustrates how customers can demonstrate their dissatisfaction with a company's actions.

        1.2.2.1.2. Pro:
        A recent survey found that, when American employees were asked to guess the amount their company's CEO was payed, they estimated a tenth [110] of their actual income.

      CEOs have several other non-income methods for receiving funds from their company.

        1.5.4.3.1. Con:
        In the last decade, efforts to increase bookkeeping transparency in companies has helped to cut down [242] the use of alternative forms of compensation for CEOs, like stock options, that increase regardless of performance.

        1.5.4.3.2. Pro:
        Some companies provide lifestyle compensation [243] to CEOs, including paying for private school tuition for children, a country club membership, and private planes or cars available for personal and family use.

        1.5.4.3.3. Pro:
        Many American CEOs have symbolic salaries of $1 [244] but receive significant compensation through stock options, dividends, assets and bonuses in the tens of millions.

      1.5.4.4. Con:
      The IRS [245] could investigate companies and punish those that circumvent the new laws.

  1.6. Pro:
  Salary caps would reduce income inequality.

    1.6.1. Con:
    Other mechanisms to reduce inequality would be more effective than a salary cap.

      1.6.1.1. Pro:
      Increasing the minimum wage would reduce income inequality better than a salary cap would.

        1.6.1.1.1. Con:
        The majority of the research to date [246] finds that minimum wage laws in the USA increase unemployment, particular for young people and low-skilled workers.

        1.6.1.1.2. Pro:
        Raising the federal minimum wage to $15, as politicians like Bernie Sanders have proposed [247], would give about 42% of American [248] workers a raise.

        1.6.1.1.3. Con:
        The value of the minimum wage has declined [249] by 24% since 1968. State-mandated adjustments for inflation have proven insufficient. By contrast, executives keep their salaries inflation-adjusted.

      1.6.1.2. Pro:
      Increasing progressive tax rates and introducing wealth taxes would reduce income inequality more effectively than a maximum wage.

        1.6.1.2.1. Con:
        Using the tax system to exert downward pressure on CEO pay has proven to be ineffective and is unlikely to be pursued in earnest.

          1.6.1.2.1.1. Pro:
          The ultra-rich have a variety of ways [250] to hide their non-income wealth from tax enforcement agencies.

          1.6.1.2.1.2. Pro:
          Wealth-based taxes, like capital gains taxes, are typically significantly lower [251] than income tax levels.

          1.6.1.2.1.3. Con:
          There exists a significant popular push [252] to target the wealth of the ultra-rich, which may make such policies easier to implement than income-related policies.

          1.6.1.2.1.4. Con:
          Wealth taxes have been successfully implemented [253] in the past which led to real redistribution.

        1.6.1.2.2. Pro:
        Between 1967 and 2015, the mean income of the lowest-earning 80% of Americans rose by 24%, while that of the top 20% increased by 84%. Taxation would be a more effective solution to this inequality (pp. 31, 35 [254]).

        1.6.1.2.3. Con:
        Most of the growth in incomes during the past decades for the top quintile of American earners went to the lower 19% [255].

        1.6.1.2.4. Pro:
        Wealth inequality is a more significant contributor [256] to economic inequality in the US than income inequality. A wealth tax would be a better solution to inquality.

          1.6.1.2.4.1. Pro:
          A report by UNU-WIDER [257] indicates that relative income inequality has been decreasing while wealth inequality continues to grow.

          1.6.1.2.4.2. Con:
          Tackling income inequality with salary caps does not preclude the tackling of wealth inequality through other means.

          1.6.1.2.4.3. Pro:
          In the United States, those making $10 million a year usually receive only around 15% [258] of their wealth from salaries.

          1.6.1.2.4.4. Con:
          Two-thirds of the increase in American income inequality over the last four decades can be attributed to the rise in wages [259] for top earners.

            1.6.1.2.4.4.1. Con:
            This study neglects the effect of taxation. In terms of after-tax incomes [260], 99% of the population have seen their income grow between 2000 and 2010. Americans with low incomes have benefitted the most.

              1.6.1.2.4.4.1.1. Con:
              In terms of after-tax income from 1979 to 2010 [261], Americans with high incomes have seen more growth than those with low income. No one has seen more growth than the top 1% of earners.

            1.6.1.2.4.4.2. Pro:
            The income inequality between the top 5% of households in the US and the bottom 95% has been steadily growing over the past decades. As this trend is poised to continue, intervention is urgently necessary before things become even worse (pp. 31-35 [262]).

      1.6.1.3. Con:
      Other such policies require more political capital to be enacted and will provoke more opposition [263]. As salary caps affect only a relatively small number of people, they are easier to implement.

      1.6.1.4. Pro:
      With an unemployment rate of 4.3% [264], the US should prioritise increasing employment opportunities rather than reducing inequality.

      1.6.1.5. Con:
      Lowering the incomes of executives and capital owners can reduce their political influence by reducing the money they can spend on lobbying or special interest groups.

    1.6.2. Pro:
    Inequality can produce dramatic economic and social issues [265].

      1.6.2.1. Pro:
      Income inequality is a threat to American democracy [266].

        1.6.2.1.1. Con:
        In a democracy, every individual, regardless of wealth, has one vote, meaning that both poor and rich individuals have equal influence on the outcome of an election.

        1.6.2.1.2. Con:
        There have been plenty of highly unequal societies [267] - including with those with worse levels than the US, like Argentina or Brazil - with functional democracies.

        1.6.2.1.3. Pro:
        Income inequality leaves people discontent and generates pressure for income redistribution, which could lead to political upheavel and the creation of a non-democratic but anti-systemic or redistributive regime (p. 3 [268]).

          1.6.2.1.3.1. Pro:
          Income inequality undermines national unity and popular rule by contributing to a sense of class divide. [269]

          1.6.2.1.3.2. Pro:
          Donald Trump's election has been characterized [270] as a working-class revolt against elites.

            1.6.2.1.3.2.1. Con:
            Trump's support was not primarily from the poor and working-class. Trump voters' median income was higher [271] than the statewide median income in nearly half of the US states.

            1.6.2.1.3.2.2. Pro:
            Trump and his supporters oppose an establishment that they view as anti-working class. [272]

          1.6.2.1.3.3. Con:
          Predictions that income inequality will undermine democratization are "at best weakly supported by the empirical evidence" (p. 1 [273]).

        1.6.2.1.4. Con:
        As long as people, regardless of class, have access to the institutions of government and are protected by the rule of law, income inequality should not be a concern.

          1.6.2.1.4.1. Pro:
          If the state guarantees procedural equality, instead of income equality, it ensures that all persons possess the minimum necessary level of personal freedom to exist and succeed in society.

          1.6.2.1.4.2. Con:
          It has been argued that America has de facto different justice systems [274] for the rich and the poor. For example the former can hire expensive lawyers whereas the latter are dependent on over-worked court-appointed counsel.

        1.6.2.1.5. Pro:
        Wealth can buy political influence [275], thus great inequality results in unequal political influence, undermining the foundations of any democracy.

          1.6.2.1.5.1. Con:
          Lobbying is driven by the regulation of money's impact on politics [276], rather than the size of payments.

            1.6.2.1.5.1.1. Con:
            Until McCutcheon v. F.E.C. [277] is successfully challenged, efforts to reduce undue influence of wealth in politics must work around that ruling, and CEO pay caps are but one such measure.

          1.6.2.1.5.2. Pro:
          Studies have shown that over 70% of Americans [278] believe that the economic system favors the powerful.

          1.6.2.1.5.3. Pro:
          The rich use their money and connections [279] to lobby politicians for their own benefit.

            1.6.2.1.5.3.1. Pro:
            Research has suggested [280] that lobbying for policies related to financial institutions and facilitating risky lending behaviour may have been a primary cause in the most recent financial crash in the USA.

            1.6.2.1.5.3.2. Pro:
            During the 2016 federal election cycle, almost a third [281] of the money raised by campaigns' super PACs was given by 23 wealthy donors. In total, these individuals gave $470 million.

            1.6.2.1.5.3.3. Con:
            Such lobbying is most likely to occur in industries that are over-regulated [282] and competition is limited. This is, therefore, a problem that has little to do with executive pay levels.

      1.6.2.2. Con:
      The US should first focus on alleviating poverty rather than implementing a salary cap.

        1.6.2.2.1. Pro:
        As long as everybody has the financial resources to live the life they want, it does not matter how much other people earn in relative terms.

        1.6.2.2.2. Con:
        Inequality can have negative effects even if poverty rates are low. Thus, it is not enough to tackle poverty alone.

          1.6.2.2.2.1. Pro:
          High income inequality causes low social mobility (pp. 1, 57 [283]). This means it can also produce more poverty in the long-term.

          1.6.2.2.2.2. Con:
          Even if this is true, the negative effects [284] associated with poverty are worse, making it a more significant issue.

          1.6.2.2.2.3. Con:
          Studies have found no significant correlation [285] between income inequality and health outcomes.

          1.6.2.2.2.4. Pro:
          There is strong evidence that income inequality causes poorer health [286].

            1.6.2.2.2.4.1. Con:
            While income inequality may play a small role in health disparities, there is also evidence that health behaviors [287], genetics [288], and environmental factors [289] play significant roles in determining health outcomes.

            1.6.2.2.2.4.2. Pro:
            An increase in wealth among some of the population leads to an increase in the products demanded by them at a higher cost. This increase in demand relative to supply leads to an increase in the cost [290] of medical services.

            1.6.2.2.2.4.3. Pro:
            The poorest quartile of American men live, on average, 15 years [291] less than the richest.

          1.6.2.2.2.5. Pro:
          Income inequality in the US has been linked to an increased prevalence of child maltreatment [292].

        1.6.2.2.3. Pro:
        More than 13% of Americans currently live in poverty. That makes it a more important and urgent issue than inequality (p. 12 [293]).

        1.6.2.2.4. Con:
        Implementing a salary cap does help in alleviating poverty. [294]

      1.6.2.3. Pro:
      Research suggests a correlation between income, happiness and life satisfaction. Income inequality thus translates into inequality of well-being (p. 1 [295]).

        1.6.2.3.1. Con:
        For many, the feelings of inadequacy created by relative poverty are based on the wealth of others being greater, not just a lack of resources [296]. This means that many of the problems of inequality will persist.

          1.6.2.3.1.1. Con:
          While this policy will not eradicate income inequality, it will decrease it. This means that it will, at least, mitigate unhappiness caused by income inequality.

          1.6.2.3.1.2. Pro:
          Social comparison theory [297] suggests that people evaluate their self-worth by comparing themselves to others. Such comparisons can lead to lower self-esteem if they compare themselves to those who earn more, even if their own income meets their needs.

        1.6.2.3.2. Pro:
        Wealth disparity increases feelings of sadness for the poorest, because of the feeling of lack of control which poverty imposes [298]. Countries with higher measures of income inequality have a higher prevalance of depression [299].

        1.6.2.3.3. Pro:
        A study confirmed that Americans were on average less happy [300] when income inequality was higher.

      1.6.2.4. Con:
      Income inequality may not directly impact the lives of many US citizens, due to social safety nets [301] and government assistance programs which mitigate its effects.

        1.6.2.4.1. Pro:
        The US government economic security programs [302] provide food assistance, housing subsidies, and working-family tax credits which bolster income and help families afford basic needs.

        1.6.2.4.2. Con:
        It is often extremely difficult to survive on welfare alone in the US. An unemployed person would make a little over $1,000 [303] a month with tax deductions, and medical insurance.

    1.6.3. Con:
    Insofar as income inequality arises naturally, it should not be corrected through state redistribution.

      1.6.3.1. Pro:
      Free market systems naturally lead to income inequality [304], and the state cannot resolve it simply by injecting money into society.

      1.6.3.2. Con:
      The state has an obligation to [305], and often does, take steps to protect workers from exploitation.

        1.6.3.2.1. Pro:
        Capitalists generally see workers as an expense, which they tend to cut first [306], in order to make a profit whenever a business goes through a loss.

        1.6.3.2.2. Con:
        A situation is imaginable in which a company has enormous differences in pay yet even the worst-paid employee makes enough money to be well-off. This would not be exploitation.

        1.6.3.2.3. Con:
        The state should not intervene on behalf of a worker who voluntarily accepted a job and the associated wage.

          1.6.3.2.3.1. Pro:
          In a free society, individuals should be responsible for their choices. The state should not decide what is a 'good' or 'bad' choice.

          1.6.3.2.3.2. Con:
          The state intervenes [307] in pay disputes, particularly in situations where an employee is being underpaid by a firm or paid below the minimum wage.

        1.6.3.2.4. Pro:
        Laws already exist [308] to address anti-discrimination, working conditions, and unfair dismissal.

      1.6.3.3. Con:
      Even if income inequality arises naturally, that does not mean that it should not be counteracted.

    1.6.4. Pro:
    America's global reputation is harmed by significant income inequality, making it challenging to exert [309] soft power.

      1.6.4.1. Con:
      America retains the most soft power [310] of any country in the current world. As a result, high income inequality is not an especially significant problem for American power projection.

      1.6.4.2. Pro:
      Several countries [311] are considering salary caps, meaning enough people in those countries believe this is a good thing. In this case, the US would gain in reputation in these countries.

      1.6.4.3. Pro:
      The US has historically criticized high executive pay and promised to constrain corporate greed. Following through on this promise would improve the international and domestic credibility of US politicians.

        1.6.4.3.1. Pro:
        During his term, President Obama set a $500,000 pay cap [312] for senior executives whose institutions were receiving large bailout sums.

        1.6.4.3.2. Con:
        Despite his criticism of high executive pay, in 2007, President Bush encouraged a move towards performance-based pay but said [313] "[g]overnment should not decide the compensation for America's corporate executives".

        1.6.4.3.3. Con:
        With the introduction of salary caps, American companies would become a symbol for a practice that many international business people will consider harmful and want to avoid.

        1.6.4.3.4. Pro:
        In 2016, Hillary Clinton [314] and Bernie Sanders [315], were outspoken against high CEO pay and, in the case of Sanders, even "largely built his presidential campaign around that fight."

        1.6.4.3.5. Pro:
        President Trump criticised pharmaceutical companies for extortionate profit making, however failed to match his rhetoric with any material policy change [316].

    1.6.5. Pro:
    Proportional salary caps give executives an incentive to significantly increase the wages of their lowest paid to avoid significant salary losses.

      1.6.5.1. Con:
      Companies may fire their lowest-paid workers [317] instead of increasing their salaries.

        1.6.5.1.1. Pro:
        With a maximum wage, companies would be incentivized to reduce their overall payrolls through increased automation. [318]

          1.6.5.1.1.1. Pro:
          Increasing automation would reduce the number of jobs [319], increasing unemployment and putting the lowest-skilled, lowest-paid workers in a worse position than they were in before.

          1.6.5.1.1.2. Con:
          Small companies often lack the necessary capital to invest in automation. [320]

        1.6.5.1.2. Pro:
        If American companies had to pay higher wages to American workers, they might choose to outsource their labor [321], resulting in fewer American jobs and higher unemployment.

          1.6.5.1.2.1. Pro:
          Most major companies are already outsourcing [322], offshoring or automating [323] low-skill jobs.

          1.6.5.1.2.2. Con:
          Companies lose control [324] over their employees when they outsource labor.

        1.6.5.1.3. Pro:
        Wage caps may lead corporations to substitute low-wage jobs with contractor work, which is less regulated [325], often lower paid and does not guarantee the same number of working hours.

          1.6.5.1.3.1. Con:
          Subcontracting can sometimes be more expensive [326] than hiring contract workers, as they may be less efficient and familiar with the company. For this reason, many companies choose not to make this switch.

          1.6.5.1.3.2. Pro:
          In 2015, nearly 6% [327] of US workers were self-employed, providing services to companies on an individual contract basis.

          1.6.5.1.3.3. Pro:
          An increased reliance on subcontractors exacerbates income inequality [328] by disincentivizing promotion or training as they are contracted solely for one task.

          1.6.5.1.3.4. Con:
          In the US, it's illegal [329] to hire contractors to act in a role that would normally be an employee's role. There are also tax and cost disincentives for companies who take this illegal action.

          1.6.5.1.3.5. Con:
          Salary cap laws could be defined to include contractors.

          1.6.5.1.3.6. Pro:
          These kinds of contracts can include abusive "at-will contracts [330]" or "zero-hour contracts [331]" which make both pay and hours extremely inconsistent and destroys financial security for entry-level workers.

        1.6.5.1.4. Con:
        Boards of directors prioritize shareholders [332] over the top employees of a company and refrain from making decisions that harm the company to benefit highly paid executives.

        1.6.5.1.5. Con:
        Most companies will likely need to retain at least some lower paid workers.

          1.6.5.1.5.1. Pro:
          Many companies will still need full-time, low-wage employees in the US, for example, to work in warehouses [333] or shipping.

          1.6.5.1.5.2. Con:
          Even if they must retain some workers, companies may offset the additional costs of salary raises by cutting back on other benefits like training, flexible work conditions, and opportunities for advancement.

      1.6.5.2. Pro:
      Companies make a profit by keeping costs of production low - especially the cost of labor [334]. Without interference, they have every incentive to keep wages low.

      1.6.5.3. Con:
      Market incentives already exist to increase compensation for low-paid employees.

        1.6.5.3.1. Con:
        Given that top CEOs currently earn hundreds of times [335] more than the average worker - a difference which is rapidly increasing - market incentives to increase wages clearly aren't working.

          1.6.5.3.1.1. Pro:
          According to the Economic Policy Institute [336], the CEO-to-worker compensation ratio in the United States peaked at 376:1 in 2000 and was 303:1 in 2014. In 1965, the ratio was more reasonable, at 20:1.

          1.6.5.3.1.2. Pro:
          From 1978 to 2014, inflation-adjusted CEO compensation increased [337] 997% while the typical worker's annual compensation only grew by 10.9% over the same period.

          1.6.5.3.1.3. Con:
          Wages have, on average, grown in the USA [338] since 1960.

        1.6.5.3.2. Pro:
        Most US employees make more [339] than the minimum wage, even though it is not required by government. Only 2.7% of hourly workers make the minimum wage or less.

        1.6.5.3.3. Pro:
        Information about compensation is freely available [340], if shareholders, employees, clients or customers wish to lobby for change.

          1.6.5.3.3.1. Con:
          Employees are the only ones who are likely to benefit from fairer wages, and they are also the least empowered [341] to lobby for change.

          1.6.5.3.3.2. Con:
          In the absence of a salary cap, shareholders and clients, who have the most influence in lobbying for change, are also the least interested [342] in implementing fair wage policies.

          1.6.5.3.3.3. Pro:
          Prospective employees determine a fair wage before accepting a job and therefore are aware of whether the wage is exploitative.

        1.6.5.3.4. Con:
        Shareholder-elected board [343] of directors determine the amount of a company's revenue distributed as dividends. Without more incentive they will look to give more money to top-earners.

          1.6.5.3.4.1. Pro:
          Shareholders' primary interest is profit [344]. Higher wages for low-skill workers would eat into this.

          1.6.5.3.4.2. Pro:
          To become a shareholder, one must invest in purchasing shares [345]. In companies that can afford to pay high wages to top earners, it is probable that those who can afford to buy shares are equally or more wealthy.

          1.6.5.3.4.3. Con:
          Shareholders are unlikely to lobby against decreasing CEO pay. They have a financial incentive [346] to want money to be reinvested in the company or spent on derivatives.

        1.6.5.3.5. Con:
        Given that the government has to support [347] tens of millions of working adults with more than $150 billion each year in payments, these market-based incentives are clearly not working.

          1.6.5.3.5.1. Con:
          Much of this has to do with the 'welfare cliff [348]': some people stay on government assistance because taking a raise would result in a loss in net income

          1.6.5.3.5.2. Pro:
          A free market incentivizes to seek out lower labor costs [349], which leads to lower wages and poorer working conditions for employees.

        1.6.5.3.6. Pro:
        Companies offering competitive worker pay can attract [350] high-performing CEOs through higher salaries, providing an incentive to increase worker compensation.

      1.6.5.4. Pro:
      Top earning CEOs currently earn about 300 [351] times more than the average worker in their companies. Companies would have to reduce the pay of CEOs to just over a tenth to fulfill the 1:35 ratio.

        1.6.5.4.1. Con:
        In order to fit the proposed salary cap, Chipotle and Walmart [352] would either have to cut the pay of its CEOs by at least 97% or increase the pay of half of its workforce by at least a factor of 40 to an annual salary of $825,000 or more.

        1.6.5.4.2. Con:
        The average CEO compensation in the top 350 US companies is $13 million. Creating a 35:1 ratio while keeping CEO salaries fixed would require a minimum salary of $400,000. This would be impossible to finance (p. 3 [353]).

        1.6.5.4.3. Pro:
        It would be a radical step to only adjust the wages of those paid the most, as their pay would have to be reduced significantly. Instead, companies are likely to react by changing pay at both ends [354].
