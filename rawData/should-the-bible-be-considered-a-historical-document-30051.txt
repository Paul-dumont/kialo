Discussion Name:
Should the Bible be considered a historical document?

1. Thesis:
The Bible should be considered a historical document.

  1.1. Con:
  Many of the claims from the Bible cannot be explained by modern science's understanding of how the universe works.

    1.1.1. Pro:
    The Old Testament [1] claims that the Canaanites - early settlers of the Near East - were wiped out after God ordered the Israelites to destroy the cities of Canaan and its people. DNA evidence [2] however, suggests that the Canaanites survived to contribute their genes to millions of people.

      1.1.1.1. Pro:
      Archaeological evidence suggests [3] that Canaanite cities were never abandoned or destroyed.

        1.1.1.1.1. Pro:
        If Canaanite cities were never abandoned or destroyed, then the whole story of Israel as a distinct group is challenged. It is just as plausible to regard the Israelites [4] as a particular group of Canaanites. But this substantially changes the history of the whole Old Testament.

          1.1.1.1.1.1. Pro:
          If the Israelites really did descend from Canaanites (and other nearby ethnicities), then it would make more sense to read the history told in the Old Testament not as a factual telling of what happened, but rather as a mythological retelling of the "origin story" of a distinct egalitarian community centered around the worship of Yahweh.

      1.1.1.2. Con:
      The Bible doesn't make the claim that the Canaanites were wiped out, and it indicates that the Canaanites lived right into the New Testament.

        1.1.1.2.1. Pro:
        References such as “Canaanites living in the hill country, the Negev and the western foothills” (Judges 1:9 [5]) and "Canaanites living in Hebron (Judges 1:10 [6]) show that the Old Testament narrative accepts the continuance of the Canaanites beyond the conquest.

        1.1.1.2.2. Pro:
        The New Testament [7] mentions Canaanites living at the time of Jesus.

        1.1.1.2.3. Pro:
        Deuteronomy 7 [8] refers to a command for the Israelites to wipe out the Canaanites. It doesn't refer to the Israelites actually successfully doing so.

          1.1.1.2.3.1. Pro:
          In fact, the Old Testament claims that the Israelites were punished for their failure to do fully drive out the Canaanites Judges 2:11-12 [9].

    1.1.2. Pro:
    The Bible's depiction of the origin of the universe has been debunked by scientific evidence.

      1.1.2.1. Con:
      The Framework interpretation of Genesis [10] successfully reconciles the Biblical creation account with science.

        1.1.2.1.1. Pro:
        According to this view [11], the days are not chronological. They are logically arranged into two triads, where in the first triad God prepares creaturely kingdoms and in the second triad God fills each kingdom with various creatures.

        1.1.2.1.2. Pro:
        According to this view [12] "Genesis was written to combat the polytheism and pantheism of other creation stories," not "to provide a scientifically accurate account of the creation."

      1.1.2.2. Con:
      The existence of a state of nothingness before the universe still leaves room for the discussion that God caused the Big Bang [13] and hence the creation of the universe.

      1.1.2.3. Pro:
      Evidence [14] of the Big Bang Theory as a naturally occurring phenomenon challenges the Biblical view that God created the universe [15] Himself.

        1.1.2.3.1. Con:
        God could have created the universe using the Big Bang.

    1.1.3. Pro:
    Modern science has revealed alternative explanations that are more plausible for phenomena that were once considered the miracles of God.

      1.1.3.1. Con:
      It is possible that science has not advanced enough to be able to prove the existence of the miracles mentioned in the Bible.

        1.1.3.1.1. Pro:
        Science has yet to conclusively determine [16] the reasons behind the origin of life in the universe.

        1.1.3.1.2. Con:
        While there is always room for new evidence to revise our sense of what is or is not possible, we are justified in believing that things which fall outside of our current understanding of the possible probably did not happen. The burden of proof is on those what want to assert that they probably did happen.

          1.1.3.1.2.1. Pro:
          To assert that science not having conclusively disproven something is sufficient reason to believe that it might exist, is a form of burden of proof shifting that relies on a fallacious [17] argument from ignorance.

      1.1.3.2. Pro:
      Demonic possessions [18] that have been mentioned in the Bible were discovered to be seizures caused by epilepsy/ epileptic seizures [19] in sick patients.

        1.1.3.2.1. Con:
        If the demonic possession in Mark 9:20-22 [20] was actually epilepsy, that wouldn't explain why "[the demon] has often thrown him into fire or water to kill him." Things such as stress and lack of sleep can trigger seizures [21], but there's no evidence that being around fire and water can.

        1.1.3.2.2. Con:
        It is incredibly difficult to diagnose a disease whose symptoms presented themselves thousands of years ago. Attempts to diagnose Biblical figures with particular diseases are therefore not credible.

      1.1.3.3. Pro:
      Though the Bible states that natural disasters are a result of God's wrath against sinning [22], science has shown that the cause of natural disasters are set forces of nature [23] that are difficult - but not impossible - to predict.

        1.1.3.3.1. Con:
        The cited Bible passage (2 Chronicles 7:13-14 [24]) doesn't say that all natural disasters are caused by God's wrath, it only says that about the individual event that the passage is discussing.

        1.1.3.3.2. Pro:
        Earthquakes [25] are usually caused when rocks underground suddenly break along a fault, causing a release of energy that makes the ground shake, as opposed to the biblical narrative of it being a manifestation of God's anger [26] (for example in Isaiah 5:25 [27]).

        1.1.3.3.3. Pro:
        Flooding is caused [28] by excessive rain, rapid melting of snow or ice or the busting of river banks, as opposed to punishment inflicted by God for corruption [29] and wickedness [30].

        1.1.3.3.4. Con:
        God, as a primary cause of events does not preclude natural secondary causes. The Bible's recording of these events and God as primary cause is more about the author's interpretation of events and does not invalidate the historical veracity of the account.

      1.1.3.4. Con:
      Supernatural events and miracles occur in most historical documents and are even found in modern documents. The Bible is not unique in this respect.

        1.1.3.4.1. Pro:
        The writings of Cicero [31] contain many examples of miracles, oracles, inexplicable healings and so forth. But they are still regarded as valuable historical documents.

          1.1.3.4.1.1. Pro:
          For example, Cicero writes [32]: "The Spartans received many warnings given at that time of their impending defeat at Leuctra. For example, a crown of wild, prickly herbs suddenly appeared on the head of the statue erected at Delphi in honour of Lysander".

        1.1.3.4.2. Pro:
        Nat Turner uses the language of [33] miracles in his account of his rebellion. These accounts are of historical value as primary documents even if we don't think that they really were miraculous.

        1.1.3.4.3. Pro:
        Soldiers often talk about "battlefield miracles [34]." Their accounts are of historical value even whether or not the events really were miraculous.

        1.1.3.4.4. Con:
        Even if the Bible is not unique in including descriptions of miracles, the presence of implausible elements in any historical document gives us reason to doubt the veracity of other parts of the document as well.

      1.1.3.5. Con:
      Miracles, by definition, cannot be tested [35] by scientific means, and hence they cannot be proven incorrect.

        1.1.3.5.1. Con:
        In the process of the canonisation of saints, a Vatican representative is appointed as the "Devil's advocate [36]." This person is required to attempt to disprove miracles attributed to candidate saints by finding other ways to explain the events.

          1.1.3.5.1.1. Pro:
          Scientific evidence would have been provided against any miraculous works during the canonization process [37]. That evidence was either insufficient, lacking in credibility, or ineffective at disproving the miraculous works because sainthood was still given to those who were said to have practiced miracles.

        1.1.3.5.2. Con:
        An assertion which cannot be empirically tested to be confirmed or disconfirmed is by definition [38] unscientific. It does not need to be proven incorrect.

      1.1.3.6. Con:
      Even if modern science provides a natural explanation for a supposedly supernatural event, that does not necessarily show that the event did not happen. It may simply be that the mechanism for the event was not properly understood.

        1.1.3.6.1. Pro:
        It might be that David didn’t slay Goliath miraculously, but because he actually had the advantage [39] (as modern science shows). But far from invalidating the story of David and Goliath, such an account provides us with evidence that such an event could plausibly have happened.

          1.1.3.6.1.1. Pro:
          The Biblical story of David and Goliath doesn’t depend on it truly being a miracle, or David really being the underdog. It depends upon both the Israelites and the Philistines thinking that David is the underdog, but David trusting in God that he’d be victorious.

        1.1.3.6.2. Pro:
        Scientists have shown that a thin ice phenomenon [40] which sometimes happens on the Sea of Galilee would have enabled something like the miracle of Jesus waking on water. But far from debunking the story of Jesus, this explanation provides us with a reason for thinking the basic story may have actually happened.

        1.1.3.6.3. Con:
        We are justified in thinking that texts which provide implausible explanatory models are likely to be unreliable in other ways as well.

    1.1.4. Con:
    Many historical documents, which are accepted as valuable historical sources, make scientifically inaccurate claims.

      1.1.4.1. Pro:
      The Quran, considered to be one of the most authentic sources of religious faith and history [41] in Islam, has made several scientific errors.

        1.1.4.1.1. Pro:
        The Quran states that the Earth is motionless and is held still by God [42], but science has proven that it rotates around the sun [43] and its own axis.

        1.1.4.1.2. Pro:
        The Quran claims that the Earth was created before the stars [44], whereas science has proven that the Earth was forged from the stars [45].

        1.1.4.1.3. Pro:
        The Quran narrates stories that suggest the existence of a flat Earth [46], whereas science has proven that the Earth is round [47].

        1.1.4.1.4. Pro:
        The Quran claims that all animals live in groups [48], but science has shown that there are several animals [49] that live alone their entire lives.

        1.1.4.1.5. Con:
        The "scientific errors" in the Quran have been accounted for as misinterpretations of the text.

          1.1.4.1.5.1. Pro:
          The verse about [50] the 'sun setting in murky water' in the Quran, if taken literally, is scientifically inaccurate. However, critics have argued [51] that it holds poetic as opposed to literal meaning, and a mere description of what Dhul-Qarnayn saw, rather than actual scientific fact.

          1.1.4.1.5.2. Pro:
          The verse of the Quran referring to the flatness of the Earth [52] has been said to be misinterpreted, because the verse merely suggests [53] that the Earth seems flat to the naked eye.

          1.1.4.1.5.3. Pro:
          According to some Islamic scholars, the verse in the Quran regarding the Earth being motionless [54] should be interpreted metaphorically [55], and understood as a description of the phenomenon to someone on Earth.

        1.1.4.1.6. Pro:
        Numerous Quranic verses [56] state or imply that the sky is solid, but science has shown that the sky is made of gas [57].

      1.1.4.2. Con:
      If a historical source contains discrepancies, it harms the reliability of the source [58].

        1.1.4.2.1. Pro:
        The reliability of a source is directly linked to the consistency of the author's account of the truth and the strength of their claim. A reliable text displays a pattern of verifiable truth-telling that tends to make the reader trust the truth value of the rest of the text.

      1.1.4.3. Con:
      If scientific evidence is able to cast significant doubt on the accuracy or validity of many of the events in the Bible, it resembles more a piece of propaganda than a historical source.

        1.1.4.3.1. Pro:
        Propaganda often [59] exaggerates the importance of events and people, and intertwines mythology with historical facts in order to promote a particular ideology or doctrine. If proven that many of the claims in the Bible are fabricated or exaggerated in order to promote a Christian ideology, historians should consider it as a piece of propaganda rather than a reliable historical source.

        1.1.4.3.2. Con:
        Propaganda is an immensely useful historical source [60]. It reveals which ideologies were promoted, and to whom, and correspondingly tells us about the agenda of powerful people. It also provides clues about what its intended recipients thought and believed.

          1.1.4.3.2.1. Con:
          We can only get these benefits from a source once it is possible to verify that it is in fact propaganda. Without a historically reliable account to compare the piece to, there is no way of knowing whether or not it exaggerates or sensationalises claims.

      1.1.4.4. Pro:
      Much early scientific work - such as Ptolemy's [61] geocentric [62] model of the solar system - has since been found to be scientifically inaccurate. This work, however, still provides historians with valuable information about how people and scientists thought at the time.

      1.1.4.5. Pro:
      Herodotus's Histories [63] repeated false stories [64] about giant, gold-digging ants. Nonetheless, his historical accounts are still largely regarded as accurate [65].

    1.1.5. Pro:
    Scientific evidence [66] for human evolution challenges the Biblical understanding of descent from Adam [67] and Eve.

      1.1.5.1. Pro:
      The evolutionary idea that man evolved from primates is not consistent with Genesis 2:7 [68], which says that man was created "from the dust of the ground."

        1.1.5.1.1. Con:
        The word "from" in "from the dust" is actually not in the original text [69]. Thus, Genesis 2:7 could be interpreted as describing (metaphorically) what man is, not what he came from.

          1.1.5.1.1.1. Pro:
          The same form is used in Exodus 8:16 [70] without the idea of "from."

      1.1.5.2. Pro:
      Several fossils have been unearthed [71] which show the existence of different kinds of organisms of similar types as those today. This suggests that evolution is a natural process affecting all organisms across the world.

      1.1.5.3. Con:
      Many historians are willing to grant that the account of the creation at the beginning of Genesis is allegorical, while still arguing that much of the Bible is a relevant historical document.

        1.1.5.3.1. Pro:
        The work of scholars [72] like Israel Finkelstein [73] and Neil Asher Silberman [74] regards the early parts of the Bible as historical, not because the creation story and other early pre-history really happened, but because these stories really do provide us with a historically accurate anthropology of ancient Israel.

        1.1.5.3.2. Pro:
        Many people of faith regard the first twelve chapters of Genesis as myth [75], using allegorical and literary elements to get at an underlying truth. Seen in this way, the story may be historically valuable without being literally true.

    1.1.6. Con:
    Modern science's understanding of the universe is incomplete and subject to revision [76]. If there are events described in the Bible that cannot be explained by modern science, this can just as easily be taken as a reason to investigate the science further as a reason for doubting the veracity of the events.

      1.1.6.1. Con:
      Revision in science generally follows incremental changes, not grand reversals. There is good reason to think some scientific knowledge will not ever be overturned in any future update to scientific knowledge. For example, the Bible claims the Earth was created before the Sun, or that birds were created before reptiles, and there is very strong scientific evidence against such claims.

      1.1.6.2. Pro:
      Unusual, seeminlgy impossible spontaneous movements of very large rocks in Death Valley are now known to be caused [77] by ice forming under the rocks and then rapidly melting. There is no reason to think that other seemingly impossible events in the Bible have similar natural explanations.

      1.1.6.3. Pro:
      Rather than dismiss accounts in the Bible of apparently supernatural visitations as unhistorical, it is possible to regard them as having natural causes, such as sleep paralysis [78] or schizophrenia [79]. There is no reason to think that other seemingly impossible events in the Bible have similar natural explanations.

    1.1.7. Con:
    Scientific explanation and historical records rely on different explanatory models. Whereas science primarily deals with explanatory [80] mechanisms about the natural world, history is rooted in an empirical account of human events.

  1.2. Pro:
  The Bible is a more reliable source than other sources written during the same time period.

    1.2.1. Pro:
    Unlike other items or texts, the Bible has remained virtually unchanged [81] throughout the course of history.

      1.2.1.1. Pro:
      The earliest manuscripts of the Bible have been shown to be virtually the same [82] as the current ones in circulation.

        1.2.1.1.1. Pro:
        The discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls [83] in 1947 demonstrated that the scribes [84] who transmitted and copied the Hebrew Scriptures were meticulous in detail, checking and rechecking for mistakes, and counting each letter and spelling in their copies to make sure that an accurate and precise text was passed on.

          1.2.1.1.1.1. Con:
          Archaeological scholars have deemed [85] some of the Biblical artifacts on display in museums, including at least one of the more recently released of the Dead Sea Scrolls, to be forgeries.

            1.2.1.1.1.1.1. Con:
            Christopher Rollston [86], a biblical scholar, notes that research into the matter of Dead Sea Scrolls as forgeries is still ongoing [87], and there can be no definitive announcement until expert analysis is concluded.

            1.2.1.1.1.1.2. Pro:
            Scientists and archaeologists have declared the Shroud of Turin as being a medieval forgery [88].

              1.2.1.1.1.1.2.1. Con:
              As an object that was identified as a relic over 1000 years after the composition of the New Testament, the Shroud of Turin's [89] authenticity or inauthenticity has no bearing on the authenticity or inauthenticity of the Bible.

            1.2.1.1.1.1.3. Con:
            While many of the more recent fragments billed as "Dead Sea Scrolls" may [90] be forgeries, the original Dead Sea scrolls [91] are not considered to be forgeries.

          1.2.1.1.1.2. Pro:
          One of the Dead Sea scrolls - a complete copy of the book of Isaiah from the Old Testament - was believed to have been copied in about 125 B.C. It is identical [92] to the modern Hebrew Bible in more than 95% of the text.

            1.2.1.1.1.2.1. Pro:
            The remaining 5% variation between the version of Isaiah in the Dead Sea Scrolls and the version used today has been attributed [93] to differences in grammar and spelling.

          1.2.1.1.1.3. Pro:
          All of the books of the Old Testament except Esther and Nehemiah have been found in the Dead Sea Scrolls [94]. This reliably places the accuracy of the Old Testament to at least the second century BCE.

            1.2.1.1.1.3.1. Con:
            There is a substantial difference between documents composed in the second to fourth century BCE and documents composed earlier than that. Thus, the Dead Sea Scrolls are not useful in assessing the reliability of the Old Testament as a primary historical document.

              1.2.1.1.1.3.1.1. Pro:
              Given the huge time range between the Dead Sea Scrolls and the earliest parts of the Histories [95], it would take extraordinary evidence to establish the reliability of the Histories as primary historical documents.

                1.2.1.1.1.3.1.1.1. Pro:
                To be contemporaneous with the events described in the early parts of the Histories, there would need to be a reliable textual tradition spanning close to a thousand years between the beginning of the Histories and the earliest of the Dead Sea Scrolls.

                  1.2.1.1.1.3.1.1.1.1. Pro:
                  The Dead Sea Scrolls were, at the earliest, composed during the Second Temple period [96].

                  1.2.1.1.1.3.1.1.1.2. Pro:
                  The period of Judges [97] ostensibly took place in the late 2nd millennium BCE.

                1.2.1.1.1.3.1.1.2. Pro:
                To accept that the Dead Sea Scrolls are faithful copies of primary historical documents, we would have to suppose a faithful textual tradition unbroken through at least one period [98] of destruction and exile.

        1.2.1.1.2. Con:
        The earliest extant manuscripts of the Old Testament are still often hundreds of years older than the alleged dates of composition. This still leaves ample room for changes to the original text.

          1.2.1.1.2.1. Pro:
          By far the oldest known versions of Biblical texts are the Silver Scrolls [99] from the late 7th or early 6th century BCE and contain passages from Numbers. But, as part of the Exodus [100] story, the narrative of Numbers would have taken place around the twelfth century BCE.

            1.2.1.1.2.1.1. Pro:
            The Silver Scrolls only contain very small fragments of Numbers [101], the bulk of which most scholars believe was written in the 5th or 6th century BCE.

          1.2.1.1.2.2. Pro:
          Other than the small fragments in the Silver Scrolls [102], the oldest known manuscripts [103] of the Old Testament are the Dead Sea Scrolls, which were from the third or fourth centuries BCE. This places them hundreds of years after most of the supposed composition dates for the books of the Old Testament.

        1.2.1.1.3. Con:
        The largely complete manuscript versions of the Bible used to compare to modern versions date [104] from the mid 4th century CE onward. This places them after the time of Constantine, at which point the canon [105] was relatively well established. However, this tells us little about the two or three hundred years between when the New Testament took place and when the canon was established.

          1.2.1.1.3.1. Con:
          Church father quotations [106] of the Bible written before the 4th century can be compared to 4th century and later translations to prove that they weren't corrupted over time.

            1.2.1.1.3.1.1. Pro:
            Ignatius's (d. 107 [107]) quotation of 1 Timothy 1:1 in his Epistle to the Magnesians 11 [108] matches our modern version [109], proving the text has not been corrupted since his time.

            1.2.1.1.3.1.2. Pro:
            Ignatius's (d. 107 [110]) quotation of Ephesians 5:2 in his Epistle to the Ephesians 1 [111] matches our modern version [112], proving the text has not been corrupted since his time.

          1.2.1.1.3.2. Pro:
          Even the more fragmentary manuscripts used to compare the ancient and modern New Testament come from the second century CE. [113] This is still decades after the texts were supposedly written.

          1.2.1.1.3.3. Pro:
          The early church father Origen [114] complained that copyists and scribes had made numerous errors in their transcrptions of the Bible. He writes this in the 3rd century CE, at least a hundred years prior to the oldest complete manuscripts of the New Testament that we have.

        1.2.1.1.4. Pro:
        The 5600 early Greek manuscripts [115] of the New Testament agree with each other 99.5% of the time.

      1.2.1.2. Con:
      In his book 'Misquoting Jesus', Bart Ehrman [116] gives several pieces of evidence for verses that have been altered over the years.

        1.2.1.2.1. Con:
        These supposedly "altered" verses only comprise a very small fraction of the Bible's contents, meaning that overall the text remains very trustworthy.

        1.2.1.2.2. Pro:
        The oldest manuscripts of the Gospel of John do not include the incident [117] about Jesus and the woman about to be stoned by the mob. It was added later by scribes and now appears in a few manuscripts [118].

      1.2.1.3. Con:
      The earliest version [119] of the Bible has significant differences from the modern version. Most notably, St. Mark's Gospel ends 12 verses early and omits the resurrection of Jesus.

        1.2.1.3.1. Con:
        Mark 16:6 [120], which is in the original version, says "He is risen!", which does teach the resurrection of Jesus.

      1.2.1.4. Con:
      Verses from the Book of Exodus were altered [121] in American versions of the Bible in the 1970s in order to serve the pro-life movement.

        1.2.1.4.1. Pro:
        The NASB 1995 [122] says, "she gives birth prematurely" in Exodus 21:22, while the NASB 1977 [123] said, "she has a miscarriage." The former translation is apparently easier to reconcile with pro-life beliefs than the latter.

          1.2.1.4.1.1. Con:
          This change was justified because the former translation better fits the original Hebrew.

            1.2.1.4.1.1.1. Pro:
            The original Hebrew [124] of Exodus 21:22 describes what happens as "yatsa yeled" (literally "the child comes out"), which does not imply a miscarriage or the death of the baby.

        1.2.1.4.2. Con:
        Even if a specific translation was changed, there's no evidence it was specifically "in order to serve the pro-life movement."

          1.2.1.4.2.1. Pro:
          Translations change in many ways over time; it is easy to cherry-pick changes that seem relevant to political movements even if these resemblances are completely coincidental.

    1.2.2. Pro:
    The Bible has more [125] bibliographic support than any other historical document of its time.

      1.2.2.1. Pro:
      The New Testament is supported [126] by over 24,000 different extant manuscripts.

        1.2.2.1.1. Pro:
        The textual tradition of the New Testament spans over 1300 years, showing textual fidelity over a remarkably long time.

          1.2.2.1.1.1. Con:
          The accuracy of the New Testament textual tradition can be established from the great uncial codexes [127] that were likely copies of the Bibles commissioned by Constantine [128]. But this at best only establishes their reliability beginning with the establishment of Christianity as Rome’s official religion in 312. It says little about the accuracy of the textual tradition in the crucial centuries prior to that.

            1.2.2.1.1.1.1. Pro:
            The Muratorian fragment [129] is the oldest known list of the Biblical canon, but it cannot be reliably dated prior to the fourth century CE, or around 150 CE at the very earliest.

      1.2.2.2. Pro:
      The earliest copy of the New Testament was made [130] only twenty five years after the original.

        1.2.2.2.1. Con:
        Claiming that there are New Testament manuscripts dating from 25 years after their supposed composition depends on accepting that Dead Sea Scroll 7Q5 [131] is a fragment of a very small passage from Mark. But this is a minority view that depends on a number of likely unwarranted assumptions [132].

      1.2.2.3. Con:
      The Bible is not significantly more reliable than other similarly ancient documents.

        1.2.2.3.1. Pro:
        The Vedas [133], which existed during the time of the Old Testament, have widespread scriptural support [134].

        1.2.2.3.2. Pro:
        The Iliad [135], a text whose earliest copy is traced back to 900 BC, has over 643 copies and each of them is 95% accurate [136] in its text.

        1.2.2.3.3. Con:
        The reliability of other ancient documents does not speak to the unreliability of the Bible.

    1.2.3. Con:
    Many accounts of the Late Roman Republic [137], written in the century before Jesus was born, provide detailed and reliable records of contemporary life to a degree of accuracy which cannot be matched by the Bible.

      1.2.3.1. Pro:
      Diodorous Siculous [138], who wrote in the late Roman Republic, has significant works and summaries preserved in the collections of Photius [139] and the excerpts of Constantine Porphyrogenitus [140].

      1.2.3.2. Pro:
      Antiquitates rerum humanarum et divinarum [141] (antiquities of things human and divine), a collective body of work by Varro spanning forty one books, that was influential in establishing the traditions of early Rome for future generations, has been preserved in its content by Augustine.

        1.2.3.2.1. Con:
        St. Augustine of Hippo [142], the same source that we rely on for our knowledge of Varro's [143] Antiquitates rerum humanum at divanum [144], regarded the Bible as even more accurate. If we rely on his judgement in the case of Varro, then we should rely on his judgement about the Bible also.

          1.2.3.2.1.1. Pro:
          The text of Antiquitates went missing but was transmitted [145] by Augustine in his De Civitate Dei in 426 CE. It is only through the extensive quotes and reconstruction of the argument that we know the work at all.

          1.2.3.2.1.2. Pro:
          Augustine also viewed the Bible as the inerrant [146] word of God.

      1.2.3.3. Pro:
      Many of the writings, and even speeches, of Marcus Tullius Cicero [147] (who was murdered before Jesus was born), are preserved in their entirety, and are verifiably accurate.

      1.2.3.4. Pro:
      -> See 1.2.2.3.3.

    1.2.4. Con:
    Being a more reliable source than other sources written during the same period is not sufficient to establish the absolute reliability of a source.

      1.2.4.1. Pro:
      Given the overall paucity of documentation and archaeological evidence for early Iron Age cultures in the Near Middle East, it is best not to rely on any one source.

  1.3. Pro:
  Archaeological evidence supports a number of claims made within the Bible.

    1.3.1. Con:
    Camel archaeology has proven that camels were first introduced [148] in Israel around 9th century BCE, which contradicts the description in Genesis 12 [149] and 24 [150].

      1.3.1.1. Con:
      While archaeologists have shown when camel use became intensive in Israel, it is impossible to show that there were absolutely no camels in Israel prior to this.

        1.3.1.1.1. Pro:

          The Scriptures were so important [288] to the nation of Israel, that a special class of scholars called Soferim [289] developed during the Second Temple period to preserve these teachings.

            1.4.4.1.6.4.1. Pro:
            These scribes invented and solidified the process [290] for creating copies of the Torah and eventually other books in the Old Testament to ensure accurate transmission of God's word.

      1.4.4.2. Pro:
      The Bible is regarded as a collection [291] of 66 books written by more than 40 authors over more than 2,000 years. As a result, it is difficult to credit any one person or assess the credibility of what they have written.

      1.4.4.3. Con:
      An unverifiable account can still be used to make a historical argument.

        1.4.4.3.1. Pro:
        Many accounts of events given by sole witnesses are unverifiable. A historian might still use those accounts to make an argument about what happened.

          1.4.4.3.1.1. Pro:
          The British explorers George Mallory and Andrew Irving were last seen alive by a single witness [292], whose account of their movements is impossible to verify. Historians still use this account to make arguments about the fate of the two men.

    1.4.5. Pro:
    The Bible is too biased a source to qualify as historical evidence.

      1.4.5.1. Pro:
      The bulk of the historical record about Jesus in the Bible comes from the four Gospels and other New Testament writings. These are all Christian and are obviously and understandably biased [293] in what they report.

      1.4.5.2. Con:
      Many non-Christian texts, the authors of which were directly opposed to Christian teachings, have validated historical claims made in the New Testament.

        1.4.5.2.1. Pro:
        The Roman governor Pliny the Younger wrote [294] to Emperor Trajan that early Christians would “sing hymns to Christ as to a god.”

        1.4.5.2.2. Pro:
        Some scholars believe [295] that the Roman historian Suetonius references Jesus in noting that Emperor Claudius had expelled Jews from Rome who “were making constant disturbances at the instigation of Chrestus."

          1.4.5.2.2.1. Con:
          Suetonius [296] lived and wrote decades after the life of Jesus.

        1.4.5.2.3. Pro:
        According [297] to Dr. Bart Ehrman, a prominent Biblical scholar, non-Christian sources are useful for realizing that Jesus was known by historians who had no vested interest in believing so.

        1.4.5.2.4. Pro:
        -> See 1.3.4.4.4.2.

        1.4.5.2.5. Con:
        None of the ancient sources that confirm the life of Jesus are actually contemporaneous with Jesus. They thus cannot prove that Jesus was real, but only that Jesus was taken to be real by the time of the Flavian emperors. [298]

      1.4.5.3. Con:
      Historians frequently use religious polemics as sources.

        1.4.5.3.1. Con:
        Polemics are ideologically biased, whereas the Bible is alleged to be historically biased. That is, polemics are biased as to which of a set of competing beliefs or claims is true. In contrast, the Bible is alleged to be biased in the recording and interpretation of events in order to build and justify a certain narrative. Thus polemics are not an apt analogy for the bias alleged in the Bible.

        1.4.5.3.2. Pro:
        Religious polemics often provide invaluable insight into the minds of important historical figures, like Martin Luther [299] and Henry VIII of England [300].

        1.4.5.3.3. Pro:
        Many religious polemics provide useful factual information, which can be verified.

          1.4.5.3.3.1. Pro:
          Many histories of religious conflict (for example, the European Reformations of the early modern period), are heavily informed by information provided in religious polemics. This is because a huge number of significant early modern texts are religious polemics.

            1.4.5.3.3.1.1. Pro:
            Thomas More's account of the death of Richard Hunne [301] is part of a polemic attacking Protestant heretics in England. It nonetheless provides important information about the state of English law and anti-clericalism in the early sixteenth century.

    1.4.6. Con:
    There are no clear-cut criteria for what qualifies as a historical source. Historians disagree on what should qualify as a source in a wide variety of cases.

      1.4.6.1. Con:
      Anything [302] that survives from the past is a potential historical source.

      1.4.6.2. Pro:
      Innovative historians frequently find new types of source to examine. Any formal criteria for what constitutes a source would have to be almost constantly revised.

        1.4.6.2.1. Pro:
        Historians of material culture have recently argued that the means by which books were assembled and bound can provide just as much information as the language written inside [303]. They have thus invented a new category of source which has never yet been studied (201).

  1.5. Con:
  The Bible was not written to be a historical document.

    1.5.1. Con:
    The purpose of something can evolve and change over time. Thus, even if the Bible were not written as a historical document, it could still serve as one today.

      1.5.1.1. Pro:
      The original use of a computer was to make calculations, but now its purpose [304] spans to use as a multimedia and connectivity device.

      1.5.1.2. Pro:
      The original purpose [305] of the Internet was to support remote access between computers, and today, it has developed into a widespread infrastructure for information and communication.

      1.5.1.3. Pro:
      Pride and Prejudice [306] was written as a novel, likely for entertainment and education more than history. But its description of inherited wealth in England was sufficiently detailed and accurate that Thomas Pikkety [307] was able to use it in his historical economic analysis in his book Capital in the Twenty-First Century [308].

      1.5.1.4. Pro:
      The discipline of art history [309] uses artworks created for a variety of reasons in order to better understand the past.

      1.5.1.5. Con:
      While it is possible that the Bible could be used as an historical document even if that were not the purpose that it was written for, the other purposes for which it was written can complicate its usefulness as a historical document.

        1.5.1.5.1. Pro:
        It may be difficult to use the material from parts of the Bible such as Job [310], Psalms [311], Proverbs [312], Ecclesiastes [313], and Lamentations [314] since it is unclear where history ends and where literary or imaginative creation begins.

        1.5.1.5.2. Pro:
        Much of the narrative of the New Testament is presented through epistles [315]. While letters can certainly be repurposed as primary historical documents, the perspective of the narrator, consideration of audience and other stylistic considerations of the literary genre [316] may complicate their use as historical documents (pp. 137-142).

    1.5.2. Con:
    Regardless of intent/content, antique narratives provide historical value because they are written by historical people.

      1.5.2.1. Pro:
      Strong examples include indigenous American and Australian narratives. Without these, we would have to rely almost wholly on the records of settlers.

      1.5.2.2. Pro:
      The Iliad [317] was not written as a historical document, however we can glean information from the context about the time period and culture that this dramatized story describes.

        1.5.2.2.1. Con:
        The abandonment of most urban centers and the loss of the Mycenaean writing system (script A) during the Greek Dark Age [318] severely limits our ability to glean information about the history of Mycenaean culture from the Iliad.

        1.5.2.2.2. Con:
        There was around a 400 year time difference between the time period in which the Iliad was set [319] (likely around 1200 BCE) and the composition of the Iliad [320] (likely around 750 BCE). This poses serious problems to gleaning information about the time period and culture from the Iliad.

        1.5.2.2.3. Pro:
        After centuries of assuming that the Trojan War was purely mythological, the discovery [321] of a city that matches the description of Troy and that appeared to have been destroyed after a siege suggests the likelihood that the story of the Trojan War preserved a kernel of genuine historical memory.

    1.5.3. Pro:
    The Bible is not intended merely to describe events, but rather to provide religious lessons which can be discovered through Interpreting [322] the text, as the events described in the Bible have many possible meanings.

      1.5.3.1. Pro:
      The fact that the Bible contradicts historical events within [323] its own text suggests that factual correctness was not its primary aim, but rather, imparting lessons [324] relating to the history that was narrated.

      1.5.3.2. Pro:
      The Bible comes under the genre of interpretive history [325] - focusing more on the importance of history to religious beliefs for people to follow, rather than the accuracy of the history in question.

      1.5.3.3. Con:
      Plenty of historical documents require interpretation and seek to impart lessons. That a document has those features does not mean it is not primarily a historical document.

        1.5.3.3.1. Pro:
        In The History of the Peloponnesian War [326] Thucydides [327], an early historian, sought to impart a lesson about the nature of power [328] and political realism. But no one would say this diminishes its value as a historical document.

    1.5.4. Con:
    The Bible contains 66 books of different genres and many of them are historical in nature. [329]

      1.5.4.1. Pro:
      Even if only the most explicitly historical books [330] in the Old Testament and the Gospels [331] in the New Testament were historical, this would still cover centuries of history of the Levant [332] region.

      1.5.4.2. Con:
      The Bible doesn't explicitly distinguish between historical, mythological, literary, poetical and allegorical writings. This undermines the reliability of taking any particular part of it to be historical.

    1.5.5. Pro:
    The purpose [333] of the Bible is to explain God's relation to humankind, and how humans are required to live their lives. (p. 1)

      1.5.5.1. Con:
      Even if the purpose of the Bible is primarily moral and existential, this is broadly compatible with the idea that the Bible makes historical claims that can be true or false.

        1.5.5.1.1. Pro:
        Historical examples can be used to illustrate existential and moral truths.

      1.5.5.2. Con:
      Given the multiple authors and incredibly long compositional period of the Bible, it is unreasonable to assume that it has one consistent, unified purpose throughout.

        1.5.5.2.1. Pro:
        The Bible was written [334] over a period of 1400 to 1800 years by more than 40 different authors.

      1.5.5.3. Pro:
      Pope Francis [335], when meeting the Catholic Biblical Federation, proclaimed the importance of how the 'Word of God and life should embrace each other and not be without one another'.

        1.5.5.3.1. Con:
        Many faith traditions other than Catholics use the Bible. The proclamation of the Pope has no bearing on how these other faith traditions ought to interpret the Bible.

      1.5.5.4. Pro:
      Many believing Jews [336] and Christians [337] regard the story of the Pentateuch as being largely mythological or symbolic, rather than recounting a historically accurate narrative.

  1.6. Con:
  The Bible contains many contradictions and inconsistencies. These contradictions and inconsistencies make it difficult to rely on the Bible as a historical document.

    1.6.1. Pro:
    The Bible's moral teachings are contradictory and inconsistent.

      1.6.1.1. Pro:
      The Bible simultaneously approves [338] and forbids [339] lying.

        1.6.1.1.1. Con:
        I Kings 22 [340] does not approve lying. It describes how God orchestrated events through the false prophets. This passage does not work against the normative command not to bear a false witness.

      1.6.1.2. Pro:
      The Bible concurrently commands [341] and forbids [342] the act of killing.

        1.6.1.2.1. Con:
        Contextual calls to kill in a war or to protect something or someone are not contradictory to a normative moral stance that one should not murder.

      1.6.1.3. Con:
      One does not have to agree or reconcile with the moral teachings that are prescribed by a historical document in order for the document to be considered a valid source of history.

      1.6.1.4. Con:
      Many historical documents put forward contradictory and inconsistent ideologies.

        1.6.1.4.1. Pro:
        While the Founding Fathers [343] of the United States fought for freedom, many of them showed how inconsistently they applied this belief through their support of slavery [344]. Their ideological inconsistencies do not make their accounts historically unimportant.

        1.6.1.4.2. Pro:
        Most professional historians would agree that the political writings of prominent Nazis are contradictory, inconsistent and incoherent [345]. They are nonetheless useful historical documents.

      1.6.1.5. Pro:
      The Bible claims that acts of goodness should be seen [346], and acts of goodness should be kept a secret [347], at the same time.

        1.6.1.5.1. Con:
        Matthew 5:16 [348] is in reference to persecution and acts of justice and mercy. Those are acts that require a subject (public). Acts kept in secret are acts of devotion and piety (private) kept secret to avoid hypocrisy and pride.

      1.6.1.6. Pro:
      The Bible simultaneously allows [349] for and forbids [350] slavery.

        1.6.1.6.1. Con:
        Slavery is a word that has a broad range of meaning [351] from kidnapping and forcing into servitude (which is prohibited in the Old and New Testament) to voluntary and/or temporary servitude which is permitted and regulated.

      1.6.1.7. Con:
      Commandments, interdictions and other normative claims do not make historical claims. Thus, issuing contradictory commands does not disqualify a document as historical.

    1.6.2. Pro:

      1.6.2.1. Pro:

        Genesis 6:3 [354] could be about the amount of time before Noah's flood would occur, not about a limit on human lifespan.

        1.6.2.1.2. Con:

        1.6.2.1.3. Con:

        1.6.2.2.1. Con:
        It would be possible for Judas to first hang himself, and after a while for the rope to rot and snap, causing Judas's corpse to fall onto the field and spill its intestines.

          1.6.2.2.1.1. Pro:
          This makes sense because it is far more likely that the guts of a rotting corpse would fall out after "falling headlong" than the guts of a living person.

      1.6.2.3. Con:
      Even two of the most well-renowned ancient historians - Arrain [358] in Ancient Greece (p. 119) and Tacitus [359] in Ancient Rome (p.3) - had contradictions in some of their accounts.

      1.6.2.4. Pro:
      The Bible mentions that Jesus was crucified after the Passover meal while simultaneously [360] mentioning that Jesus died before the Passover meal.

        1.6.2.4.1. Con:

        1.6.2.4.2. Con:
        This apparent discrepancy is reconciled through an understanding of the Passover meal and subsequent feast described [362] in the Old Testament. John 18:28 is referring to their desire to participate in the seven days of feasting which would begin that evening. Mark is referring to the evening of the fourteenth of Nisan.

      1.6.2.5. Con:
      Even if there are contradictions in the Bible, the core of the story remains unchanged. A comparison with other ancient historical documents demonstrates that this is sufficient to establish their historical value.

        1.6.2.5.1. Con:
        Insofar as the meaning of a text depends upon the accumulation of specific detail, a text that is riven with contradiction cannot be said to have a stable, unchanged core narrative.

        1.6.2.5.2. Pro:
        There are two entirely different narratives of Hannibal crossing the Alps to attack Rome [363], and they are mutually incompatible and irreconcilable. Yet no classical historian doubts [364] the fact that Hannibal did mount such a campaign.

        1.6.2.5.3. Pro:
        While assessing ancient literary sources which have different narratives surrounding a particular event, the evidence [365] is acceptable if it is firm and plausible enough to support the same core story from each narrative.

          1.6.2.5.3.1. Pro:
          For instance, the narratives surrounding Jesus' resurrection disagree over minor facts and details, but all of them point towards the same core story. This is likely to be true for the seemingly contradictory narratives where the disagreement ultimately turns on minor differences in detail.

            1.6.2.5.3.1.1. Pro:
            The examples such as the lifespan given to people, the exact manner of Goliath's and Judas's deaths, and the precise moment in the day when Jesus's death occurred are all examples of insignificant details that don't change the global meaning of a story.

        1.7.1.8.1. Pro:
        Q [419], the hypothetical proto-Gospel on which it is thought that Matthew, Mark and Luke are based, was likely written between 65 and 95 CE. Any ancient sources after that that mention Jesus could plausibly be just basing their information on the Gospel. Such a "verification" would then be circular.

        1.7.1.8.2. Con:
        Like Jesus's life, the historical sources that validate the existence of Jesus come from the Early Roman Empire [420]. It is thus reasonable to take them to be reliable even when they are not eyewitness or entirely contemporaneous accounts.

    1.7.2. Pro:
    Other historical sources have validated the existence of major figures mentioned in the Bible.

      1.7.2.1. Con:
      The fact that the Bible talks about public historical figures who would have been well known throughout the ancient world does not verify the narrative of the Bible, since the information available to include them would have been available to the authors of these stories and could just have easily been added into the text.

      1.7.2.2. Pro:
      The chronological order of 29 kings from 10 nations who are mentioned in the Bible has been proven [421] to be correct by archaeologists.

      1.7.2.3. Con:
      The existence of certain figures does not on its own prove the historicity of the Bible. It would be necessary to show that at least some of the actions that are described in the Bible are verifiable from other sources.

      1.7.2.4. Pro:
      A researcher at Purdue University has confirmed [422] the existence of 53 people mentioned in the Hebrew Bible.

        1.7.2.4.1. Con:
        There are around 3,237 characters [423] mentioned in the Bible. The 53 people who can be verified amount to less than 2% of the overall characters, hardly a significant sample.

      1.7.2.5. Pro:
      Some public figures mentioned in the Bible - such as Augustus Caesar and Herod the Great - have [424] widespread historical acceptance.

        1.7.2.5.1. Con:
        Augustus Caesar [425] and Herod the Great [426] were incredibly important political figures who are tangential to the story of the New Testament. Using them to prove the historicity of the New Testament would be like using the existence of Queen Victoria [427] to prove the existence [428] of Sherlock Holmes.

      1.7.2.6. Pro:
      Major biblical figures such as Christ's apostles - including Thomas, Mathew, Philip and Paul - have been confirmed [429] by extra-biblical sources.

    1.7.3. Pro:
    Other historical sources have confirmed the Bible's depiction of Ancient Israel.

      1.7.3.1. Con:
      Even if some specific events in the Bible can be confirmed, to generalize from these events to the historicity of the whole narrative is to make a (fallacious) hasty generalization [430].

      1.7.3.2. Con:
      The fact that certain archaeological sites are consistent with the history presented in the Bible does not, on its own, confirm that depiction, particularly when other explanations are also possible. It only shows that the narrative of the Bible has not been ruled out.

        1.7.3.2.1. Con:
        Even if the fact of consistency between archaeological finds and the narrative of the Bible is not in itself enough to prove the historicity of a specific Biblical event, it is sufficient, absent compelling counter evidence, to regard the Biblical narrative as probably reliable.

      1.7.3.3. Con:
      -> See 1.3.3.2.

      1.7.3.4. Pro:
      -> See 1.3.3.3.1.1.

      1.7.3.5. Pro:
      -> See 1.3.4.7.2.

      1.7.3.6. Pro:
      Claims [431] made in the Bible regarding Jacob’s Well in Samaria (John 4:11), have been confirmed by Biblical archaeologists.

      1.7.3.7. Pro:
      Many events that occurred in Ancient Israel according to the Bible have been confirmed by historical findings.

        1.7.3.7.1. Con:
        Even if some events in the Bible are supported by the archaeological record, this is quite different from saying that the archaeological record supports the way in which these events fit into the totality of the Biblical narrative.

          1.7.3.7.1.1. Pro:
          In historical inquiry, context [432] is crucial.

          1.7.3.7.1.2. Con:
          The relevance of external confirmation of some Biblical elements is not just that it means those specific events happens. It is that, inductively [433], it means that we have increasingly good reason to believe that other Biblical claims are also true.

        1.7.3.7.2. Pro:
        The Hebrew Bible states that at the time of the breakup of the Israelite monarchy, an Egyptian pharaoh named Shishak [434] launched a successful military campaign against Jerusalem. Egyptian records [435] confirm the same.

        1.7.3.7.3. Pro:
        Numerous accounts [436] of wars and battles that involved ancient Israel and other powers in the region are recorded in the Bible.

        1.7.3.7.4. Pro:
        Cuneiform tablets written in Nebuchadnezzar II’s [437] time have corroborated the Hebrew Bibles’ depiction [438] of the Assyrian involvement during the time of Israel's King Pekah.

      1.7.3.8. Pro:
      The Biblical text has been a critical component [439] of anthropological and archaeological studies concerned with reconstructing certain features pertaining to the role played by ancient Israelite women in Israel's agricultural and pastoral economies.

        1.7.3.8.1. Pro:
        The discussions of Israelite marriage and family law in Deuteronomy [440], coupled with stories about women's place in family life [441] found throughout the Old Testament, give a detailed picture of the Israelite understanding of women's roles in kinship structures.

          1.7.3.8.1.1. Pro:
          The general understanding of women's role in ancient Israelite society provides valuable historical information even if certain stories turn out not to to be true, because they provide a general picture of the culture of the Israelite authors.

        1.7.3.8.2. Pro:
        By reading about the sort of work that the Bible's authors ascribe to women, it is possible to reconstruct information about the gendered division of labor [442] in ancient Israelite society.

        1.7.3.8.3. Con:
        Any reconstruction of the features of ancient Israelite culture on the basis of the Bible cannot itself then be used to confirm the accuracy of the Bible. Doing so would result in a circular argument [443].

          1.7.3.8.3.1. Con:
          While history and culture reconstructed with the aid of the Bible cannot on their own establish the accuracy of the Bible, to the extent that they develop a greater picture of life in ancient Israel that is itself supported by other evidence, they still provide us with a more coherent web of beliefs [444]. We are justified in regarding that web of beliefs as being more likely to be true than not.

            1.7.3.8.3.1.1. Pro:
            Using inferences from a hypothesis to form a stronger web of belief is the approach of abductive reasoning [445], which is generally regarded as a legitimate form of reasoning about historical explanation.

    1.7.4. Con:
    There are few, if any, independent sources that verify the claims and events discussed in the Old Testament, which makes up a significant portion of the Bible.

      1.7.4.1. Pro:
      Certain historians agree [446] that of the three parts of the Old Testament, only the events occurring from the narration of the life of King David onward, are factual.

        1.7.4.1.1. Con:
        Even if only the narration of the live of King David onward were factual, this would mean that the Bible provided a reliable account of roughly 1000 years of history. This would be more than enough to establish its usefulness as a historical document.

      1.7.4.2. Pro:
      There is no evidence [447] for the Exodus from Egypt, one of the principle stories of the Old Testament.

        1.7.4.2.1. Pro:
        There has been no evidence [448] found of a mass migration of the Jewish people found in the Sinai desert.

        1.7.4.2.2. Pro:
        There is no indication [449] that the Israelites lived in ancient Egypt, and the Sinai Peninsula shows no sign of occupation for the 2nd millennium BCE.

        1.7.4.2.3. Con:
        Some scientists claim [450] that the plagues described in the Old Testament may have actually occurred as the result of global warming and a volcanic eruption.

        1.7.4.2.4. Pro:
        There is no historical evidence, beside the Old Testament, for the existence of Moses [451].

        1.7.4.2.5. Con:
        The circumstances of the Exodus from Egypt are recounted [452] in many documents, most notably by Manetho [453], an Egyptian priest, whose writings are preserved in the work of the Jewish historian Josephus Flavius [454].

          1.7.4.2.5.1. Con:
          Manetho [455] was most likely a scribe from around the third century BCE. This places him over a thousand years after the ostensible time of Exodus.

        1.7.4.2.6. Con:
        A leather scroll dating to the time of Ramesses II [456] has been discovered [457] that describes an account of brick-making apparently by enslaved prisoners of the wars in Canaan and Syria, which sounds very much like the Biblical account of Exodus from Egypt. The scroll describes 40 taskmasters, each with a daily target of 2,000 bricks (Exodus 5:6 [458]).

          1.7.4.2.6.1. Pro:
          The Ipuwer Papyrus [459] corroborates the plagues that preceded the Exodus.

      1.7.4.3. Con:
      Some archaeologists believe they have found proof [460] of the Great Flood from the story of Noah's ark. [461]

        1.7.4.3.1. Pro:
        Historians [462] from Ancient Greece, Mesopotamia, and Egypt all report a large flood in primordial times, which may be the same as the one described in the Old Testament.

          1.7.4.3.1.1. Con:
          A common narrative motif among different groups can just as easily point to a shared cultural and mythological heritages as to an actual historical event.

            1.7.4.3.1.1.1. Pro:
            Many early Indo-European cultures [463] believed that the cosmos is a product of a Sky Father [464] and an Earth Mother [465], but we would not take this as evidence that this really happened.

        1.7.4.3.2. Con:
        Acceptance of the historicity of the flood is by no means a commonly accepted belief, and is a matter of contention among archaeologists. For instance, other archaeologists argue [466] that there is a lack of physical evidence or imprint on archaeological record anywhere of this event.

          1.7.4.3.2.1. Con:
          Many of the mentioned difficulties (e.g. having to bring in animals from all over the world) are resolved if the flood is interpreted to be local to Mesopotamia.

      1.7.4.4. Con:
      -> See 1.3.3.6.

      1.7.4.5. Pro:
      Scientific and genetic studies contradict [467] the Old Testament story of Adam and Eve.

        1.7.4.5.1. Pro:
        Biologists, through genetic analysis, have shown [468] that there was never a time when the human population numbered two (only Adam and Eve). Rather, the most conservative estimates for the minimum size of a genetic bottleneck is around two thousand.

          1.7.4.5.1.1. Con:
          Dennis Bonnette [469] argues that genetic bottleneck calculations rely upon many assumptions [470] about mutation rate, recombination rate, and other factors. Verifiable data for events in the far distant past, such as that of Adam and Eve's creation, is likely not available to scientists.

        1.7.4.5.2. Con:
        Research [471] involving experts from Rockefeller University found, through research on mitochondrial DNA, that all humans descended from a single couple.

          1.7.4.5.2.1. Con:
          Research [472] into the nature of mitochondrial DNA, and its choice as a marker for reconstructing historical patterns of population demography, suggests that the pervasive nature of direct and indirect selection on this molecule renders any conclusion derived from it ambiguous.
