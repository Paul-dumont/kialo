Discussion Name:
Should homeschooling be banned?

1. Thesis:
Homeschooling should be banned.

  1.1. Pro:
  Homeschooling harms the relationship between families and the community at large.

    1.1.1. Pro:
    The wider uptake of homeschooling harms those who continue to stay in traditional schools.

      1.1.1.1. Pro:
      Increased homeschooling in a given area or community can decrease unity and create more tension and division, leading to isolation.

        1.1.1.1.1. Pro:
        Homeschooling parents often form cliques [1] that exclude families from support networks and the rest of the homeschooling community.

          1.1.1.1.1.1. Pro:
          Homeschooling communities often inadvertently or even purposely push out [2] those who do not share the same beliefs as them.

          1.1.1.1.1.2. Con:
          Recreational sports clubs [3] are often criticized for being cliquey and exclusionary. Yet, few argue these activities should be regulated or banned.

            1.1.1.1.1.2.1. Con:
            Homeschool cliques are especially damaging as they exclude other parents—especially those who do not homeschool—from forms of local support.

        1.1.1.1.2. Con:
        Many groups, particularly discriminated ones, use homeschooling as a means of protecting their children and building closer communities.

          1.1.1.1.2.1. Pro:
          Homeschooling communities allow marginalized groups to easily teach their children their native languages, cultures, and histories [4].

          1.1.1.1.2.2. Con:
          Many marginalized communities oppose [5] homeschooling due to worries that they cause segregation, leading to the sidelining of political issues in schools and beyond.

          1.1.1.1.2.3. Pro:
          Homeschooling is often chosen by parents of marginalized communities who are worried about discrimination and bullying [6] in schools.

        1.1.1.1.3. Con:
        During the COVID-19 pandemic, many families created 'pods' [7] with other local children, creating diverse environments for homeschooling. Many of these were successful in teaching and providing a community for children.

          1.1.1.1.3.1. Con:
          Many of these pods still got assistance and support from traditional schools and teachers [8], demonstrating the need for that social structure.

          1.1.1.1.3.2. Con:
          These pods were largely considered to not be as good [9] as the experience and education of conventional schooling.

          1.1.1.1.3.3. Pro:
          These pods allowed children to continue interacting with their friends regularly and created deeper connections [10] between families.

      1.1.1.2. Con:
      Wider uptake of homeschooling could help relieve school problems [11] related to overcrowded classrooms and insufficient resources which produce social problems in many schools.

        1.1.1.2.1. Pro:
        With fewer students, teachers in conventional schools can spend more time giving individual students attention.

        1.1.1.2.2. Con:
        At best, this would impact richer schools as poorer children cannot afford to homeschool to the same extent.

          1.1.1.2.2.1. Pro:
          Private schools or public schools in rich areas already have plenty of money and do not face the same problems of overcrowding [12]. As such, the benefits of having fewer students would be minimal.

          1.1.1.2.2.2. Con:
          The benefits to traditional schools would exist across the income spectrum as many middle class and poorer families [13] have recently begun to homeschool their children.

          1.1.1.2.2.3. Pro:
          Poor families often cannot afford to homeschool. Many children who were forced to homeschool during the COVID-19 pandemic [14] were subject to substandard learning conditions.

      1.1.1.3. Con:
      In the US, taxpayers spend more than $15,000 [15] per public school pupil. American taxpayers actually save an estimated $56 billion [16] a year on homeschooled students. This can provide a smaller number of children with additional opportunities and resources.

      1.1.1.4. Pro:
      When parents withdraw their students from the school system, the system suffers from the loss of active and engaged parents.

        1.1.1.4.1. Pro:
        Widespread parent participation in parent-teacher association [17] meetings supports the creation of new curricula or activities and helps the parents remain engaged in the broader schooling community. A smaller number of engaged parents would diminish this influence.

        1.1.1.4.2. Con:
        Homeschooling parents often choose homeschooling because their involvement with their children's schools is disdained or ignored [18].

        1.1.1.4.3. Con:
        Children and their parents should not be held responsible for improving the education system.

    1.1.2. Con:
    Homeschooling families save money on expenditures which public schools would otherwise demand. This can make them more secure and less likely to get into conflicts about money.

      1.1.2.1. Con:
      In the US, the brunt of public schooling costs are paid through household taxes [19]. Regardless of where their children attend school, homeschooling parents are required to pay [20] these taxes.

        1.1.2.1.1. Pro:
        Only five US states [21] give parents who homeschool their children some form of tax break.

        1.1.2.1.2. Con:
        The parents of private school and homeschooled students could be given tax breaks [22] on their property and other associated taxes as they do not use public schools.

        1.1.2.1.3. Con:
        Education makes up a small fraction [23] of federal spending in the US. Therefore, a very small amount of one's taxes go to funding public schools.

      1.1.2.2. Pro:
      Parents often have to pay for school lunches [24] in traditional schools; they could save money at home by making more cost effective homemade lunches.

        1.1.2.2.1. Con:
        Sending kids to school with a packed lunch [25] would cost the same as having eaten at home.

        1.1.2.2.2. Con:
        Many school lunches are subsidized [26] and could even be free, depending on the family's income.

        1.1.2.2.3. Pro:
        Due to inflation, the cost of school lunches is rising [27], which puts parents in a difficult situation where they likely have to spend more to feed their children.

      1.1.2.3. Con:
      Families often have to forego a second income [28] in order to homeschool their children.

      1.1.2.4. Pro:
      Parents often have to pay to transport their children to schools, either by driving or paying [29] for school bus services.

        1.1.2.4.1. Con:
        In many countries, school bus services are paid [30] for with funding from taxes, meaning that parents do not have to spend any money out-of-pocket (p. 1 [31]).

      1.1.2.5. Pro:
      School uniforms are often prohibitively expensive [32].

        1.1.2.5.1. Con:
        Only 20% [33] of US schools require students to wear uniforms.

          1.1.2.5.1.1. Con:
          In countries like the UK [34] and El Salvador [35], school uniforms are required for all students until they are 16 years old.

        1.1.2.5.2. Con:
        Many authorities offer grants [36] to help parents pay for school uniforms.

          1.1.2.5.2.1. Pro:
          In an effort to reduce uniform costs, the UK government mandated [37] that all unnecessary branding be removed from school uniforms to allow parents to shop around or buy second hand.

          1.1.2.5.2.2. Con:
          Many school systems do not provide financial support [38] for uniform costs.

        1.1.2.5.3. Pro:
        Nearly a quarter of surveyed parents claimed that their child had worn an ill-fitting, unclean, or incorrect uniform, which has negative effects [39] on the child's social experience and educational attainment.

        1.1.2.5.4. Pro:
        The Children’s Society found the annual cost [40] of a school uniform for UK children in both primary and secondary school is more than £300.

    1.1.3. Pro:
    Homeschooling can disrupt family relationships.

      1.1.3.1. Con:
      A survey found that 95% of 7,000 homeschool graduates were glad they were homeschooled (p. 6 [41]). A further 82% plan to homeschool their children, indicating that their families were not largely disrupted.

        1.1.3.1.1. Con:
        The NHERI, which produces the Home School Researcher Journal, is closely aligned [42] with the Home School Legal Defence Association (HSLDA), the largest pro-homeschool lobby group in the US.

      1.1.3.2. Con:
      Homeschooling can bring families closer together than they would be otherwise.

        1.1.3.2.1. Pro:
        A sample [43] of homeschooling families found that they score higher on measures of family cohesion than the general population.

          1.1.3.2.1.1. Con:
          This study has been criticized for its methodological weaknesses [44].

          1.1.3.2.1.2. Pro:
          Homeschooling parents have a lower divorce rate [45] than families who use traditional schooling.

          1.1.3.2.1.3. Pro:
          One study found that homeschooling reduced stress levels in families, mainly due to a reduction in the number of external demands on them (pp. 200-201 [46]).

        1.1.3.2.2. Con:
        Homeschooled families are generally closer because the parents tend to share core beliefs and a feeling of persecution [47]. As a result, studies on their closeness say little about the effect of homeschooling itself.

          1.1.3.2.2.1. Pro:
          This extreme approach to ideological or religious beliefs allows families to silo themselves within religious or political echo chambers. This can breed dysfunction (p. 5 [48]).

          1.1.3.2.2.2. Con:
          The parents of homeschooled children typically belong to a wide variety of social spaces (p. 36 [49]).

        1.1.3.2.3. Con:
        Many homeschooled children return to traditional schools after homeschooling because of conflicts [50] between parent and child or between spouses.

        1.1.3.2.4. Pro:
        Homeschooling increases the quantity of meaningful interactions [51] between children and their parents and siblings.

          1.1.3.2.4.1. Con:
          These increased interactions mean that parents never have breaks from their children [52], which can strain their relationships.

      1.1.3.3. Pro:
      The time and resources required of a stay-at-home parent to homeschool can result in unbalanced marriages [53], particularly if one parent works away from home.

        1.1.3.3.1. Pro:
        If the teacher parent is also the one responsible for cleaning the house and cooking, they may feel as though their partner is not contributing enough (p. 1 [54]).

        1.1.3.3.2. Con:
        Imbalances in child-rearing responsibilities are frequent and can be worsened by any parentally involved activity, like competitive youth athletics [55]. Such demands do not merit a ban.

        1.1.3.3.3. Con:
        Homeschooling parents often make sure they agree beforehand on the division of labor and the material [56] they will teach, making conflict less likely.

      1.1.3.4. Pro:
      Parents angling to gain greater control over their children's lives through homeschooling are more likely to be co-dependent parents [57] who 'over-parent' their children.

        1.1.3.4.1. Con:
        Many homeschooling parents still allow their children plenty of freedom.

          1.1.3.4.1.1. Pro:
          Some homeschool students are given plenty of freedom to plan their learning and social activities [58]. Such freedoms are not afforded in the rigid school environment.

          1.1.3.4.1.2. Pro:
          Progressive homeschooling often uses the small and familiar environment of homeschooling to pursue more free, equal, and unhierarchical [59] forms of teaching.

          1.1.3.4.1.3. Con:
          Many parents use homeschooling as a way to enforce authoritarian types [60] of parenting on their children. Even if they do not intend to, the amount of control parents get through homeschooling enables severe restriction.

        1.1.3.4.2. Pro:
        Over-parenting [61] often leads to children growing up with greater levels of anxiety and lower life satisfaction.

        1.1.3.4.3. Con:
        The children of over-protective parents are often more confident and less impulsive [62].

        1.1.3.4.4. Pro:
        Infantilized children have unhealthy relationships [63] with their parents, creating boundary issues and an inability to function properly without their parents nearby.

  1.2. Pro:
  Homeschooling can make children more vulnerable to abuse and other harmful situations, preventing them from continuing their education later in life.

    1.2.1. Pro:
    Due to greater oversight, attending a traditional school can prevent and protect children from abusive or neglectful parents [64] from hiding their abuse from the authorities.

      1.2.1.1. Con:
      Many school jurisdictions are implementing laws to increase oversight on homeschooled children. The adoption of such laws will likely continue to increase in the future, creating sufficient oversight.

        1.2.1.1.1. Pro:
        School jurisdictions can implement regulations to require social workers to conduct regular check-ins [65] on homeschooled students to check up on any safety or academic issues.

        1.2.1.1.2. Con:
        A homeschooling ban would require less bureaucratic involvement than implementing a series of laws to increase oversight, making them easier to implement.

        1.2.1.1.3. Con:
        Some analyses indicate that oversight measures to protect homeschooled children may in fact increase their risk [66] of child homicide.

        1.2.1.1.4. Pro:
        After a mother killed her homeschooled son, the US state of Ohio passed “Teddy’s Law [67],” which gave social services the authority to interview parents and examine databases for parents who have previous or ongoing investigations on them.

      1.2.1.2. Con:
      Traditional schooling puts children at risk of forms of abuse and violence [68] from people other than their parents.

        1.2.1.2.1. Pro:
        Traditional schools expose students to bullying, as well as physical, psychological, and sexual violence from other students (p. 7 [69]).

          1.2.1.2.1.1. Pro:
          A classmate is more likely than a teacher to sexually abuse a fellow student, especially among young boys (p. 19 [70]).

          1.2.1.2.1.2. Pro:
          Oftentimes, aggressive and violent students are protected [71] instead of being expelled after successive outbursts.

          1.2.1.2.1.3. Con:
          Homeschoolers are still exposed to these dangers in the community—especially at church, in youth groups [72], and on sports teams [73].

        1.2.1.2.2. Con:
        Raising awareness about these forms of abuse in traditional schools is easier [74] than making homeschooled children aware of parental abuse.

          1.2.1.2.2.1. Pro:
          Traditional schools dedicate a significant amount of resources to stop bullying; children will likely be safer and better off [75] with access to such programs [76].

          1.2.1.2.2.2. Con:
          Homeschooling programs could have required safety components to help students more easily identify cases of abuse, like ensuring parents teach children how to spot abuse.

            1.2.1.2.2.2.1. Con:
            "Spot the abuse" classes are unlikely to be successful if taught by the abuser.

            1.2.1.2.2.2.2. Pro:
            As children are taught at home, they are protected from most abusers [77], assuming their parents themselves are not abusive.

          1.2.1.2.2.3. Con:
          Hundreds of parents who homeschool their children have voluntarily signed a homeschooling Bill of Rights [78] written by homeschool graduates to protect future homeschool students.

          1.2.1.2.2.4. Pro:
          Students learn about Safe Haven laws in some school districts. These laws are significant for preventing infant abandonment and infanticide at home (p. 16 [79]).

        1.2.1.2.3. Con:
        Many schools have security guards [80] that can prevent the worst and most immediate forms of violence from occurring.

          1.2.1.2.3.1. Con:
          School security guards do not receive training to work with children or face sufficient scrutiny. As a result, they have been implicated [81] in cases of assault on school grounds.

          1.2.1.2.3.2. Pro:
          Statistics have found that security guards diminish [82] the amount of violence and number of fights in schools.

        1.2.1.2.4. Pro:
        Educator sexual abuse [83], particularly in public schools, is more widespread [84] than in homeschools.

          1.2.1.2.4.1. Con:
          The most likely place for sexual abuse to occur is at home [85].

            1.2.1.2.4.1.1. Con:
            Most child abusers [86] are not the parents, making the relationship between abuse and homeschooling statistically uncorrelated.

          1.2.1.2.4.2. Con:
          Schools are improving their oversight, guidance, and internal discussions, allowing them to better protect children from abusers.

            1.2.1.2.4.2.1. Con:
            Many governments have been criticized for not devoting enough resources [87] towards stopping such abuses.

            1.2.1.2.4.2.2. Pro:
            In many schools, students and staff have been involved in campaigns [88] to raise awareness about and punish forms of sexual abuse.

          1.2.1.2.4.3. Pro:
          In the 2010–2011 school year, 48% of students surveyed in a US study experienced some form of sexual harassment at school. Of that, 38% were harassed by teachers or other adult school employees (p. 20 [89]).

          1.2.1.2.4.4. Con:
          Homeschooling is often used by abusers [90] as a means of carrying out especially severe forms of abuse.

            1.2.1.2.4.4.1. Pro:
            When abuse occurs at home, traditionally schooled children are often able to find refuge at school [91]; homeschooled children do not have that option.

            1.2.1.2.4.4.2. Con:
            More than three-quarters of reported deaths from severe child abuse were children under the age of three, who were too young to be enrolled at school (p. 4 [92]).

            1.2.1.2.4.4.3. Pro:
            More than 380 [93] cases of severe or fatal [94] child neglect have been recorded since 2000 in homeschooling families in the US.

              1.2.1.2.4.4.3.1. Con:
              A few hundred cases of neglect out of millions of homeschooled children [95] in the US pales in comparison to the hundreds of thousands [96] of children attending traditional schools who have been abused.

              1.2.1.2.4.4.3.2. Pro:
              Among children who were victims of severe abuse [97] in the US, 29% were not allowed to attend school, 12% were removed from school without explanation, and 47% were withdrawn to be homeschooled [98].

          1.2.1.2.4.5. Pro:
          One-in-ten of all K-12 students has been a victim of educator sexual misconduct [99] (p. 1 [100]).

          1.2.1.2.4.6. Pro:
          Conventional schools have been known to protect abusive staff, making enforcement especially difficult. For example, a school in South Africa [101] silenced 87 children who had allegedly been abused by a school guard to protect the adult.

        1.2.1.2.5. Pro:
        Gun violence is an epidemic [102] in American public schools; homeschooled students are generally safe from this kind of gun violence.

          1.2.1.2.5.1. Con:
          Children are far more likely [103] to become the victim of an accidental shooting at home than they are at risk of a mass shooting at school.

          1.2.1.2.5.2. Pro:
          Trauma from gun violence [104] affects not only survivors, but also those preparing to defend themselves [105] against gun violence, as public schools are now required to do.

      1.2.1.3. Pro:
      Children homeschooled in fundamentalist Christian households are at a particularly high risk [106] of being abused.

        1.2.1.3.1. Pro:
        Some parents follow fundamentalist religious beliefs that advocate for the use of “the rod” [107] to control and discipline children, a practice which has been blamed for the deaths of at least three homeschooled children.

        1.2.1.3.2. Con:
        Blame can be placed on American far-right political movements [108] that call for little oversight or regulation, opening opportunities for parents in American Christian homeschooling communities to commit abuse [109]. This is an aberration in the global context.

        1.2.1.3.3. Pro:
        Homeschooled students in fundamentalist Christian homes are more than three times [110] as likely to be victims of abuse compared to homeschooled students from non-fundamentalist families.

      1.2.1.4. Pro:
      Teachers are trained to notice and report students who seem undernourished [111] or have injuries.

        1.2.1.4.1. Con:
        Teachers are often underequipped or unable [112] to act, even if they know a child is being abused. This leads to under-reporting [113].

        1.2.1.4.2. Con:
        Teachers' schedules are often full; many feel overwhelmed [114] and have too many students to be able to notice signs of abuse in individual children.

        1.2.1.4.3. Pro:
        Abusive parents who homeschool do not have to worry about a teacher noticing or reporting [115] their children’s bruises or other physical manifestations of abuse.

          1.2.1.4.3.1. Pro:
          Teachers certainly would have noticed many of the worst cases of child abuse that have injured and killed [116] children kept at home under the guise of homeschooling.

          1.2.1.4.3.2. Con:
          Other adults, such as neighbors, coaches, and community leaders, should be capable [117] of recognizing signs of abuse in children.

      1.2.1.5. Pro:
      Social isolation [118] and educational neglect are forms of emotional abuse which can occur more often when abusive parents are permitted to homeschool their children.

        1.2.1.5.1. Con:
        Parents can just as easily socially isolate [119] their children without homeschooling them by prohibiting them from leaving the house or having friends over once they get home from school.

        1.2.1.5.2. Pro:
        Some parents deliberately keep their children from receiving a proper education, known as educational neglect [120], either to make children dependent upon their parents or to mask other forms of abuse.

        1.2.1.5.3. Pro:
        There have been a small number of cases [121] where homeschooled children were so isolated that they developed their own language or only spoke in grunts.

    1.2.2. Pro:
    Many parents who homeschool their children specifically [122] do so to avoid vaccination requirements [123].

      1.2.2.1. Con:
      Such vaccine skeptical parents would likely not immunize [124] their children even if they were forced to send them to traditional schools.

        1.2.2.1.1. Pro:
        Many parents claim religious exemptions [125] to avoid vaccinating their children.

        1.2.2.1.2. Pro:
        Parents have taken advantage of loopholes [126] in vaccine requirements for decades.

        1.2.2.1.3. Con:
        Even if a small number of parents would still avoid these vaccines, mandatory laws would make doing so difficult for most parents, meaning that a greater number of children would be vaccinated.

        1.2.2.1.4. Con:
        US states are diligently closing anti-vaccine loopholes [127] across the country, making it harder for parents to avoid vaccinating their children, regardless of where they are educated.

      1.2.2.2. Pro:
      Parents who homeschool their children often do not immunize [128] them, making them vulnerable to otherwise avoidable diseases.

      1.2.2.3. Con:
      Some US states subject homeschooled students to the same vaccination requirements [129] as conventional students.

    1.2.3. Con:
    Homeschooling would help address issues of student tardiness and truancy, which are major barriers to completing education [130] and are endemic in traditional schools.

      1.2.3.1. Pro:
      Students in traditional schools are often not supervised closely. Some of these students actively decide not to attend [131] school when required.

        1.2.3.1.1. Con:
        There are severe consequences for truancy in most school districts and parents are usually notified after just one occurrence [132].

        1.2.3.1.2. Pro:
        Students 'playing hooky [133]' is a common phenomenon in schools around the world.

      1.2.3.2. Con:
      Some parents claim homeschooled status without legally homeschooling their children as they are not required [134] to notify the school or state that they are withdrawing children from school.

        1.2.3.2.1. Con:
        Most states in the US have checks in place to avoid homeschooling being used as a cover for truancy. School districts frequently reach out to families [135] to check in and make sure students are being taken care of at home.

        1.2.3.2.2. Pro:
        Eleven US states do not require parents to notify schools [136] when they begin homeschooling their children. The vast majority of states merely require parents to complete a form annually.

          1.2.3.2.2.1. Pro:
          In one Missouri school district [137], officials sent letters saying “we assume you have decided to homeschool” to the parents of any child absent from school for more than 10 days.

        1.2.3.2.3. Pro:
        Nearly one-fifth [138] of Illinois parents withdrew their children from traditional schools to avoid compulsory attendance laws.

  1.3. Con:
  Homeschooling can improve the academic attainment of many students.

    1.3.1. Pro:
    Homeschooled students often achieve more academic success than traditionally schooled ones.

      1.3.1.1. Pro:
      Homeschooled students typically produce higher scores on assessments and exams.

        1.3.1.1.1. Con:
        Most of the positive research on the academic effects of homeschooling has been conducted by the National Home Education Research Institute (NHERI), which is generally biased and untrustworthy.

          1.3.1.1.1.1. Con:
          The NHERI is the primary source of empirical homeschooling research and is often cited in academic papers, demonstrating its trustworthiness (p. 123 [139]).

          1.3.1.1.1.2. Pro:
          -> See 1.1.3.1.1.

          1.3.1.1.1.3. Pro:
          The NHERI's studies generally do not include other variables (such as demographics, income, and parents' education) which may be responsible [140] for the higher results.

        1.3.1.1.2. Con:
        Homeschooled students take the SAT at a much lower rate [141] than their traditionally schooled peers. The small sample accounts for the higher scores and could be a proxy for lower college attendance for homeschooled students.

        1.3.1.1.3. Pro:
        Homeschooled students tend to score above average [142] on achievement tests regardless of their parents’ level of formal education or their family’s household income.

        1.3.1.1.4. Pro:
        Homeschooled students in the US [143] typically score 15-25% above their public school peers on standardized academic achievement tests.

      1.3.1.2. Pro:
      Homeschooled students in the US have a 67% college graduation rate [144] compared to 59% for conventionally schooled students.

      1.3.1.3. Pro:
      Homeschooled children in Australia achieve slightly better academic outcomes in most learning areas (p. 5 [145]).

        1.3.1.3.1. Con:
        The available data comes from a small minority of homeschooled children (10%) that elected to participate in voluntary tests (pp. 1, 3 [146]).

      1.3.1.4. Con:
      Studies demonstrating the academic benefits of homeschooling often rely on self-reported data from parents. These largely reflect a wealthier, predominantly white sample group [147].

        1.3.1.4.1. Con:
        Without self-reported data, any access to statistics regarding homeschooling would be hard to come by, given the fact that classes occur in the privacy of homes.

        1.3.1.4.2. Pro:
        Wealthier and white students are more likely to achieve academic success in North America and Europe, regardless of what type of schooling they receive and at all levels [148].

        1.3.1.4.3. Pro:
        Low response rates [149] can skew these studies towards more motivated parents who wish to report on how well their children are doing.

          1.3.1.4.3.1. Pro:
          Parents have to voluntarily sign up for tests to contribute data to these studies. Parents of children who are likely to do better are more likely [150] to put them forward for the test, creating a reporting problem.

      1.3.1.5. Con:
      Most of the elite school programs for students, such as the International Baccalaureate [151] (IB), are only available through traditional schools. These provide some of the best academic outcomes.

        1.3.1.5.1. Con:
        Advanced Placement [152] (AP) courses and exams can be taken by homeschooled students.

        1.3.1.5.2. Pro:
        The lack of access to elite programs could cost homeschooled students the opportunity to attend elite universities—less than 1% [153] of the class of 2026 at Princeton was homeschooled.

      1.3.1.6. Con:
      Traditional assessments for homeschooled children are only required in fewer than half of the states [154] in the US, which makes academic success hard to measure accurately.

        1.3.1.6.1. Pro:
        The vast majority [155] of these states either do not require assessments to be submitted, do not require minimum scores, or allow parents to bypass the requirements altogether. Measuring academic attainment in these states is not feasible.

        1.3.1.6.2. Con:
        Traditional assessments are acknowledged to be a poor form of assessing [156] the abilities of children; some even advocate for them to be abolished.

    1.3.2. Con:
    Traditional schools have more qualified personnel and better resources than homeschools.

      1.3.2.1. Pro:
      Certified teachers are likely better prepared to educate children than parents.

        1.3.2.1.1. Pro:
        Parents are frequently less able to objectively assess [157] their child's educational needs.

          1.3.2.1.1.1. Con:
          Teachers do not have a continuing relationship with students that allows them to fully understand how to work with them, while parents can observe their children from birth.

            1.3.2.1.1.1.1. Con:
            In schools, children are taught by a range of teachers, with different strengths and personalities. This is more beneficial than being taught by one person for their entire education.

            1.3.2.1.1.1.2. Con:
            Homeschooled children have parents who are especially interested and involved [158] in their education. Even if they were not homeschooled, it is likely that they would still achieve better academic outcomes.

            1.3.2.1.1.1.3. Pro:
            Inconsistency in approach and unfamiliarity with the student can be significant detriments to the quality of education.

          1.3.2.1.1.2. Pro:
          Parents may be less likely to recognize [159] or admit that their child has difficulty reading and fail to give them the additional support they need in that area.

            1.3.2.1.1.2.1. Con:
            Certified teachers may be less likely to admit that their students have difficulty reading [160] as they're focused on 'teaching for the test.' Some may choose to help struggling students cheat to pass their exams, rather than provide them with the additional support they need to improve their reading levels.

        1.3.2.1.2. Con:
        Teachers often have to teach children to improve test scores, which does not necessarily improve learning.

          1.3.2.1.2.1. Con:
          Given exam performance determines college and university entry and future job applications, they are a fundamental part of doing well in an educational sense.

          1.3.2.1.2.2. Pro:
          In many areas, teachers are assessed and evaluated [161] based on test scores. When teachers' wages and career prospects are based on pupils' test scores, they have an incentive to focus on improving those rather than taking a more holistic educational approach.

        1.3.2.1.3. Con:
        Many states already have powers to regulate or inspect the quality and adequacy of the homeschooled child's education.

          1.3.2.1.3.1. Pro:
          States can require at least one homeschooling parent to have a teaching certificate. Many such curricula [162] already exist.

          1.3.2.1.3.2. Pro:
          In the UK, the relevant Local Authority can issue a notice [163] to parents asking them to demonstrate that their child is receiving a sufficient education. If they cannot do so, the Authority can compel them to attend school.

          1.3.2.1.3.3. Pro:
          In New South Wales, parents must prove that the child's education will accord with state standards, and that the educational system and timetable the parent wishes to use is suitable for the child (p. 10 [164]).

          1.3.2.1.3.4. Con:
          A regulatory and inspection process takes time to complete. If it finds that a child is in a substandard educational environment, it cannot undo the damage done in the time that the child was in that environment.

          1.3.2.1.3.5. Con:
          Among European countries that allow homeschooling, regulations are highly localized [165], and therefore inconsistent and not transparent.

        1.3.2.1.4. Pro:
        Parents usually do not have the pedagogical or academic knowledge to make informed decisions about their child's education.

          1.3.2.1.4.1. Con:
          Teacher quality varies widely, and is highly associated with the wealth [166] of the school's catchment area.

          1.3.2.1.4.2. Con:
          Parents who teach from a young age will have over a decade worth of experience by the time their child is finished, more if they have multiple children.

            1.3.2.1.4.2.1. Pro:
            Children's programming on broadcast television [167] can also assist parents with homeschooling their children.

            1.3.2.1.4.2.2. Con:
            This is not useful for their eldest children and implies that the first few years of homeschooling will be subpar.

          1.3.2.1.4.3. Pro:
          Three-quarters of US parents who homeschool their children did not take any preparatory course for home instruction (p. 14 [168]).

            1.3.2.1.4.3.1. Con:
            Parents who use curated lessons and curricula have been able to achieve noticable academic success [169].

          1.3.2.1.4.4. Pro:
          Teachers have a better psychological understanding [170] of how to teach children and help them develop.

          1.3.2.1.4.5. Pro:
          Professional teachers must be certified and evaluated and are more likely to use effective pedagogical techniques (p. 105 [171]).

            1.3.2.1.4.5.1. Con:
            There are over 1,500 [172] different teacher qualifications in the US, of varying quality. Consequently, the consistent application of "best practices" in public schools does not often occur.

              1.3.2.1.4.5.1.1. Con:
              That may be a failure from the US system that could be reformed. The countries with the consistently best international results, like Singapore or Finland, have only one academically rigorous teacher education program [173].

            1.3.2.1.4.5.2. Pro:
            Most teachers have a university degree [174] and have been trained in effective pedagogical techniques.

            1.3.2.1.4.5.3. Pro:
            European requirements [175] to become a pre-primary or primary teacher require a Bachelor’s degree or equivalent, which takes three to four years of study and certain countries even require a Master’s level.

      1.3.2.2. Con:
      Parents often lack the option to send their children to better or well-funded schools.

        1.3.2.2.1. Con:
        Consistently enforcing educational standards for homeschooled children can be difficult as the standards are quite convoluted (pp. 17-18 [176]).

          1.3.2.2.1.1. Pro:
          Hiring professionals to ensure that parents are adhering to proper educational standards is costly.

          1.3.2.2.1.2. Con:
          There are a variety of ways for states to enforce standards on schools, like through inspections and standardized tests [177].

        1.3.2.2.2. Pro:
        One in eight [178] primary school pupils are taught in overcrowded classes compromising the standard of education that schools provide to children.

        1.3.2.2.3. Pro:
        Many traditional schools lack basic resources. Parents usually lack the ability to change this.

          1.3.2.2.3.1. Pro:
          Nearly half [179] of US public school funding comes from local property taxes, which means schools in poorer areas are deprived of funding and lack resources.

          1.3.2.2.3.2. Pro:
          Some districts have poor quality schools [180], which forces parents to homeschool their children.

          1.3.2.2.3.3. Con:
          Nearly all of the resources for public schools are provided by government funding [181]; in a homeschooling environment, parents have to provide those resources out-of-pocket.

          1.3.2.2.3.4. Con:
          Children in poorer areas would be deprived either way; the stresses of living in an impoverished home environment make the household a poor learning environment [182].

          1.3.2.2.3.5. Con:
          Parents should, instead, join the growing calls [183] to increase funding to conventional schools.

          1.3.2.2.3.6. Pro:
          Local support groups [184] give parents who homeschool access to educational assistance [185], ideas, and resources to improve their teaching and support their children better.

            1.3.2.2.3.6.1. Con:
            Just 3.4% [186] of children in the US are homeschooled. It is unlikely that such a small number could sustain effective support groups across the country.

              1.3.2.2.3.6.1.1. Con:
              Homeschooling in the United States is growing [187] quickly. This will, in the long-term, make it easier for parents to support each other effectively.

              1.3.2.2.3.6.1.2. Con:
              Social media [188] allows parents to reach a wider network of support groups.

              1.3.2.2.3.6.1.3. Con:
              Even small local groups [189] with the same objective can be effective at supporting one another.

              1.3.2.2.3.6.1.4. Pro:
              Research suggests that movements need to at least partially convince one-quarter of the population [190] to create significant and sustained social change. Without that, homeschooling communities would likely not receive much support from non-homeschooling families.

              1.3.2.2.3.6.1.5. Pro:
              For many homeschooling families, this means that there will not be other similar families nearby, making it harder to find a group to coordinate with.

            1.3.2.2.3.6.2. Pro:
            Support groups often organize special seminars and classes [191] pertaining to subjects that are difficult to teach at home.

        1.3.2.2.4. Con:
        Some parents move closer to the school they want to send their children to in order to fall into its geographic or catchment [192] area.

        1.3.2.2.5. Con:
        Due to the baptism barrier [193] in Ireland, many people who were not raised Catholic decided to baptize their children in order to get them into Catholic schools with superior reputations.

      1.3.2.3. Pro:
      The school system offers students greater resources and educational support than homeschooling.

        1.3.2.3.1. Pro:
        Traditional schools often have computer labs, science facilities, woodshop areas, and libraries. It would be difficult and expensive for homeschooling families to access such a wide variety [194] of resources.

          1.3.2.3.1.1. Con:
          Most homes, especially those used for homeschooling, have at least basic equivalent facilities [195] for adequate learning environments.

          1.3.2.3.1.2. Pro:
          Science facilities at traditional schools are often capable of inspiring students to pursue the sciences [196] as a profession.

          1.3.2.3.1.3. Con:
          Performing science projects at home has been credited with a boost in academic attainment [197].

          1.3.2.3.1.4. Pro:
          Science facilities give students a greater understanding of scientific experiments than books and household education could provide.

        1.3.2.3.2. Pro:
        Traditional schools often provide tutoring at school [198] to help struggling students.

        1.3.2.3.3. Con:
        Student education and development is hindered by seasonal breaks [199] and limited classroom time. Parents could theoretically homeschool their children for longer periods of time if they wish.

          1.3.2.3.3.1. Con:
          A team of specialists collaborate to design the regulations that establish teaching and vacation times in accordance for different students. The fact that parents can arbitrarily increase those times may be actually harmful.

            1.3.2.3.3.1.1. Con:
            The current education system was established to create docile and obedient [200] factory workers and was not designed by pedagogues or psychologists.

            1.3.2.3.3.1.2. Con:
            Long summer vacations are a relic of agricultural societies [201] that needed children to work during summer months. It remains only due to inertia.

            1.3.2.3.3.1.3. Pro:
            Education guidelines and rules—including vacation periods, teaching times, curricula, and evaluations—in the UK are designed by the Department of Education [202] through several non-ministerial departments as well as advisory bodies.

          1.3.2.3.3.2. Pro:
          Summer learning loss [203], where children lose a significant amount of educational progress over the summer vacation, could be avoided in a homeschooling environment.

    1.3.3. Pro:
    Mixed-age learning, common in homeschooling environments, is superior to classes of students organized by age (p. 1 [204]).

      1.3.3.1. Pro:
      Children in mixed-age classrooms are more likely to cooperate [205]—instead of competing—with one another.

      1.3.3.2. Pro:
      Children with different abilities in same-grade classrooms may be labeled as "below grade level [206]."

      1.3.3.3. Con:
      Children who attend public school still socialize with their siblings, an important source of growth and social stimulation (p. 54 [207]).

        1.3.3.3.1. Con:
        Some older children in mixed-age classrooms take on leadership roles [208], which help boost their confidence and create a sense of belonging among younger children. The same may not happen in other mixed-age contexts.

    1.3.4. Con:
    Parents who homeschool are more likely to give their children a biased education.

      1.3.4.1. Pro:
      Some religious groups advocate homeschooling as a way of ensuring children adopt the same religious views.

        1.3.4.1.1. Pro:
        More than 60% [209] of parents who homeschool in the US quoted religion as being an important reason and nearly one-fifth said it was the single most important reason (p. 12 [210]).

          1.3.4.1.1.1. Con:
          Parents in the US often quote a variety of other reasons [211] for homeschooling their children; two-thirds [212] of homeschooling parents do so to provide moral instruction, and many have concerns regarding the quality of academic instruction and school environments.

            1.3.4.1.1.1.1. Con:
            Voluntary surveys may not adequately represent the importance of religious beliefs; religion can still be a secondary or tertiary factor for parents.

          1.3.4.1.1.2. Pro:
          Christian fathers often view homeschooling as a system of "total socialization [213]" aimed at rejecting the influence of other types of socialization and achieving a “moral conversion [214].”

        1.3.4.1.2. Con:
        Homeschooled students are no more religious [215] than demographically similar young people who attended traditional schools.

          1.3.4.1.2.1. Pro:
          Some studies found that even those homeschooled students who continue to hold religious beliefs are more open and tolerant [216] than their traditionally educated peers.

          1.3.4.1.2.2. Pro:
          Many adult Christians who were homeschooled reported that they no longer shared [217] their family's religious beliefs, with a substantial minority viewing the religion unfavorably.

            1.3.4.1.2.2.1. Con:
            Four out of five children in this study still identified as Christian. Compared to the vast rates [218] of young people in the general population leaving the church, this statistic is very high.

          1.3.4.1.2.3. Con:
          Even though many homeschool alumni may reject their parents' faith, a majority of those surveyed [219] still viewed their family's religion favorably.

        1.3.4.1.3. Con:
        Non-religious [220] homeschooling is growing in popularity in both the US and Europe [221].

          1.3.4.1.3.1. Con:
          Non-religious homeschooling continues to be a minor portion [222] of homeschooling overall, and an even more minuscule part of all forms of schooling.

          1.3.4.1.3.2. Pro:
          In Australia, it is estimated that only about 6% [223] of parents who homeschool do so for religious reasons.

          1.3.4.1.3.3. Pro:
          A growing number of progressive families view homeschooling as a better [224] and freer way to educate their children.

        1.3.4.1.4. Con:
        Some private and charter schools require specific religious affiliations [225], offering similar degrees of religious and ideological bias as homeschooling.

          1.3.4.1.4.1. Pro:
          Some religious schools, along with homeschool environments, refuse to teach the theory of evolution, opting instead for religious-based creationist science and history [226].

            1.3.4.1.4.1.1. Pro:
            In a 2014 survey of homeschool alumni, approximately 80% of respondents reported [227] they were taught Creationism. Only 2% of the sample were taught evolution.

          1.3.4.1.4.2. Con:
          In England, schools with religious charters are required to reserve at least 50% of their enrollment spots for anyone regardless of religious affiliation (p. 6 [228]).

        1.3.4.1.5. Pro:
        Nearly 60% of respondents [229] to a 2014 survey of homeschooled alumni agreed that their family had been fundamentalist.

          1.3.4.1.5.1. Pro:
          Some Mennonites [230] homeschool to better inculcate their beliefs in their children..

            1.3.4.1.5.1.1. Con:
            Most Mennonite schools [231] are private and very expensive. Some parents who do not have the budget for these schools choose homeschooling over public schooling.

          1.3.4.1.5.2. Con:
          This is mostly true only in the United States; in other countries [232], religious homeschooling comprises a smaller proportion of the total homeschooled population and, even then, the majority is not fundamentalist.

          1.3.4.1.5.3. Pro:
          The Quiverfull movement—a group of conservative Christian couples associated with the evangelical right in the USA—promotes homeschooling [233] as a way of ensuring children adopt the fundamentalist values of their parents.

      1.3.4.2. Con:
      Traditional schools can also give students a biased education.

        1.3.4.2.1. Con:
        Traditional school education can make it less likely that children maintain the beliefs and worldviews of their parents into adulthood.

          1.3.4.2.1.1. Pro:
          Children being shut off from alternative viewpoints at an early age could prevent them from ever truly understanding or appreciating such opinions.

          1.3.4.2.1.2. Con:
          Access to the internet, television, and interactions with other people make it fairly unlikely that homeschooled children would not be able to explore and engage with alternative beliefs.

          1.3.4.2.1.3. Pro:
          Teachers have been trained [234] to accurately explain a diverse set of beliefs to students.

          1.3.4.2.1.4. Con:
          Children often adopt biased views [235] from their parents even if they go to school.

        1.3.4.2.2. Pro:
        Both sides of the political aisle accuse their local school districts of being politically biased in the opposite direction.

          1.3.4.2.2.1. Pro:
          Conservatives often criticize contemporary education surrounding history and literature classes which discuss racism and gender issues [236].

          1.3.4.2.2.2. Con:
          Anecdotal evidence of a few biased teachers, textbooks, and schools is not evidence of an overarching problem.

          1.3.4.2.2.3. Pro:
          There is a conservative political bias [237] in how science is being taught in many US schools, particularly with regards to the subject of global warming.

          1.3.4.2.2.4. Pro:
          A teacher gave children at a US high school a biased summer reading assignment [238]. The majority of the books on the assignment list were heavily slanted towards conservative political viewpoints.

        1.3.4.2.3. Con:
        Teachers are more neutral than parents because they have less of a personal attachment or agenda.

          1.3.4.2.3.1. Pro:
          Schools generally have a legal obligation [239] to teach a politically neutral curriculum.

          1.3.4.2.3.2. Con:
          Governments can and often do force their own agendas on teachers. Some US states prohibit discussing gender, sexuality, critical race theory, and other so-called controversial subjects [240].

            1.3.4.2.3.2.1. Con:
            Biased parents enable politicians [241] with the same agenda, forcing teachers to bend to their will.

        1.3.4.2.4. Pro:
        Teachers with racist [242], sexist, and hateful views can harm students and their education while normalizing unacceptable ideas.

      1.3.4.3. Con:
      Homeschooling may make it easier for children to fight against any misinformation or bias that they come across.

        1.3.4.3.1. Con:
        Without the exchange of information between pupils, many homeschooled students might be deprived of differing viewpoints, making them more susceptible to their parents' biases and opinions.

        1.3.4.3.2. Pro:
        There are a variety of resources [243] for homeschooling families to teach their children about misinformation.

        1.3.4.3.3. Pro:
        Given that many homeschooling parents are critical of traditional schools [244], children invariably gain access to multiple views, including those which are rarely discussed in classrooms.

        1.3.4.3.4. Con:
        The school system promotes curricula and programs designed to prevent racism and discrimination, including programs targeting Islamophobia [245] and anti-Semitism [246].

      1.3.4.4. Pro:
      Extremist values [247] are often taught in some homeschools communities; such ventures should be strongly discouraged.

        1.3.4.4.1. Con:
        Such extremist schools often disguise themselves as language schools and operate after school or on weekends [248]. A ban on homeschooling would do little to stop this.

      1.3.4.5. Pro:
      Eighty percent of Christian homeschooling families have negative attitudes towards LGBTQ+ rights (p. 5 [249]). They are therefore unlikely to provide a supportive educational environment, especially if their child identifies as a member of the LGBTQ+ community.

    1.3.5. Pro:
    Homeschooling education can be adapted to cater to a child's specific needs. Traditional schools must cater to the needs of the entire class.

      1.3.5.1. Pro:
      Homeschooling can be beneficial for children with mental or physical disabilities.

        1.3.5.1.1. Con:
        In one study, more than half of homeschooled children with autism did not meet the legal requirement of 4.5 hours of daily instruction and did not study content in all required educational areas (p. 27 [250]).

        1.3.5.1.2. Pro:
        A great number of [251] schools claim to lack the facilities and resources [252] necessary to support every child’s needs. Parents who homeschool their children likely have access to better resources [253].

      1.3.5.2. Pro:
      Homeschooled students can pursue different subjects, qualifications, and curricula [254], which are not offered at their local public schools.

        1.3.5.2.1. Con:
        Parents and children can still pursue these qualifications and skills through extra-curricular activities [255], which many students already do.

        1.3.5.2.2. Pro:
        Homeschooled students—especially those in high school—can design their own course map [256] and take classes in the order of their choosing to best suit their needs.

        1.3.5.2.3. Pro:
        With subjects like music [257] and art being taken out of school curricula due to budget cuts (p. 3 [258]), homeschooling can be a way for children to receive a complete education in a variety of subjects.

      1.3.5.3. Con:
      Teachers are willing to provide a personal touch and often help and support their students who need an extra boost or additional assistance [259].

        1.3.5.3.1. Con:
        Homeschooling promotes an encouraging environment for the child to learn through familiarity [260] with the space. This makes children more comfortable in class.

        1.3.5.3.2. Con:
        Between the burnout faced by teachers and the general lack of teachers available, a "personal touch" is probably hard to come by [261] these days.

        1.3.5.3.3. Pro:
        Teachers often work with students in their lunch times or after school [262].

        1.3.5.3.4. Pro:
        Teachers often stay in touch with parents, like during parent-teacher nights [263], to provide additional support to meet the needs of their children.

      1.3.5.4. Con:
      Schools often have a variety of strategies to help students learning at different speeds, making this only a minor problem.

        1.3.5.4.1. Pro:
        Many traditional schools use streaming [264] to organize their students according to their relative abilities in specific subjects.

          1.3.5.4.1.1. Con:
          Large class sizes [265] still prevent meaningful streaming and differentiation.

          1.3.5.4.1.2. Pro:
          Ability grouping [266] has been shown to be an effective method of teaching accelerated students.

          1.3.5.4.1.3. Con:
          Most public schools do not serve accelerated students. Therefore, many highly skilled students have never been placed into ability grouped classes (p. 507 [267]).

        1.3.5.4.2. Con:
        Child geniuses can more easily adapt to homeschool environments, where they can take classes that match their intellectual prowess [268] rather than their age.

        1.3.5.4.3. Pro:
        Teachers can assign their classes activities [269] that allow some students to take on more responsibilities.

      1.3.5.5. Pro:
      Boys are often harmed by traditional school environments and could benefit from the additional attention in homeschool set-ups.

        1.3.5.5.1. Pro:
        Boys in public schools are 2.5 times more likely [270] than girls to be diagnosed with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Giving children more attention increases the length [271] of their attention span.

          1.3.5.5.1.1. Con:
          These findings have been disputed by the fact that ADHD is often underdiagnosed in girls [272].

        1.3.5.5.2. Con:
        Boys are more likely to experience social isolation [273], suggesting that homeschooling could be detrimental to their mental health.

        1.3.5.5.3. Pro:
        Girls have more serotonin and oxytocin [274], allowing them to sit and focus in the classroom for longer periods of time than boys.

  1.4. Con:
  A ban on homeschooling would be difficult or even impossible to enforce.

    1.4.1. Pro:
    Parents committed to homeschooling would likely be able to find and exploit workarounds, loopholes, or exceptions to the law. To avoid such scenarios, enforcement would require extreme tactics.

      1.4.1.1. Con:
      Families would be unlikely to be able to hide their homeschooling for very long—most families would eventually get caught or avoid trying in the first place.

      1.4.1.2. Pro:
      A mother and her partner could reasonably hide their child if they decline to register a birth at home.

        1.4.1.2.1. Con:
        If the parents decide not to register their baby, as the child grows up, they will encounter a number of problems [275].

        1.4.1.2.2. Pro:
        Around the world, more than one-quarter [276] of children under the age of five are not registered by any state, demonstrating the feasibility of such a plan.

      1.4.1.3. Con:
      Most parents are unlikely to be willing to raise their children outside of the law.

        1.4.1.3.1. Pro:
        Most families would not be able to afford the penalties levied against homeschooling, and therefore would be reticent to break the law.

        1.4.1.3.2. Con:
        Homeschooling communities would likely band together to make it difficult for the state to enforce a homeschooling ban. This could increase tensions between the state and the people.

          1.4.1.3.2.1. Pro:
          Upwards of 6,000 children [277] are already being taught in illegal schools in the UK. Similar tactics could be used and expanded if homeschooling is banned.

          1.4.1.3.2.2. Con:
          Very few communities would be able to band together as it requires a large number of homeschooling families in a small area with few non-conforming families.

            1.4.1.3.2.2.1. Con:
            After-school homeschooling environments are common [278] around the world and could be an easy way for parents to hide supplemental learning from authorities.

        1.4.1.3.3. Pro:
        The Swedish government threatened and fined a family who refused to stop homeschooling [279] their children. Most parents would not endure the stress and public shame and give up.

        1.4.1.3.4. Con:
        There are many examples of homeschooling parents persevering [280] in the face of enormous legal consequences.

      1.4.1.4. Pro:
      States may have to take children out of the custody of their parents to enforce such a ban.

        1.4.1.4.1. Con:
        If parents are inappropriately and illegally homeschooling their children, taking them out of their parents' custody may be in the children's best interests.

        1.4.1.4.2. Pro:
        A family [281] in Germany temporarily lost custody of their children after illegally homeschooling them.

        1.4.1.4.3. Pro:
        The Swedish government kept seven year-old Dominic Johansson [282] in their custody for more than half a decade because his parents had been homeschooling him.

        1.4.1.4.4. Con:
        Less extreme methods of enforcement, such as fines, can be used.

    1.4.2. Con:
    A homeschooling ban could be enforced in the same ways that other child exploitation laws are enforced.

      1.4.2.1. Pro:
      Most school districts are already aware of the children living in their jurisdiction. Therefore, it will likely be easy to identify [283] families shirking the ban.

      1.4.2.2. Con:
      Some families may prefer to move to other countries [284] rather than accept bans on homeschooling.

        1.4.2.2.1. Con:
        Moving to another country is not a realistic possibility [285] for anyone but a very slim number of privileged families.

      1.4.2.3. Pro:
      Parents would likely be deterred from homeschooling if they worry someone could tell the authorities about their illegal actions.

        1.4.2.3.1. Pro:
        Parents may worry their neighbors will find out they are homeschooling and alert the police.

        1.4.2.3.2. Pro:
        Parents may worry that, if their child turns out to oppose their homeschooling, they could tell authorities about them and any other homeschooling families they know.

        1.4.2.3.3. Con:
        This worry would likely incentivize homeschooling parents to become more controlling and manipulative, so as to prevent their children from telling the police.

    1.4.3. Con:
    Bans on many undesirable behaviors can be difficult to enforce. Despite that, people still believe those behaviors ought to be banned.

      1.4.3.1. Pro:
      Enforcing petty crime bans [286] can be difficult, but governments still make it illegal and there is no popular support to do otherwise.

      1.4.3.2. Con:
      There are many calls to decriminalize petty crimes as their enforcement is often predicated on classist and racist lines and make communities feel less safe [287]. The same logic could apply to the enforcement of homeschooling bans.

      1.4.3.3. Pro:
      Government legislation can act as a moral signal [288], indicating which practices are deemed acceptable.

    1.4.4. Pro:
    Aggressive regulation would be a better way to deal with any harms of homeschooling, rather than banning it outright.

      1.4.4.1. Pro:
      The more than one million [289] students currently being homeschooled in the US would experience significant disruption to their education if they were forced to integrate into traditional schools.

        1.4.4.1.1. Con:
        Governments banning homeschooling could find ways to make the transition easier.

          1.4.4.1.1.1. Pro:
          Special classes could be established to ease the transition from homeschooling to traditional schooling.

          1.4.4.1.1.2. Con:
          Methods to make the transition easier would involve more spending and more wasted resources. From an efficiency perspective, a clean break and singular transition would be more effective.

          1.4.4.1.1.3. Pro:
          A ban can be implemented such that no new homeschooling is permitted, but children currently being homeschooled can continue until they finish their education.

        1.4.4.1.2. Pro:
        In many areas, traditional schools may not have the facilities to take in such a large number of students at once.

        1.4.4.1.3. Pro:
        Homeschooled children may struggle in conventional schools.

          1.4.4.1.3.1. Con:
          Students often face similar disruptions when changing schools or moving to a new area. It likely will not be detrimental for most.

          1.4.4.1.3.2. Pro:
          Homeschooled students may find themselves more advanced in some classes and behind in others [290].

      1.4.4.2. Pro:
      Some jurisdictions require homeschooled students to take standardized exams [291].

      1.4.4.3. Con:
      Regulating homeschooling may be difficult since homeschooling associations are often incredibly powerful and hostile to increased regulation. Regulation is likely to remain minimal, making a ban more effective.

        1.4.4.3.1. Con:
        Pro-homeschooling parents protested outside the UK parliament in 2009 [292]. That response would likely be more passionate and harder to ignore if governments sought bans.

          1.4.4.3.1.1. Pro:
          Pro-homeschooling interest groups often have powerful connections and large amounts of money [293]. They would able to stage powerful protests if governments considered banning homeschooling.

        1.4.4.3.2. Con:
        Most families [294] still prefer traditional schooling over homeschooling, making any such resistannce a relatively small affair.

        1.4.4.3.3. Pro:
        Lobby power is why current regulations are so lax. Many US states do not require homeschooling [295] parents to register their children or even comply with educational requirements including keeping records, minimum instruction time, following state-approved curricula, or administering assessments.

        1.4.4.3.4. Pro:
        In the US, the Home School Legal Defense Association [296] (HSLDA), the most effective educational lobby group in Washington, advocates for practically no regulations on homeschooling.

          1.4.4.3.4.1. Pro:
          Due to the Homeschool Legal Defense Association (HSLDA), every state which had prohibited homeschooling reversed course and legalized the practice (p. 548 [297]).

  1.5. Con:
  A ban on homeschooling would infringe upon the basic rights and freedoms [298] of parents.

    1.5.1. Con:
    Homeschooling barely existed [299] in the US until the 1980s, suggesting that it is far from a necessity or basic human right.

      1.5.1.1. Con:
      Human rights are not immutable features of life; they can and do change over time [300] and across cultures [301]. It follows that access to homeschooling may not have been a right before, but now it is.

        1.5.1.1.1. Pro:
        Even though it did not exist before the 1990s, many people agree that access to the internet [302] is a human right now. Homeschooling could be thought of in a similar way.

        1.5.1.1.2. Pro:
        New technology [303] has made widespread homeschooling possible; therefore, people should have a right to use it.

        1.5.1.1.3. Con:
        Insofar as children can—and most do—obtain good and fulfilling educations without homeschooling, it can hardly be considered a necessary right.

      1.5.1.2. Pro:
      Historically, education itself used to be reserved solely for the elite [304]; the vast majority of the population was never afforded the opportunity [305] to become educated.

        1.5.1.2.1. Con:
        The right to education [306] is itself evidence that, just because some right has not historically been provided to people, it does not follow that it is not a fundamental human right.

      1.5.1.3. Con:
      Homeschooling has a long and significant history [307] in the US even before the 1980s.

    1.5.2. Con:
    Parents should not be the sole teacher of their children. Both society and the government [308] have a right to influence a child's development.

      1.5.2.1. Pro:
      Having more than one actor tasked with teaching children ensures that no one actor wields too much control over the information taught to children.

      1.5.2.2. Con:
      Parents have a democratic right to help determine what is taught [309] in schools. When they feel that a school no longer represents them, they have the right to deny the government the ability to influence their children.

        1.5.2.2.1. Pro:
        In such cases, parents may decide that the state is becoming too strong of an influence on their children. In such cases, homeschooling can be a more democratic option for families.

        1.5.2.2.2. Con:
        Parents still have the ability to democratically influence what happens in traditional schools through elections, participation in parent-teacher associations and grassroots movements.

          1.5.2.2.2.1. Pro:
          Democratic rights imply democratic responsibilities [310], including the recognition of when to concede a lost debate. It is undemocratic to respond to such a defeat by attempting to usurp the democratic system, for example, by homeschooling.

          1.5.2.2.2.2. Con:
          Increased parental influence over public education policy has been thwarted [311] or stalled [312] in several US states.

          1.5.2.2.2.3. Con:
          School boards and parent-teacher associations are largely dysfunctional [313] and pander to voters for power. These weakened organizations leave parents with little power to make significant changes to traditional schools.

        1.5.2.2.3. Con:
        Parents demanding the right to directly influence school curricula has a negative impact [314] on students who have benefited from inclusion efforts, as their voices and opinions are drowned out by the vocal majority.

      1.5.2.3. Con:
      No one cares more, or is more accountable [315] for a child's education, than their own parents. Generally parents have more interest in their child's welfare than a state bureaucracy.

      1.5.2.4. Pro:
      The state has an obligation to intervene [316] and limit parental power in cases where the safety and well-being of vulnerable children may be at stake. Such intervention aims to both protect children and prepare them to contribute to society (p. 1 [317]).

        1.5.2.4.1. Pro:
        The government—and society more generally—should play an independent role [318] in raising and developing children so as to ensure that they are able and willing to contribute to their society when they are older.

        1.5.2.4.2. Con:
        Children are, in many cases, legally not seen as mature or rational [319] enough to make independent decisions. Therefore, parents are given the right to make decisions on their behalf.

          1.5.2.4.2.1. Con:
          Children still have other rights that protect them from abusive parents.

            1.5.2.4.2.1.1. Pro:
            Most states ban parents from forcing their children into several behaviors. For example, despite it being common in the past, child labor [320] is illegal in most of the world.

            1.5.2.4.2.1.2. Con:
            The innocence of children must be protected at all costs, allowing them to grow and learn throughout their childhood. As such, more protections are better.

          1.5.2.4.2.2. Pro:
          This is why parents have the right to choose which traditional school they send their children to—even if it means sending them to bad, ideological, or religious schools.

        1.5.2.4.3. Pro:
        States frequently intervene in the lives of children, determining what they learn, read [321], and which activities are required.

        1.5.2.4.4. Con:
        One's vulnerability does not give the government the right to be more paternalistic. Such a rule does not exist for other vulnerable populations.

          1.5.2.4.4.1. Con:
          Children are citizens with their own rights [322], needs, and desires and are not their parents' property (p. 17 [323]). Therefore, when they cannot advocate for their own rights, states must take extra care to monitor and act to protect them.

            1.5.2.4.4.1.1. Con:
            Many other vulnerable populations, like undocumented immigrants, are unable to access [324] judicial recourse when their rights are violated. States rarely take extra precautions to safeguard one group's rights at the expense of another.

          1.5.2.4.4.2. Con:
          Governments frequently intervene to help equalize conditions for those who are vulnerable, for example, those from historically discriminated communities [325].

          1.5.2.4.4.3. Pro:
          Governments do not generally force vulnerable people to undertake mandatory programs on account of their vulnerability.

        1.5.2.4.5. Con:
        The majority of education-based policies aim to improve the potential future for children and create more skilled workers (p. 3 [326]); these are not protection policies.

          1.5.2.4.5.1. Pro:
          Parents reserve the right to make sure certain skills and knowledge [327] are passed on to their children.

            1.5.2.4.5.1.1. Con:
            There is no reason why these skills cannot be passed down after school, over the weekend, or during summer vacation when parents spend more time [328] with their children.

          1.5.2.4.5.2. Con:
          States have a collective responsibility to prepare children for the future and the society they will enter. Failing to do so would be a violation of their responsibility to protect children.

            1.5.2.4.5.2.1. Con:
            Many basic life skills [329] are not taught in schools.

            1.5.2.4.5.2.2. Pro:
            States must ensure that students possess certain skills—like math and computer skills—that will allow them to do basic tasks in the future economy [330].

            1.5.2.4.5.2.3. Pro:
            States make sure all children have a mostly common knowledge of the most relevant history and culture that unites them to create national unity (p. 502 [331]).

    1.5.3. Pro:
    There have been a number of US court rulings [332] in favor of parents being able to control their child's education.

      1.5.3.1. Con:
      The most important court in America, the US Supreme Court, has never distinctly ruled on any homeschooling cases (p. 565 [333]).

        1.5.3.1.1. Pro:
        Without a direct Supreme Court decision [334], it cannot be said that American citizens have anything like a right to homeschool their children.

        1.5.3.1.2. Con:
        The 1925 "Pierce vs. Society of Sisters [335]" US Supreme Court ruling asserts that the State cannot "standardize its children by forcing them to accept instruction from public teachers only."

      1.5.3.2. Pro:
      There have been several cases where judges have protected the right to homeschool as a constitutional right [336].

        1.5.3.2.1. Con:
        Despite judges' rulings, the constitutional right to an education [337] is limited to attending public schools without cost. Homeschooling is not protected by the US Constitution.

        1.5.3.2.2. Pro:
        In 1933, the Michigan Supreme Court held that parents who are not trained teachers have a general right to homeschool [338].

    1.5.4. Pro:
    A parent homeschooling their children does not infringe upon anyone else's rights. Therefore, the state must respect their freedom of choice.

      1.5.4.1. Pro:
      Restrictions on individual freedoms are only justified in cases where one's actions necessarily imply the loss of someone else's [339] freedoms. Given that homeschooling can be done safely and effectively, it is unjust to ban it.

      1.5.4.2. Con:
      Even if an action does not infringe upon one person's rights, governments are allowed to ban it if it is bad for society as a whole.

        1.5.4.2.1. Pro:
        Even though not getting vaccinated does not infringe upon any one individual's rights, many governments still require people to get them due to the danger to everyone [340] in an unvaccinated society.

        1.5.4.2.2. Con:
        Even if this is true, it is usually only the case for serious social dangers, like stopping the spread of deadly illnesses. Homeschooling does not meet this bar.

  1.6. Pro:
  Homeschooling deprives children of an important environment for socializing.

    1.6.1. Con:
    Homeschooling promotes life skills [341] in addition to compulsory subject learning.

      1.6.1.1. Con:
      Homeschools are often unable to teach students important lessons and habits regarding their physical health. Conventional skills are better at this.

        1.6.1.1.1. Pro:
        Homeschooled students run the risk of health problems due to their isolated environment.

          1.6.1.1.1.1. Con:
          Homeschooling parents have many options to ensure their children are active and play sports.

            1.6.1.1.1.1.1. Pro:
            Many conventional schools allow homeschooled students to play sports with them [342].

            1.6.1.1.1.1.2. Pro:
            Many parents of competitive athletes opt for homeschooling precisely because it gives their children more time to play sports [343].

            1.6.1.1.1.1.3. Con:
            Many places restrict homeschooled students' access [344] to public sports teams.

            1.6.1.1.1.1.4. Con:
            When physical education classes are not mandatory, it is likely that more students will slip through the cracks, not engaging in any regular exercise.

          1.6.1.1.1.2. Pro:
          A study found that homeschooled children have higher risks of cardiovascular [345] diseases due to a lack of regular physical exercise.

        1.6.1.1.2. Pro:
        In most of the world, schools must give their students physical education [346], which generally involves some form of exercise and sport.

        1.6.1.1.3. Con:
        Children in conventional schools tend to have more unhealthy diets and lifestyles, leading to an increased rate of obesity [347].

      1.6.1.2. Pro:
      Homeschooled students are able to nurture their individuality [348] outside of the conformist environment [349] of traditional schools.

        1.6.1.2.1. Con:
        Homeschooling environments are more conformist, making it harder for children to rebel collectively [350].

        1.6.1.2.2. Pro:
        A study [351] of 30 homeschooling mothers found that most were motivated by a desire to more effectively teach their children life skills and moral, family, and religious values.

          1.6.1.2.2.1. Con:
          The sample size of this study is too small [352] to draw clear conclusions given the estimated 4.3 million [353] homeschooled children in the US.

        1.6.1.2.3. Pro:
        Homeschooled students report a higher sense of identity [354] than traditionally schooled students.

      1.6.1.3. Con:
      Many schools teach social skills and relationship lessons [355] as part of their curricula. These lessons are not guaranteed in homeschooling.

        1.6.1.3.1. Con:
        Many of these classes in conventional schools are poorly taught or completely neglected [356] by school staff.

        1.6.1.3.2. Con:
        Homeschooled students tend to score above average [357] on measures of social, emotional, and psychological development.

          1.6.1.3.2.1. Pro:
          Homeschooled children often learn to be self-sufficient [358] by learning how to make a budget, balance a checkbook, and problem solve real-life issues.

          1.6.1.3.2.2. Con:
          The vast majority of research on socialization [359] has been conducted by parents who have volunteered and are typically more engaged, and rely on self-assessments.

        1.6.1.3.3. Pro:
        Goals for social and emotional learning are included in the Social, Personal and Health Education Curriculum [360] in Ireland and the BC curriculum in Britain (p. 1 [361]).

    1.6.2. Pro:
    Homeschooled children find it more difficult to make friends [362].

      1.6.2.1. Con:
      Homeschooled students can interact regularly [363] with other children from a greater diversity of physical locations, allowing them more exposure outside of their socioeconomic and cultural groups.

        1.6.2.1.1. Pro:
        Homeschooling gives students more free time [364] than traditional educational environments. This gives them more time to engage in social activities in their communities.

          1.6.2.1.1.1. Pro:
          Homeschooled students do not waste time travelling long distances to get to school.

          1.6.2.1.1.2. Pro:
          Class lessons often involve a lot of wasted time [365]. Homeschooled lessons could be faster, leaving children with more free time.

            1.6.2.1.1.2.1. Con:
            Much of the 'wasted' time in schools is inherently social [366]. Many students travel to school together and hang out in their free time at school.

          1.6.2.1.1.3. Con:
          Stricter parents might opt to use the additional free time for more structured learning, which could cause fatigue and stress [367].

        1.6.2.1.2. Con:
        Children who attend traditional schools can interact with their classmates and other children during extra-curricular leisure and sports activities [368].

        1.6.2.1.3. Pro:
        Homeschooled children tend to be involved in a greater number [369] of extracurricular activities, and so have other opportunities to socialize with children their own age.

          1.6.2.1.3.1. Pro:
          Homeschooled students are significantly more involved in their communities [370]. Nearly nine in ten homeschoolers participate in volunteer associations [371] compared to 39% of final-year students [372] at traditional schools.

      1.6.2.2. Pro:
      Homeschooled students may be less aware of the popular trends, interests [373], and activities [374] in traditional schools. This can make it harder for them to find shared topics of interest with others.

        1.6.2.2.1. Con:
        With social media [375], new trends can easily be accessed by anyone, regardless of the school they attend.

        1.6.2.2.2. Pro:
        Social trends, new ideas, and artistic preferences often spread in the close environment [376] of schools.

        1.6.2.2.3. Con:
        The same issues affect countless students, like those who move from other countries with different cultures [377] and media.

      1.6.2.3. Pro:
      Homeschooling offers fewer opportunities for students to collaborate with peers on group projects, such as class assignments, theater or art performances, and team sports (pp. 64-65 [378]).

        1.6.2.3.1. Con:
        Many homeschooling communities, particularly within cities, set up study groups [379] for students, giving them a social environment similar to those in schools.

        1.6.2.3.2. Con:
        Many homeschooled children undertake a variety of social learning activities [380], like formal homeschool groups, that enable children to form intentional learning communities.

        1.6.2.3.3. Pro:
        Studies have shown that positive group experiences contribute to student learning, retention, and overall college success [381].

          1.6.2.3.3.1. Pro:
          Group projects can help students develop soft and social skills [382] that are increasingly important in the professional world.

      1.6.2.4. Con:
      Schools just provide a pool of children. They do not guarantee that students will make friends.

        1.6.2.4.1. Con:
        For low-income parents or families who live in remote areas [383], regular schools provide a minimum of socialization for children that homeschooling does not offer.

        1.6.2.4.2. Pro:
        Many children find school to be a lonely experience [384] and are unable to make many friends.

    1.6.3. Pro:
    Insufficient socialization [385] can make homeschooled children less equipped to interact with others, especially once they leave home (p. 3 [386]).

      1.6.3.1. Con:
      Schools are often not good socializing environments [387]. A more controlled home setting may be preferable.

        1.6.3.1.1. Pro:
        Bullying is one of the principal reasons [388] parents remove their children from traditional schooling and begin homeschooling.

          1.6.3.1.1.1. Con:
          Removing children from school because of bullying can have unintended consequences [389] such as impaired problem-solving skills and an over-reliance on authority figures.

          1.6.3.1.1.2. Con:
          Bullying by siblings [390] or even parents can occur in homeschooling situations as well.

          1.6.3.1.1.3. Pro:
          Children who are bullied are more likely to experience [391] depression, anxiety, physical health issues, and lower academic achievement.

        1.6.3.1.2. Pro:
        Social environments in homeschooling tend to be a lot less clique-driven than traditional schools [392].

        1.6.3.1.3. Con:
        Encountering anti-social behavior early in life provides children with the opportunity to learn how to deal with it early on [393] and avoid problems with it in the future.

      1.6.3.2. Con:
      Homeschooled students have statistically similar social [394] and emotional skills [395] compared to traditionally schooled students.

      1.6.3.3. Con:
      Homeschooled children are often taught the importance of family [396], which bodes well for their future and is more important than socializing with random children they will likely never see again [397] after graduation.

      1.6.3.4. Con:
      Homeschooled students avoid negative and dangerous peer influences and pressures.

        1.6.3.4.1. Pro:
        Social trends spread quickly [398] in traditional school environments due to the size of schools and high number of interactions between students.

        1.6.3.4.2. Con:
        Homeschooled students also face peer pressure on social media [399].

          1.6.3.4.2.1. Pro:
          This influence may be more pernicious as homeschooled students have less access to children of the same age, where they can see that many social media trends [400] are actually not being taken up by students.

          1.6.3.4.2.2. Con:
          Parents can more easily monitor [401] and help children cope with virtual peer pressure compared to physical, in-person pressure at a school.

        1.6.3.4.3. Pro:
        According to the National Survey on Drug Use and Health, religious adolescent homeschoolers were half as likely to report having been arrested or booked [402] and far less likely to report having a substance disorder (p. 1 [403]).

      1.6.3.5. Pro:
      Homeschooling attempts to construct a world for the child that often does not match reality. This can make adult life, particularly university or college, more challenging for the child.

        1.6.3.5.1. Pro:
        When choosing a homeschooling method parents might opt for the easiest option [404], which makes the child more susceptible to becoming overconfident in their abilities [405] and puts them at risk for a rude awakening in the real world.

          1.6.3.5.1.1. Con:
          Some types [406] of homeschooling encourage pupils to motivate themselves and develop their own approaches to learning which leaves them better equipped for the more independent learning-style environment of a university.

        1.6.3.5.2. Con:
        During their first year of college, homeschooled students scored significantly lower [407] on indicators of anxiety, emotional distress and social problems.

        1.6.3.5.3. Pro:
        Some homeschooled women feel that they were held back by their parents' ideologies, stunting their growth and potential at college (p. 117 [408]).

          1.6.3.5.3.1. Con:
          This study included 11 participants and only a minority of them expressed that they felt held captive by their parents' ideology (p. 114 [409]). This is not statistically significant evidence.

        1.6.3.5.4. Con:
        Conventional schools often fail to match reality. This can make adult life more challenging for the child.

          1.6.3.5.4.1. Pro:
          Public school students are often coddled [410], especially due to recent changes in teaching norms.

          1.6.3.5.4.2. Con:
          Traditionally schooled children still interact with the 'real world' in school, particularly through the experiences of other students facing 'real life' challenges [411].

            1.6.3.5.4.2.1. Pro:
            Many children have older siblings or friends who are less sheltered [412] due to their age.

            1.6.3.5.4.2.2. Pro:
            Many children experience 'real life' issues like budgeting and money issues [413] with their families.

            1.6.3.5.4.2.3. Con:
            Much of this interaction with the 'real world' comes from experiences children have with family. Homeschooling increases the quantity of these interactions, giving children a better understanding of what their life will be like.

      1.6.3.6. Pro:
      Compared to those who go to traditional schools, children educated at home in the UK are twice as likely [414] to be known by social services and four times as likely to be out of work, education, or training as a young adult.

        1.6.3.6.1. Con:
        Traditionally schooled children have been found to have eight times [415] as many problem behaviors as homeschooled children.

    1.6.4. Con:
    Homeschooled children are both more knowledgeable of, and active in, politics than those who attend traditional schools.

      1.6.4.1. Pro:
      In the US, nearly three-quarters [416] of 18-24 year-old homeschool graduates voted in national and state elections, while only 36% of public school graduates did the same; the gap grows with age.

      1.6.4.2. Con:
      Homeschooling often occurs in largely homogenous communities [417], making students less tolerant of those who differ greatly from those communities.

        1.6.4.2.1. Pro:
        Homeschool graduates often return to their communities, making them increasingly insular (p. 4 [418]).

        1.6.4.2.2. Pro:
        Given that many families choose homeschooling for political reasons [419], political education is a big part of many homeschooling experiences.

          1.6.4.2.2.1. Pro:
          Many conservative movements, particularly in the US, have promoted homeschooling [420] precisely to ensure that children are not taught left-wing views.

          1.6.4.2.2.2. Con:
          Political education is only useful if the child is given the opportunity to explore the entirety of the spectrum, and decide for themselves [421] how to self-identify politically. In many contexts, these opportunities do not exist.

        1.6.4.2.3. Con:
        Adults who were homeschooled are more politically tolerant [422] than those who attended traditional schools [423].

      1.6.4.3. Con:
      This is not an inherent aspect of traditional schools. It is the result of many schools not having enough political education [424]—something that could be solved through other reforms.

      1.6.4.4. Pro:
      More than one-third of US adults [425] consider politics and government too complicated to understand, compared to only 4.2% of homeschool graduates.
