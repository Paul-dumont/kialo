Discussion Name:
According to the Bible, salvation is by faith alone (sola fide)

1. Thesis:
According to the Bible, salvation is by faith alone (sola fide).

  1.1. Con:
  Baptism is also required for salvation.

    1.1.1. Pro:
    Mark 16:16 [1] says that "Whoever believes and is baptized will be saved."

      1.1.1.1. Con:
      The portion of Mark where this verse appears is missing from "the earliest manuscripts and some other ancient witnesses [2]."

      1.1.1.2. Con:
      This passage may imply that all who believe and are baptized are saved. However, this does not mean that those who believe but are not baptized are not saved.

    1.1.2. Pro:
    Galatians 3:27 [3] says that "all of you who were baptized into Christ have clothed yourselves with Christ."

      1.1.2.1. Con:
      This passage is not necessarily referring to water baptism.

        1.1.2.1.1. Pro:
        1 Corinthians 12:13 [4] discusses a nonliteral, spiritual baptism into Christ. Therefore, baptism into Christ does not have to refer to water baptism.

          1.1.2.1.1.1. Con:
          The Holy Spirit is closely associated [5] with Christian water baptism ever since Jesus' own baptism [6]. There is no need to think that baptism by the Spirit is different from water baptism.

          1.1.2.1.1.2. Con:
          If baptism is what adds [7] one to the church, Christ's body, then 1 Corinthians 12:13 is discussing a literal, water baptism for each individual.

        1.1.2.1.2. Con:
        Ephesians 4:15 shows that there is only one baptism as far as the church goes. The other baptism (holy Spirit baptism/baptism by fire) happened on the day of Pentecost to the apostles (Acts 1:1-5 and Acts 2:1-4) and to Cornelius and his household (Acts 11:15). Peter claims the Spirit fell upon them as it had in the beginning which shows that this type of baptism was not the standard.

    1.1.3. Pro:
    1 Peter 3:21 [8] refers to the "baptism that saves you."

      1.1.3.1. Con:
      The baptism Peter is talking about is "not the removal of dirt from the body but the pledge of a clear conscience toward God." [9] He clarifies that it's not the ritual itself that saves, but the pledge that accompanies the ritual.

        1.1.3.1.1. Pro:
        Being saved by pledging one's faith in Christ is consistent with sola fide.

          1.1.3.1.1.1. Con:
          There is no conflict between Sola Fide and the belief that baptism saves. Lutherans believe both.

            1.1.3.1.1.1.1. Con:
            Most people who promote 'faith-only' doctrines believe there is a conflict between faith-alone and baptism [10] being essential.

            1.1.3.1.1.1.2. Pro:
            Baptism would be involved as part of acting upon one's faith.

        1.1.3.1.2. Pro:
        Early Christians frequently baptized soon after the confession of faith [11], so it would make sense for Peter to closely associate the confession of faith with the baptism ritual.

        1.1.3.1.3. Con:
        Peter is saying the significance of baptism is that it's a pledge, not a mere removal of dirt from the body. The pledge doesn't accompany baptism, baptism is itself the pledge.

        1.1.3.1.4. Con:
        The language Peter uses (not... but...) is used to place emphasis. We might say it this way: baptism is not so much about getting wet as it is about receiving forgiveness for your sins and the joy that forgiveness brings.

          1.1.3.1.4.1. Pro:
          People went on their way rejoicing [12] following their baptisms.

          1.1.3.1.4.2. Pro:
          Similar language (not... but...) is used by Jesus to emphasis the importance of working for spiritual food more than for physical food in John 6:27 [13]

      1.1.3.2. Con:
      1 Peter 3:20 [14] compares the waters of baptism to the waters of Noah's flood. However, Noah was not saved by the flood waters, he was saved through the flood waters by the ark. Therefore, the waters of baptism symbolize salvation but do not cause it.

    1.1.4. Pro:
    In Acts 2:38 [15] Peter tells potential converts to "Repent and be baptized, every one of you, in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins. And you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit". This seems to imply that baptism is needed to be saved.

      1.1.4.1. Con:
      The Greek word eis [16], which is translated "for," should be understood as meaning "because of" rather than "in order to get" in this passage.

        1.1.4.1.1. Pro:
        Each of the other three Biblical uses of eis [17] where the passage is about baptism make sense when understood to mean "because of."

          1.1.4.1.1.1. Pro:
          Matthew 3:11 [18] describes baptism with water "to (eis) repentance." Being baptized "in order to get repentance" does not make sense, but being baptized "because of repentance" does make sense.

          1.1.4.1.1.2. Pro:
          Romans 6:3 [19] describes being baptized "into Christ Jesus" and "into His death." The idea of being baptized because of these things makes sense.

          1.1.4.1.1.3. Pro:
          1 Corinthians 10:2 [20] describes being baptized "into Moses in the cloud and in the sea." It makes sense that the Israelites were baptized "because of Moses," but they were not baptized "in order to get Moses."

      1.1.4.2. Con:
      This passage may imply that all those who repent and are baptized are saved. However, this says nothing about those who repent but are not baptized.

    1.1.5. Pro:
    Romans 6:3 [21] says that "all of us who were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death." Since Jesus's death is what saves us, this passage is saying we are baptized into salvation.

      1.1.5.1. Con:
      -> See 1.1.2.1.1.

      1.1.5.2. Con:
      The Greek word eis [22], which is translated "into," should be understood as meaning "because of" rather than "in order to get" in this passage [23].

        1.1.5.2.1. Pro:
        At least two other Biblical uses of eis [24] where the passage is about baptism make sense when understood to mean "because of."

          1.1.5.2.1.1. Pro:
          -> See 1.1.4.1.1.1.

          1.1.5.2.1.2. Pro:
          -> See 1.1.4.1.1.3.

        1.1.5.2.2. Con:
        The "causal use of eis" has been rejected by scholars of Greek [25], including many [26] who disagree that baptism accomplishes the forgiveness of sins.

      1.1.5.3. Con:
      Being baptized into something means "to be publicly identified with the thing you are being baptized into." [27]

        1.1.5.3.1. Con:
        There is no indication in Romans 6 that the baptism that is being addressed is a public identification.

      1.1.5.4. Con:
      Matthew 3:11 I [John the baptist] indeed baptize you with water unto repentance. but he that cometh after me [Jesus] is mightier than I, whose shoes I am not worthy to bear: he shall baptize you with the Holy Ghost, and with fire:

    1.1.6. Pro:
    John 3:5 [28] says that one must be "born of water and the spirit" to be saved. The "born of water" part means that water baptism is required.

      1.1.6.1. Con:
      The context of this passage does not mention baptism, so it wouldn't make sense to refer to baptism here.

      1.1.6.2. Con:
      "born of water" could reference ordinary birth, with the "water" meaning amniotic fluid.

      1.1.6.3. Con:
      "water" could refer to "spiritual cleansing or renewal." [29]

        1.1.6.3.1. Pro:
        Imagery involving water or washing is used to indicate spiritual cleansing elsewhere in the Bible.

          1.1.6.3.1.1. Pro:
          Titus 3:5 [30] describes "the washing of rebirth and renewal by the Holy Spirit."

          1.1.6.3.1.2. Pro:
          John 13:10 [31] uses literal washing as a metaphor for being spiritually clean.

          1.1.6.3.1.3. Pro:
          John 15:3 [32] describes those who received the word as "clean."

          1.1.6.3.1.4. Pro:
          1 Corinthians 6:11 [33] uses washing as a metaphor for sanctification.

          1.1.6.3.1.5. Pro:
          Hebrews 10:22 [34] speaks of "our hearts sprinkled clean from an evil conscience and our bodies washed with pure water," which is figurative language describing holiness.

          1.1.6.3.1.6. Pro:
          Ephesians 5:26 [35] connects "the washing of water with the word" with sanctification.

          1.1.6.3.1.7. Pro:
          Ezekiel 36:25-27 [36] uses imagery of clean water to represent sanctification.

          1.1.6.3.1.8. Pro:
          Isaiah 44:3 [37] compares the Holy Spirit to water metaphorically.

        1.1.6.3.2. Con:
        Romans 6:4 [38] explains that we are buried with Christ into his death when we are baptized by water, and that similar to the resurrection of Christ, we receive a newness of life when we are raised out of the water. See also Colossians 2:12-13 [39].

          1.1.6.3.2.1. Pro:
          There is no other way that one can be safely buried, except momentarily with water.

      1.1.6.4. Pro:
      Church fathers interpreted John 3:5 to refer to water baptism.

        1.1.6.4.1. Pro:
        Justin Martyr interpreted John 3:5 to refer to water baptism (First Apology 61 [40]).

        1.1.6.4.2. Pro:
        Tertullian interpreted John 3:5 to refer to water baptism (On Baptism 12 [41]).

      1.1.6.5. Pro:
      -> See 1.1.6.3.2.

    1.1.7. Con:
    Abraham is used as an example of how to be saved (Romans 4:1-3 [42]), even though he was not baptized. Paul would not have chosen an unbaptized individual for this if he intended to teach that baptism was required for salvation.

      1.1.7.1. Con:
      James 2:20-24 explains that Abraham's faith involved doing what God told him to do (namely, sacrifice his son, Isaac). Abraham wasn't told to be baptized, but had he been told to do so, he would have acted in faith to be baptized.

    1.1.8. Pro:
    If work isn’t required, “why are they then baptized for the dead?” (1 Corinthians 15:29 [43])

      1.1.8.1. Con:
      Paul is describing the practice of baptism for the dead but he never actually comments on it being necessary or important.

      1.1.8.2. Con:
      Baptism for the dead in 1 Corinthians 15:29 refers to new converts being baptized to replace those who have died, not a work done to achieve salvation for dead people.

    1.1.9. Con:
    There are accounts of people being saved before experiencing salvation. This shows that the two function independently.

      1.1.9.1. Pro:
      In Acts 9:17-18 [44] Saul was saved before being baptized.

        1.1.9.1.1. Pro:
        First, Ananias says “Brother Saul, the Lord—Jesus, who appeared to you on the road as you were coming here—has sent me so that you may see again and be filled with the Holy Spirit.” Immediately afterward, "something like scales fell from Saul’s eyes, and he could see again," which sounds like Paul was saved at that point. He was only baptized after that point.

          1.1.9.1.1.1. Con:
          There are two separate things taking place. 'Seeing again' and 'being filled with the Holy Spirit.' The scales falling from his eyes allowed him to see again after having been blind for 3 days. The physical healing is not what saves Paul spiritually. Rather, it is the baptism which followed the physical healing that saves Paul (cf Acts 9:17-18)

          1.1.9.1.1.2. Con:
          If we look at the retelling of this event in Acts 22, we see Ananias telling Saul (after he received his sight) to be baptized and wash away [his] sins. If Saul was saved when the scales fell, Ananias wouldn't have told him he still needed to wash away his sins.

      1.1.9.2. Pro:
      In Acts 10:44-48 [45] Cornelius is saved before being baptized.

        1.1.9.2.1. Con:
        Acts 10:44-48 [46] describes an extraordinary circumstance, which means that the regular rules regarding baptism may not apply.

          1.1.9.2.1.1. Con:
          In Acts 11:16-17 [47] Peter connected the way the believers in Acts 10 were saved with the way he and other believers were saved. He would not have done this if Acts 10 was an "extraordinary circumstance."

            1.1.9.2.1.1.1. Con:
            Peter wasn't willing to stand in 'God's way' (Acts 11:17) by forbidding baptism to the Gentiles. If this had been an ordinary circumstance, Peter would have understood that the Gentiles could be baptized for forgiveness of their sins without God's display of the Holy Spirit coming on Cornelius as 'at the beginning' (cf Acts 11:15)

    1.1.10. Pro:
    Acts 22:16 [48] says that we should "be baptized and wash [our] sins away."

      1.1.10.1. Con:
      The clause is modified with the phrase "[while] calling on his name." Our sins are washed away by calling on the name of the Lord, not by being baptized.

      1.1.10.2. Con:
      "Baptism" does not only refer to water baptisism. John the Baptist said he baptises by water but the only coming after him [Jesus] would baptise by "truth and spirit". Acts 22:4 [49] And, being assembled together with them, commanded them that they should not depart from Jerusalem, but wait for the promise of the Father, which, saith he, ye have heard of me. 5 [50] For John truly baptized with water; but ye shall be baptized with the Holy Ghost not many days hence.

  1.2. Pro:
  Habakkuk 2:4 [51] says that "the one who is right and good will live by his faith."

    1.2.1. Con:
    The word [52] translated as "faith" arguably means "faithfulness," which could include works.

    1.2.2. Pro:
    Galatians 3:11 [53] reaffirms this passage in the New Testament, saying "Clearly no one who relies on the law is justified before God, because “the righteous will live by faith.”".

    1.2.3. Con:
    Living by faith involves acting upon that faith. Case in point: This person demonstrates righteousness and goodness as he 'lives by his faith.'

  1.3. Pro:
  Romans 5:1 [54] says that "since we have been justified through faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ."

    1.3.1. Con:
    Technically, the passage doesn't say "since we have been justified through faith alone..."

    1.3.2. Con:
    James 2:22 [55] says, "You see that his faith and his actions were working together, and his faith was made complete by what he did." To be justified by faith, you need actions, as this verse points out. Pointing to Romans 5:1 alone as proof for "faith alone" is rejecting the scriptures.

  1.4. Pro:
  The Bible suggests that a belief in Jesus creates salvation.

    1.4.1. Pro:
    Genesis 15:6 [56] says that "Abram believed the Lord, and he credited it to him as righteousness." No works were required.

      1.4.1.1. Con:
      In Psalm 106:30-31 [57] Phinehas's good work was credited to him as righteousness.

        1.4.1.1.1. Con:
        The contexts are different. Genesis 15:6 [58] is describing how Abram got saved, while Psalm 106:30-31 [59] happens after Phinehas had been saved in the past.

        1.4.1.1.2. Con:
        Genesis 15:6 is reaffirmed as still applicable in the New Testament era [60], while Psalm 106:30-31 is not.

        1.4.1.1.3. Con:
        The righteousness being credited to Phinehas involved his descendants gaining a "covenant of a lasting priesthood" (Numbers 25:10-13 [61]), whereas in Abraham's case it was justification. Thus, Phinehas's being "credited righteousness" is not relevant to justification in context.

        1.4.1.1.4. Con:
        Phinehas's work was credited as righteousness by the future generations of Israelite people (Psalm 106:31 [62]), not by God. Thus, Psalm 106 isn't relevant to justification because justification is specifically God crediting righteousness to a person.

        1.4.1.1.5. Pro:
        The same language is used to describe being credited righteousness in Genesis 15:6 and Psalm 106:30-31.

      1.4.1.2. Con:
      Abram believed the lord so stopped being a murdering warlord. As Jesus taught, "thou shalt not murder", and Abram repented because he believed the lord, which is what brought him salvation. If Abram did not believe God then Abram would of continued to be a murdering warlord, and be condemned for murder.

    1.4.2. Pro:
    In Luke 23:42-43 [63] the thief on the cross was saved just by asking Jesus to remember him. No further works were required.

      1.4.2.1. Con:
      Jesus did not expect the thief to do any works because the thief was unable to do any before he died. However, Jesus may expect more from us.

      1.4.2.2. Con:
      The thief was not under the new covenant because Jesus hadn't died yet (Ephesians 2:14 and Colossians 2:14-15)

    1.4.3. Con:
    The "belief" in these verses could be trust in all that Jesus taught rather than singling out one line of what He said.

      1.4.3.1. Con:
      The Greek verb pisteuo [64], which is translated "believing" or "who believes" in John 3:16 [65], does not include works by definition according to several credible lexicons.

        1.4.3.1.1. Pro:
        Strong's Concordance [66] defines it as "believe", "have faith in", or "trust in."

        1.4.3.1.2. Pro:
        HELPS Word Studies [67] says that it means "believe," "affirm," or "have confidence." This source also says that the word derives from a term meaning "to be persuaded."

        1.4.3.1.3. Pro:
        NAS Exhaustive Concordance [68] defines it as "to believe" or "entrust."

        1.4.3.1.4. Pro:
        Thayer's Greek Lexicon [69] gives two possible usages: "to think to be true; to be persuaded of; to credit, place confidence in," or "to intrust [sic] a thing to one, i.e. to his fidelity."

        1.4.3.1.5. Pro:
        Strong's Exhaustive Concordance [70] gives the definition "believe, put in trust with."

      1.4.3.2. Pro:
      If I (accurately) tell my brother a bomb is going to go off in the basement where he is and then say he's alive because he believed me, people will understand that my brother got out or disarmed the bomb or took some other necessary course of action. If we truly believe in Jesus, the Word made flesh, we will take the necessary course of action that He puts forth in plenty of other verses which are amply supplied in other claims.

    1.4.4. Con:
    John 8:31 [71] states "To the Jews who had believed him, Jesus said, “If you hold to my teaching, you are really my disciples." However, in John 8:44 [72] he makes it clear these Jews are not saved. Thus, belief is insufficient for salvation.

      1.4.4.1. Con:
      The Jews mentioned in John 8:30-32 are different [73] from the ones Jesus talks to elsewhere in the chapter.

        1.4.4.1.1. Con:
        The use of "they" in John 8:33 [74] and 8:39 imply that the Jews in 8:30-32 are the same ones Jesus talks to elsewhere.

          1.4.4.1.1.1. Con:
          The pronoun "they" does not necessarily have to refer to the closest nearby noun it could refer to, and in John 8:33 and 8:39 it probably refers to the Jews from 8:27.

      1.4.4.2. Con:
      The Jews in John 8:31 and 8:44 do believe, but this is insufficient for salvation because it lacks parts of the belief required for sola fide justification.

        1.4.4.2.1. Con:
        This is implausible because the same language used in John 8:30 [75] to describe the Jews believing in Jesus is used in John 3:16 [76] to say "everyone believing in him shall not perish."

        1.4.4.2.2. Pro:
        The Jews in John 8:31 and 8:44 believed in Jesus's words in 8:28-29, but did not accept Jesus as their savior.

        1.4.4.2.3. Pro:
        The Jews in John 8:31 and 8:44 intellectually assented to Christianity but refused to accept Jesus's call to obedience.

      1.4.4.3. Con:
      The Jews mentioned in John 8:30-36 are different from the ones Jesus talks to elsewhere in the chapter.

        1.4.4.3.1. Con:
        The use of "you" both in John 8:36 and 8:37 implies that Jesus is talking to the same people in these verses.

      1.4.4.4. Con:
      The Jews believed in Jesus in John 8:30 but gave up this belief by 8:31.

        1.4.4.4.1. Pro:
        Many translations [77] use the pluperfect "had believed" in John 8:31.

        1.4.4.4.2. Con:
        This is unacceptable for Sola Fide proponents who also hold to eternal security.

    1.4.5. Pro:
    Several passages in the book of Romans state that belief is needed for salvation.

      1.4.5.1. Pro:
      In Romans 1:16 [78] Paul calls the gospel the "power of God that brings salvation to everyone who believes: first to the Jew, then to the Gentile."

      1.4.5.2. Pro:
      In Romans 4:11 [79], God is described as "the father of all who believe but have not been circumcised, in order that righteousness might be credited to them."

      1.4.5.3. Pro:
      Romans 4:23-24 [80] says that "The words 'it was credited to him' were written not for [Abram] alone, 24 but also for us, to whom God will credit righteousness—for us who believe in him who raised Jesus our Lord from the dead."

      1.4.5.4. Pro:
      Romans 10:4 [81] says that "Christ is the culmination of the law so that there may be righteousness for everyone who believes."

        1.4.5.4.1. Con:
        Righteousness involves a choice. Making a choice requires an action.

          1.4.5.4.1.1. Con:
          However, Romans 10:4 says that this righteousness is guaranteed for all those who believe, so therefore nothing other than faith is required to achieve this righteousness that leads to salvation.

            1.4.5.4.1.1.1. Con:
            Romans 10:4 really says that for everyone who believes, Christ is the END of the law for righteousness. Christ is the end because of his suffering in Gethsemane and on the cross. Christ is the end because he suffered the punishment for our sins and therefore sets the terms for our salvation. In John 14:6, Christ says that no man comes to the Father except by him.

        1.4.5.4.2. Pro:
        (Premise 1) There is righteousness for everyone who believes (Romans 10:4).

        1.4.5.4.3. Pro:
        (Premise 2) If there is righteousness for someone, then they will be saved because the righteous are saved.

        1.4.5.4.4. Pro:
        (Conclusion) Everyone who believes is saved.

        1.4.5.4.5. Con:
        Righteousness is acting in accord to divine or moral law and being free from guilt or sin. Repentance is required to be free from sin and in doing so we purify ourselves and become more like Christ. Repentance takes work, not just belief, but acting on those beliefs. See 1 John 3:1-10 [82].

          1.4.5.4.5.1. Pro:
          We purify ourselves through repentance [83]. We repent by recognizing what we did is wrong, confessing to God and those we've wronged, doing what we can to correct our wrongs, and most importantly we stop sinning and never repeat the same sin again. Such a process leads to purification and helps us to become more like Christ.

      1.4.5.5. Pro:
      Romans 10:9-10 [84] says that "if you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved."

        1.4.5.5.1. Con:
        Confessing your sins involves an action, not just faith or belief alone.

        1.4.5.5.2. Con:
        Philippians 2:10-12 [85] and Romans 14:11 [86] show that not any one scripture can be used as a proof for what is required for salvation. If every knee shall bow and every tongue shall confess that Jesus Christ is Lord to the glory of the Father then according to these scriptures alone all are saved regardless of their level of belief or their good works.

          1.4.5.5.2.1. Con:
          Under an annihilationist [87] view of final judgment, it is indeed the case that all people "bowing" before Jesus in these passages are saved, so this fails to refute the argument if annihilationism is true.

            1.4.5.5.2.1.1. Pro:
            The annihilationist [88] can argue that these passages happen after God has already annihilated all the wicked, so those who remain are all saved individuals.

          1.4.5.5.2.2. Con:
          Under a universalist [89] view of final judgment, it is indeed the case that all people "bowing" before Jesus in these passages are saved, so this fails to refute the argument if universalism is true.

            1.4.5.5.2.2.1. Pro:
            The universalist can argue that these passages are set after all those who are currently wicked have been saved, so all the people in these passages really are saved.

    1.4.6. Pro:
    Apostle John declares that belief in Jesus is necessary for salvation.

      1.4.6.1. Pro:
      John 3:16 [90] says that all who believe in Jesus will have eternal life. If a requirement other than faith was needed for salvation, some people who believed in Jesus would not be saved and the verse would be contradicted.

      1.4.6.2. Pro:
      In John 6:33-35 [91] Jesus says "I am the bread of life; he who comes to Me will not hunger, and he who believes in Me will never thirst."

        1.4.6.2.1. Pro:
        Coming to Jesus and believing in him are treated as synonymous in this passage.

      1.4.6.3. Pro:
      In John 6:28-29 [92] Jesus is asked what works one must do to be saved, and he replies that the work of God is to believe.

        1.4.6.3.1. Pro:
        Jesus took the question about erga (works plural) [93] and responded by identifying the ergon (work singular) [94] that he thought was necessary. If something other than faith was needed to be saved he would not have used the singular in verse 29.

        1.4.6.3.2. Con:
        John 6:28-29 [95] can easily be interpreted that they must have faith in Jesus Christ themselves first, also a work of God, to work the works of God.

      1.4.6.4. Pro:
      John 1:12 [96] says that all "who believed in his name" were saved. If someone who believed in Jesus's name was not saved because they failed to fulfil another requirement, that would contradict this passage.

    1.4.7. Pro:
    Several passages in Acts imply that belief is needed for salvation.

      1.4.7.1. Pro:
      Acts 10:43 [97] says that "All the prophets testify about him that everyone who believes in him receives forgiveness of sins through his name."

        1.4.7.1.1. Con:
        If Acts 10:43 [98] was only about belief, then Paul would not have commanded everyone who heard to do the work of baptism in Acts 10:48 [99].

          1.4.7.1.1.1. Con:
          Acts 10:48 [100] says that "...he commanded them to be baptism in the name of the Lord." However, there is no indication from either Peter or the author of Acts that if they weren't baptized, they would not have been saved.

            1.4.7.1.1.1.1. Con:
            Paul was commanded by Christ to teach the gospel and even Christ himself was baptized. We are taught to be examples to others like Christ is our own ensample or exemplar . If Baptism was not necessary, he wouldn’t have commanded it of everyone within the reach of his voice. And baptism is such a simple act to do. What kind of faith is it really to be teaching people not to follow and do the things Christ taught and telling them it’s not necessary?

              1.4.7.1.1.1.1.1. Con:
              Paul commanded baptism not because it was required for salvation, but because it is an excellent work for showing one's dedication to God and is highly recommended.

        1.4.7.1.2. Con:
        The issue is the term "believe in". If you "believe in" exercise you go to the gym and lose weight, or you don't "believe in" exercise, you "believe in" being lazy. A belief that exercise will make you healthy does nothing if you don't follow your belief. If you "believe in" Jesus then you follow His teachings of "love your neighbor as yourself" and are saved, otherwise you "believe in" serving only yourself.

      1.4.7.2. Pro:
      Acts 16:31 [101] implies that if one believes, they will be saved.

    1.4.8. Pro:

      1.7.8.3. Con:
      In Matthew 5:48 [204] Christ commands us to be perfect, even as our Father in Heaven is perfect, therefore there must be a prescribed way of becoming perfect.

        1.7.8.3.1. Pro:
        Christ prescribed the way to perfection by offering the infinite atonement and suffering for our sins. By using Christ’s prescription where our repentance is a key part, we can eliminate sins from our life as if they never happened and become perfect (Isaiah 1:16-18 [205]).

    1.7.9. Pro:
    Faith alone is contrary to the Bible's teachings on salvation. Our Lord said, "Not every one that saith to me, Lord, shall enter into the kingdom of heaven: but he that doth the will of my Father who is in heaven, he shall enter into the kingdom of heaven." (Matthew 7, 21)

    1.7.10. Pro:
    Many verses in the first Corinthians support work being required for salvation.

      1.7.10.1. Pro:
      1 Corinthians 3:10-15 [206] describes people's works being used as a basis for the rewards they receive in the afterlife. This contradicts sola fide, which denies that works play a role in bringing about salvation.

        1.7.10.1.1. Con:
        In 1 Corinthians 3:10-15 [207] both groups of builders, the ones with works of "gold, silver, costly stones" and the ones with works of "wood, hay or straw," are saved. Verses 14 and 15 clearly teach this. Since this passage is not differentiating between saved and unsaved people, and is instead describing how different saved people will experience differing amounts of reward in Heaven, it is not relevant to the sola fide debate.

      1.7.10.2. Pro:
      -> See 1.1.8.

      1.7.10.3. Pro:
      In 1 Corinthians 9:24-27 [208] Paul does works so that he might "win the prize" (i.e. be saved).

        1.7.10.3.1. Con:
        In 1 Corinthians 9:24-27 [209] the prize is Paul's ability to continue in his ministry, not in his salvation. Works can influence a person's ability to be a minister even if they don't influence salvation.

          1.7.10.3.1.1. Pro:
          In Acts 20:24 [210] Paul uses the imagery of finishing a race to refer to "the task of testifying to the good news of God’s grace," which is a reference to Paul's ministry work, not his salvation.

          1.7.10.3.1.2. Con:
          When Paul says that "all the runners run" in this race (1 Corinthians 9:24 [211]), he is implying that everyone is involved in the race he is describing, not just ministers. Therefore, this is about salvation, not ministry.

            1.7.10.3.1.2.1. Con:
            It could be that the "runners" are simply all the other ministers.

      1.7.10.4. Pro:
      According to 2 Corinthians 13:5 [212] Paul says "Examine yourselves to see whether you are in the faith; test yourselves. Do you not realize that Christ Jesus is in you—unless, of course, you fail the test?" The "test" sounds like works being required for salvation.

        1.7.10.4.1. Con:
        We need to test ourselves with works to prove that we are saved, not to cause our salvation. Sola fide is about whether works causally contribute to salvation, not whether they can demonstrate our having been saved.

    1.7.11. Pro:
    Philippians 2:12 [213] says that we should "continue to work out [our] salvation with fear and trembling." This seems to indicate that our salvation is dependent upon continued works.

      1.7.11.1. Con:
      The usage of "work out salvation" instead of "work for salvation" means that the salvation preceded the works, and therefore the works did not cause salvation.

        1.7.11.1.1. Pro:
        When someone "works out" a muscle, it implies that they already have the muscle and are just making it stronger. Similarly, when one "works out" their salvation, they already have salvation and are making it "stronger" (by providing benefit to others or increasing their future rewards in Heaven).

      1.7.11.2. Con:
      In Philippians 1:1 [214] Paul had established his audience as "God's holy people," implying that his intended audience consists of people who are already saved. This cannot be about how to gain salvation because Paul's audience has already gained salvation.

      1.7.11.3. Pro:
      In Roman's 2 Paul said both the believer in God and the unbeliever will be judged exactly the same, by their "deeds". The "works" that do not save are the works of the law of Moses, circumcision, not eating pork, etc...         1.7.11.3.1. Con:
        -> See 1.7.3.1.5.3.1.4.

        1.7.11.3.2. Con:
        -> See 1.7.3.1.5.3.1.5.

  1.8. Con:
  The Eucharist [215] (i.e. communion/the sacrament) is also required for salvation.

    1.8.1. Pro:
    Matthew 26:27-28 [216] describes the Eucharistic wine as being "poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins." This implies that the Eucharist is needed for someone to have their sins forgiven and be saved.

      1.8.1.1. Con:
      The thing that is actually poured out in the passage [217] is "[Jesus's] blood of the covenant." Whether the communion wafer is literally or symbolically Jesus's blood, the point is that Jesus's blood is what forgives sins, not the communion ritual.

      1.8.1.2. Con:
      The elements of communion are part of a symbolic practice prescribed by God as a way of remembering the sacrifice Jesus made for us in His death on the cross and as an act of praise and thanksgiving for His death and resurrection power. This is very similar to Old Testament festivals commanded by God through with the Jews passed down testimonies of God’s work to subsequent generations.

    1.8.2. Pro:
    1 Corinthians 11:23-29 [218] says we should examine ourselves for if we partake of the sacrament unworthily, we eat and drink damnation to ourselves. This suggests those who are damned are not saved and the sacrament is required for salvation. See also John 5:29 [219].

      1.8.2.1. Con:
      This may say that "not taking the sacrament unworthily is required for salvation," but not "taking the sacrament is required for salvation." Those are logically distinct ideas.

        1.8.2.1.1. Pro:
        Someone who never takes the sacrament at all may be saved according to the former statement but is definitely not saved according to the latter one.

      1.8.2.2. Con:
      Those that "eat and drink unworthily" may be precisely those that didn't have any faith to begin with.

    1.8.3. Con:
    Children under the age of reason can achieve salvation without the need for the Eucharist [220].

    1.8.4. Pro:
    In John 6:53 [221], Jesus says "Truly, truly, I say to you, unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink his blood, you have no life in you."

      1.8.4.1. Con:
      This passage, in its context, is talking about accepting the teachings/word of Jesus. The Lord's supper is not instituted until later in His ministry.

    1.8.5. Con:
    If someone believes, repents, professes Christ as Lord, and is baptized into Him on a Thursday and then dies on Saturday, they would have done everything required for them (never having had an opportunity to observe communion on the first day of the week (Acts 20:7 [222]).

      1.8.5.1. Pro:
      The biblical example of communion took place on the first day of the week (Acts 20:7 [223]). However, we see a sense of urgency concerning baptism (the Philippian jailer was baptized in the same hour of the night that he learned about the gospel, the Ethiopian eunuch did not want to delay as soon as he and Phillip came across water, Ananias asked Saul what he was waiting for).

  1.9. Pro:
  The Bible shares that a person will be saved by calling on the Lord. Such an act requires faith.

    1.9.1. Pro:
    Romans‬ ‭10:13 [224]‬ states, "For, "whoever calls on the name of the LORD shall be saved.”

      1.9.1.1. Con:
      Calling on the name of the Lord is prayer and prayer is work, not just faith.

        1.9.1.1.1. Con:
        One cannot truly pray without faith, nor can one truly have faith without engaging in some sort of prayer. This lack of separation makes it hard to distinctly call prayer a work separate from faith.

    1.9.2. Con:
    In Matthew 7:21 [225], Jesus says “Not everyone who says to me, ‘Lord, Lord,’ will enter the kingdom of heaven, but the one who does the will of my Father who is in heaven."

    1.9.3. Con:
    There's a difference between an act that requires faith and faith alone.

      1.9.3.1. Pro:
      The controversial part of 'sola fide' is the 'sola part, not the 'fide' part. Since this verse doesn't mention 'faith alone', it doesn't prove 'sola fide'.

        1.9.3.1.1. Pro:
        Many Christians believe in salvation by faith without believing in salvation by faith -alone-.

          1.9.3.1.1.1. Pro:
          Catholics believe in salvation by faith, but not salvation by faith alone.

          1.9.3.1.1.2. Pro:
          Eastern Orthodoxy teaches salvation by faith, but not salvation by faith alone.

          1.9.3.1.1.3. Pro:
          Less than half [226] of US Protestants believe that salvation is by faith alone.

  1.10. Con:
  The only time the phrase "faith alone [227]" is used in the Bible, it's rejected.

  1.11. Con:
  The Bible contains too many contradicting perspectives to provide a definitive answer about what is required for salvation.

    1.11.1. Con:
    The Bible offers a combination of experiences that lead to salvation, all of which are required. Namely, one must hear (Romans 10:14), believe and confess (Romans 10:10), repent and be baptized (Acts 2:38), and remain faithful (Revelation 2:10). These progress respectively as follows; one must have a change of information, accept and acknowledge that information, have a change of heart and response to the call of the information, maintain allegiance to the information.

  1.12. Con:
  Love is also required for salvation.

    1.12.1. Pro:
    Galatians 5:6 [228] says that "The only thing that counts is faith expressing itself through love." Apparently faith only matters if it is paired with love.

    1.12.2. Con:
    Anyone who truly believes in Christ will have love as a result of this belief.

      1.12.2.1. Pro:
      If one truly trusts in Christ, one will also trust in his commandments, including the commandment to love thy neighbor [229]. This does not mean that love helps to cause salvation, it means that true faith leads to both salvation and love.

    1.12.3. Pro:
    1 Corinthians 13:2 [230] says that one can have "all the faith" yet still be nothing if they do not also have love. Thus, salvation is not by faith alone.

    1.12.4. Pro:
    1 Corinthians 13:1-3 [231] says that one who does not have love gains nothing.
