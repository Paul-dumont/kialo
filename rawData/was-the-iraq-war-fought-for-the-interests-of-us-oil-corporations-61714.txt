Discussion Name:
Was the Iraq War fought for the interests of U.S Oil Corporations?

1. Thesis:
The Iraq War was fought for the interests of U.S Oil Companies.

  1.1. Pro:
  The Global Policy Forum commented in 2007 that " 'friendly' companies expect to gain most of the lucrative oil deals that will be worth hundreds of billions of dollars in profits in the coming decades [1]".

    1.1.1. Pro:
    The GPF furthers states that because of U.S influence on the 2005 Constitution of Iraq, the Constitution "contains language that guarantees a major role for foreign companies [2]".

  1.2. Con:
  In 1998, the Chevron CEO at the time, Kenneth T. Derr, stated in a speech that "It might surprise you to learn that even though Iraq possesses huge reserves of oil and gas – reserves I'd love Chevron to have access to – I fully agree with the sanctions we have imposed on Iraq [3]".

  1.3. Con:
  It was believed at the time that Iraq possessed [4] weapons of mass destruction and therefore had to be stopped.

    1.3.1. Con:
    Few countries own more weapons of mass destruction [5] than the US, so this is not a valid justification for waging the Iraq War.

  1.4. Con:
  Saddam said [6] that the U.S did not need to invade Iraq to obtain control of Iraq's Oil Supply, and just prior to the invasion was ready to sign a deal giving the U.S priority over other nations. The fact that Bush(Who certainly had a conflict of interest regarding the oil industry [7]) rejected this offer leads to the conclusion that control of Iraqi Oil by U.S Oil companies was not a serious consideration for the Bush Adminstration's justification for the war.

    1.4.1. Pro:
    Journalist Muhammad Idrees Ahmad stated that "Gulf energy resources have always been a vital US interest. On no other occasion has the US had to occupy a country to secure them. [8]"

    1.4.2. Con:
    That would depend on the specific details of the deal. Sure, Saddam could have offered priority access, but something must have been demanded in return for the administration to reject it.

  1.5. Con:
  According to the political scientist Jeff Colgan, the evidence leans against such a claim because oil contracts post invasion were given to non-American companies, including Chinese and Russian ones. [9]

    1.5.1. Con:
    Oil interest includes more than direct contracts with the Iraqi government. If the oil production and export infrastructure in Iraq is damaged by the war then the value of oil produced in other countries increases. This is one reason why Chevron, who did not have oil production contracts in Iraq, but elsewhere saw an oil net-income increase [10] 20 fold in the 5 years following the war in Iraq. The war was in the direct interest of oil companies who did not have Iraq based contracts (page FS-2).

    1.5.2. Con:
    Just because non-American companies received contracts does not mean the war, primarily fought by the United States, was not primarily fought in the interests of their oil companies.

      1.5.2.1. Con:
      Nor, however, does it mean that it was primarily fought for that reason in a definite. It is possible, but not demonstrable simply because it cannot be disproven that it was, in fact, a corporate oil war.

  1.6. Con:
  Instead, the true drivers of the Iraq War were more aligned with national security concerns, neoconservative ideology, and a desire to reshape the Middle East. The Bush administration, influenced by neoconservative thinkers, sought to eliminate Saddam Hussein, not just because of Iraq’s oil but due to a belief that regime change could spread democracy and stability across the region. This goal, while flawed, demonstrates the administration’s broader ambitions beyond corporate gain.

    1.6.1. Con:
    “Not just because of Iraq’s oil” implies some rationale for the war was due to oil interests.

  1.7. Pro:
  Numerous officials in the Bush Adminstration(Including Bush) had oil industry connections or had held prior post there.

    1.7.1. Pro:
    National Security Advisor Condolezza Rice was a former director of Chevron. [11]

    1.7.2. Pro:
    Commerce Secretary Donald Evans formerly was the head of Tom Brown Inc, an oil company. [12]

    1.7.3. Con:
    If the rationale was oil control(corporate or not), then it would have made more sense to invade Venezuela, which contains the largest oil reserves in the world, rather than Iraq, concluded [13] journalist Muhammad Idrees Ahmad.

      1.7.3.1. Pro:
      U.S Oil interests were far more threatened in Latin America than any other place, according [14] to Ahmad.

      1.7.3.2. Con:
      Just because another choice was better in one journalist’s mind doesn’t mean that the Bush administration shared their reasoning.

      1.7.3.3. Con:
      The United States had no justification to invade Venezuela at this time, as they could not have blamed the September 11th attacks on them.

    1.7.4. Con:
    Political figures often have connections with prominent business people, but this, in itself, is not evidence that a war was waged by the former for the benefit of the latter.

      1.7.4.1. Con:
      While true, one politician in particular, Dick Cheney, was the former CEO of Halliburton who later became Vice President. I think it would be reasonable to argue that the person holding the second-highest political office formerly holding ownership of a company that benefitted from the war *his administration started* represents some conflict of interest.

    1.7.5. Pro:
    George W. Bush and Dick Cheney were CEOs of companies such as Arbusto, Harken Energy, Spectrum 7, and Haliburton(All oil or oil related companies). [15]
