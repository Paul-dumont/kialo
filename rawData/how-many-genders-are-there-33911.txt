            1.2.2.1.1.2.1. Con:

          1.2.2.1.2.1. Pro:

          1.2.2.1.2.2. Con:

          Intersex people are often subjected to surgery without their consent to force them into one of the two biological sex categories [136]. This proves that the binary conception of sex is a social mischaracterisation.

          1.2.5.2.1.2. Con:
          Less than 2% [137] of the population is intersex. This figure is too small to warrant a change in how sex is conceptualized and classified, when its established definition adequately accommodates 98% of the population.

            1.2.5.2.1.2.1. Con:
            Changing the conception of sex to include intersex identities may have little real impact on the remainder of the population, but it would significantly improve [138] the lives of intersex individuals.

            1.2.5.2.1.2.2. Pro:
            Biological males and females are not only a majority but also relatively homogeneous in their sexual characteristics. In contrast, intersex individuals are significantly heterogeneous [139]; this limits the practical utility of such a category.

        1.2.5.2.2. Con:
        This implies that those with a gender identity outside of the binary must have a sex that is outside of the binary, which is not the case.

        1.2.5.2.3. Pro:
        Sex is determined based on a variety of markers, including chromosomes, sexual organs, and hormones. Individuals present with a varied combination [140] of these markers and can, therefore, be situated along a continuum ranging from typical biological female to typical biological male.

          1.2.5.2.3.1. Pro:
          Development can uncover several deviations from what is typically expected for each sex. Consequently, throughout the developmental lifespan [141], the sex of an individual can change within the spectrum.

        1.2.5.2.4. Pro:
        There are several chromosomal aberrations that lead to biological males and females having sex chromosomes that differ from the typical configuration.

          1.2.5.2.4.1. Pro:
          Females with Turner Syndrome [142] have only one X chromosome and no Y chromosome.

          1.2.5.2.4.2. Pro:
          Males with Klinefelter Syndrome [143] have two X chromosomes in addition to their single Y chromosome.

          1.2.5.2.4.3. Pro:
          Females with Trisomy X [144] have a third X chromosome. Though this condition is in 0.1% of all females, most are never diagnosed.

          1.2.5.2.4.4. Con:
          Chromosomal aberrations [145] are - as the name suggests - disorders [146], and give rise to other medical problems [147]. Therefore, the number of sex categories in the normal population should not be determined based on what is observed among clinical samples.

          1.2.5.2.4.5. Con:
          The percentage of the population with aberrant sex chromosomes is minuscule [148]. The sexual spectrum within this population cannot be generalized to biological males and females, who can be neatly categorized into two groups.

        1.2.5.2.5. Con:
        The bimodality of sex [149] supports the categorization of sex into three categories: male and female for the two modes, which constitute a majority, and a third option for the minority of individuals who do not belong to the two modal categories.

      1.2.5.3. Pro:

        1.2.5.3.1. Con:

      1.2.5.4. Pro:

        1.2.5.4.2. Pro:
        The capacity for females to give birth is a valued aspect of womanhood [155]. This creates a strong link between the biological capabilities of females to the idea of womanhood.

          1.2.5.4.2.1. Con:

          1.2.5.4.2.2. Pro:
          Childbirth is inextricably linked to lactation, which is by definition an act of nurturing another life. Therefore, it is not surprising that women are commonly regarded as more nurturing than men [157].

        1.2.5.4.3. Con:
        Sex-based reproductive roles have little to no implications for gendered behavior.

          1.2.5.4.3.1. Pro:

      1.2.5.5. Con:

        1.2.5.5.1. Pro:
        Gender is often self-identified [161], while sex is assigned [162] at birth by a doctor. As such, the two concepts are not linked.

        1.2.5.5.2. Con:

          1.2.5.5.2.2. Con:

            1.2.5.5.2.2.1. Pro:
            What is often labelled "gender socialization [165]" is actually socialization based on the sex of a child. Thus, children may grow up to adopt a gender that matches their sex, because it is consistent with their socialization experiences.

            1.2.5.5.2.2.2. Con:

            1.2.5.5.2.2.3. Con:
            Psychological variables rarely operate in isolation. Therefore, regardless of other possible influential factors, sex can be one of many statistically significant causal contributors to gender identity.

  1.6. Con:
  Gender exists on a spectrum and, therefore, cannot be restricted to only three categories.

        1.6.2.5.2. Con:
