Discussion Name:
Is Morality Objective?

1. Thesis:
Morality is objective.

  1.1. Pro:
  All major ethical systems indicate that morality is objective.

    1.1.1. Pro:
    Basic virtue/vice morality is virtually universal across time and culture. As per Aristotelian ethics [1], Courage>cowardice, honesty>dishonesty, charity>selfishness, kindness>cruelty, etc. This fact suggests an objective morality, the application of which, in various situations, will always require debate and reflection.

      1.1.1.1. Con:
      The study of virtue is notoriously conflicted by fundamental disagreements about what defines and delineates particular virtues, and the type of acts subsumed by each. We all talk about courage, perseverance, etc. but we often disagree about whether or not some action is an instance of some particular virtue.

    1.1.2. Pro:
    The non-aggression principle [2] is a necessary truth for sentient physical beings, and is therefore objective.

      1.1.2.1. Con:
      The non-aggression principle is voluntary, binding only to the individual adherent, and has proven to not be universal. Therefore, if the non-aggression principle has any ties to objective morality, it can only be as an effect, not as a cause.

    1.1.3. Con:
    Making any sort of broad sweeping across the board statement about right and wrong fails to take many variables into account. Giving a homeless man twenty dollars is charitable, but if you're a low income single parent with hungry children at home and you give away your last twenty dollars to this homeless person, you're literally taking food from children whom you are responsible for. In this case, charity is not the responsible action to take.

      1.1.3.1. Con:
      Charity is still considered an ethically good thing. If everyone did the same thing, the impoverished woman would be supported as would her children. And the point about responsibility doesn't discredit the morality of charity but it actually points to her lack of wisdom. These are two distinct virtues in virtue ethics.

    1.1.4. Pro:
    Across all societies and cultures you can identify a core set of virtues. These sort of archetypes of moral expression point to the deepest and most fundamental aspects of human perception. Given that we can say there are shared human psychologies, its obvious that there would be shared morals. Morality's objectivity derives from the mind.

      1.1.4.1. Con:
      It is logically illicit to derive an ought from an (anthropo-psychological) is.

      1.1.4.2. Con:
      38 USA states have laws about fetal homicide [3], and they differ on what constitutes fetal homicide, what age of development counts for fetal homicide and the remaining states do not have fetal homicide laws. This would suggest that what homicide is is not morally universal.

        1.1.4.2.1. Con:
        There are differences of what counts as homicide, but everyone agrees that homicide is a legal offense and violation of morality. People debate what constitutes homicide but all of that debate is rooted in the universal view or axiom that homicide is wrong. “Thou shall not kill” from the 10 commandments of Christianity, or the first precept of the Aṭṭāṅga-sīla of Buddhism “I undertake the precept of abstinance from killing”.

      1.1.4.3. Con:
      Not all states in the USA consider female-on-male [4] rape as rape. This would suggest that the very definition of rape as a crime is subject to interpenetration and is not universal.

        1.1.4.3.1. Con:
        Different interpretations of rape would suggest the universal importance of rape as a moral topic. Why are we trying to define rape as an ethical and legal violation? Because people believe that rape is immoral.

      1.1.4.4. Con:
      The similarity in human psychology is much better and more simply explained by similarities in biology and a shared evolutionary history (other organisms also exhibit similar psychologies to one another within a given species).

    1.1.5. Con:
    The framework of virtue ethics is so general that it makes no prescriptions itself. Rather, it depends on which virtues are used and emphasized. Different sets of virtues will produce different prescriptions, and there is no objective way to choose and emphasize the virtues. Thus, virtue ethics cannot be "objectively true".

    1.1.6. Con:
    Applying true or false statements to a system of ethics is a category error [5].

    1.1.7. Con:
    The underlying principle behind Virtue Ethics involves determining what individuals admire in others. It is inherently subjective.

      1.1.7.1. Con:
      Virtue ethics is an attempt to describe the efficacy of a thing in it's simplest form. A good table holds objects off the floor. A bad table cannot hold objects off the floor. The best table would be subjective, but virtue ethics doesn't deal in "bests" or "worsts."

        1.1.7.1.1. Con:
        We make tables to hold things off the floor. That is, we (subjectively) want something to hold things off the floor. So we get or make a table, and the table is "good" inasmuch as it's serves out need for the table, namely, holding things off the floor. The table is not "objectively good". It objectively holds things off the floor, but designating this "good" is subjective.

    1.1.8. Pro:
    Virtue ethics is a logical, practical way to discern and prescribe behaviors that are objectively good.

      1.1.8.1. Con:
      -> See 1.1.7.

      1.1.8.2. Con:
      The definition of virtues have changed over time, making virtue ethics a difficult tool to use as a practical guide for making moral decisions.

        1.1.8.2.1. Con:
        Evolution (the reason for the change of virtues) is too slow to cause tangible practical difficulties for making moral decisions.

          1.1.8.2.1.1. Con:
          Moral intuitions are not only shaped by genes, but also by external factors such as social and cultural environments, even financial situations.

            1.1.8.2.1.1.1. Con:
            The presence of SOME subjective moral position is not indicative that all morals are subjective.

      1.1.8.3. Con:
      Virtues themselves are subjective and the objectivity can also be found in their context if these are specified. For example, one could ask 'which course of action would be most consistent for maximizing dignity, wisdom and justice' in an individual's problem context which could to some extent be objective but still subjective in regards to broader morality or in such a case 'the most moral course of action'.

    1.1.9. Con:
    Ethical systems [6] are developed by individuals or cultures. As such, they are, definitionally, subjective.

      1.1.9.1. Pro:
      Bushido, an ancient Japanese ethical system [7] stresses obedience and duty as being supremely important [8], whereas Spartan Agoge ethics saw mindless obedience as unethical [9].

    1.1.10. Pro:
    Kant's first formulation of categorical imperatives [10] allows us to objectively determine the morality of a given action.

      1.1.10.1. Con:
      Under Categorical Imperatives, when the Nazis come to your house where you are hiding Anne Frank you must tell them you are hiding her if they ask.

        1.1.10.1.1. Con:
        Under the categorical imperative we cannot universalise lying, but we may well be able to universalise defence of a weaker party against the unjust.

      1.1.10.2. Con:
      It seems implausible that there is always one correct decision to do under all situations possible.

        1.1.10.2.1. Con:
        Objective morality does not mandate singular actions. Just as two moves may be equivalently strategic in chess, so too can two actions be equivalently moral with objective morality.

      1.1.10.3. Con:
      The categorical imperative (CI) is only one rule. There are other potential rules one could follow instead. That one ought to follow the CI instead of a different rule is itself a rule that is beyond the CI to demand. Thus, the CI is not a sufficient basis for normativity, as it requires some normative rule to demand the application of the CI instead of an alternative.

      1.1.10.4. Pro:
      Philosophical arguments provided by Christine Korsgaard [11] support a constructivist [12] deontological theory of ethics.

        1.1.10.4.1. Pro:
        (1) To rationally choose to do X, you must regard doing X as good. (2) You cannot regard doing X as good in itself, but can only regard doing X as good because it satisfies your needs, desires, inclinations, etc. (3) You cannot regard your desiring or needing to do X as making it good unless you regard yourself as valuable. (4) Therefore, you must regard yourself as valuable, if you are to make any rational choice. (Korsgaard 1996: 122 [13])

          1.1.10.4.1.1. Pro:
          To rationally choose to eat this piece of chocolate cake, I must think that eating the cake is good... It seems implausible to say that eating the cake is good in itself... It also seems implausible to say that it is good just because it satisfies a desire as such: for even if I was bulimic it might do that, but still not be regarded as good. A third suggestion, then, is that it can be seen as good because it is good for me... But if I think this, I must value myself...

            1.1.10.4.1.1.1. Pro:
            Put conversely: suppose that you thought that you and your life were utterly... [without value]. And suppose that you are faced with a piece of cake... It seems unlikely that there is something intrinsically good about eating it, or that you should do so just because you find yourself with a desire... It seems that the only reason to do so would be if you thought eating the cake brought you [benefit]- but if you thought your life was worthless, how could you see this as a reason either?

        1.1.10.4.2. Pro:
        Korsgaard begins by arguing that you must value yourself qua rational agent, if you are to make any rational choice.

          1.1.10.4.2.1. Con:
          Valuing oneself is insufficient to determine a comprehensive moral system, as many incompatible moral systems all assign value to oneself.

          1.1.10.4.2.2. Con:
          It is impossible not to value oneself as an agent, inasmuch as one cannot choose not to choose.

          1.1.10.4.2.3. Pro:
          You must value yourself qua rational agent, if you are to make any rational choice. Unless you value yourself qua rational agent, you cannot regard your leading a rationally structured life as valuable. Unless you regard your leading a rationally structured life as valuable, you cannot regard reasons and values as important . Unless you think your having a life containing reasons and values is important, you cannot see having any particular practical identity as valuable. (e.g. being rich).

            1.1.10.4.2.3.1. Pro:
            You cannot regard your identity as making doing X the rational thing to do unless you can see some value in that identity. You cannot see any value in any identity as such, but can regard it as valuable only because of the contribution it makes to giving you reasons and values by which to live.

              1.1.10.4.2.3.1.1. Pro:
              To rationally choose to X, you must take it that doing X is a rational thing.

              1.1.10.4.2.3.1.2. Pro:
              Since there's no reason in itself to X, you can take it that X is the rational thing to do iff you regard your identity as making X a rational thing to do.

    1.1.11. Pro:
    A utilitarian [14] approach to morality - the principle that the maximization of well-being is good - is correct and objective.

      1.1.11.1. Pro:
      All notions of value will bear some relationship to the actual or potential experience of conscious beings.

        1.1.11.1.1. Con:
        Bearing relation does not indubitably prescribe objectivity. These relations fluctuate enormously, ascribing further doubt to their objectivity.

      1.1.11.2. Pro:
      It is good to avoid behaving in such a way as to produce the worst possible misery for everyone.

        1.1.11.2.1. Pro:
        A state of the universe in which everyone suffers as much as he or she (or it) possibly can would be bad.

        1.1.11.2.2. Pro:
        A universe in which all conscious beings suffer the worst possible misery is worse than a universe in which they experience well-being.

        1.1.11.2.3. Con:
        This makes a value judgement, which undercuts the notion that utilitarian morality is objective.

        1.1.11.2.4. Con:
        It is possible to imagine a being which desires the worst possible misery for everyone, such as a supernatural demon, or an AGI with a minus sign on the intended utility function, and there is no objective basis on which to call their preferences incorrect.

      1.1.11.3. Con:
      A utilitarian approach may wall be objective if one accepts the axiom that wellbeing is the core of morality, but there is no objective reason to accept this axiom as true, therefore the objective approach of utilitarianism is not sufficient to prove the existence of objective morality. Utilitarians themselves do not necessarily believe in objective morality.

        1.1.11.3.1. Con:
        This critique applies to every moral system; at the highest meta-level, a subjective choice to behave along some set of principles must be made. This isn't a critique of utilitarianism specifically; this is a restatement of the Is/Ought Problem.

      1.1.11.4. Pro:
      The very definition of morality is human well-being. Nothing else can be a reasonable foundation.

        1.1.11.4.1. Pro:
        Contradicting well-being as the foundation of morality is like claiming that chemistry is only objective once we have subjectively agreed that chemicals and their reactions should be the foundation. Clearly, chemicals and their reactions are the very definition of the study of chemistry. Similarly, well-being is the fundamental unit of interest in the study of morality.

      1.1.11.5. Pro:
      Morality is about values. Values are a type of fact about the well-being of conscious creatures. The well-being of conscious creatures is discoverable by science. Scientific facts are objective. -Sam Harris, The Moral Landscape [15]

        1.1.11.5.1. Pro:
        Morality is the result of objective values which are chosen or discarded by the subject(s) with the hope of better operating in the physical present and future. Therefore, having formed as a response to objective realities, Morality is Objective.

          1.1.11.5.1.1. Con:
          Morality, being derived from (a subjective) consience, is subjective.

            1.1.11.5.1.1.1. Con:
            By this reasoning one should ignore all instincts, both the constructive and the destructive, which leads invariably to evolutionary dysfunction.

        1.1.11.5.2. Con:
        Sam Harris draws a false equivalence between 'facts' and 'values'.

          1.1.11.5.2.1. Pro:
          Morality concerns values, but this linear method of thinking equates the objectivity of scientific facts to the objectivity of morality. Values are an element of emotion. Emotion is abstract and immeasurable. So, any derivative of it, including morality, is also immeasurable and not objective.

          1.1.11.5.2.2. Pro:
          As David Hume pointed out, what "it is" (facts) isn't immediately followed by what "it should be" (moral values), without additional reasoning.

            1.1.11.5.2.2.1. Pro:
            If this were not the case, dystopias such as the one in Huxley's "Brave New World", would be morally accepted because they would cause the most happiness and well-being for the most people.

          1.1.11.5.2.3. Pro:
          Facts cannot contradict each other, and are certain. Values are uncertain and can vary between persons. Values are not facts.

            1.1.11.5.2.3.1. Con:
            Claims about moral facts can contradict each other. The same holds for claims about empirical facts. This does not mean that there is no truth in the matter.

            1.1.11.5.2.3.2. Con:
            One can be uncertain about what counts as a fact. Also, certainty is an epistemic category, which needs conscious beings. But facts are those which do not need conscious beings. The speed an asteroid goes is a fact even though we are uncertain about it, and even if no one existed to discern it.

        1.1.11.5.3. Con:
        Values are not any sort of fact. There is a complete distinction between facts and values. What leads to the increased well-being of conscious creatures may be discoverable by science, but that well-being itself is "good" is not: that is a value that has to be assumed.

          1.1.11.5.3.1. Pro:
          If morality is about values, and values are totally distinct from facts, and only facts can be objective, then morality cannot be objective.

        1.1.11.5.4. Con:
        There is no scientific approach that can determine if we should value well-being.

          1.1.11.5.4.1. Con:
          The demand for radical justification cannot be met by any branch of science.

            1.1.11.5.4.1.1. Pro:
            All science rests, eventually, on some assumption that is not scientifically verifiable - for example, the assumption that the external world really exists.

          1.1.11.5.4.2. Pro:
          We cannot scientifically discover moral truths. Science discovers descriptive facts about the world (facts about what "is"), and it is impossible to move from facts about what "is" to facts about what "ought to be".

            1.1.11.5.4.2.1. Pro:
            The goal of science is to understand the processes at work in the universe. We cannot justify this goal scientifically.

            1.1.11.5.4.2.2. Con:
            To the degree that it is in our power to produce the worst possible misery for everyone in this universe, we can say that if we don’t want everyone to experience the worst possible misery, we shouldn’t do X.

              1.1.11.5.4.2.2.1. Con:
              By making the goal of morality extinguishing misery for most people, many problematic and immoral events and decisions could be justified.

                1.1.11.5.4.2.2.1.1. Pro:
                Extinguishing the life of a minority of people to allow the majority to thrive would be justified.

                1.1.11.5.4.2.2.1.2. Pro:
                Extinguishing all life may be justifiable by this logic, as all life involves suffering, and the only way to not experience suffering is to not live.

              1.1.11.5.4.2.2.2. Pro:
              -> See 1.1.11.2.

              1.1.11.5.4.2.2.3. Con:
              This is an example of a subjective reason (a reason that is based on the fact that we want something), rather than an objective reason (a reason which holds whether or not we care about it).

          1.1.11.5.4.3. Pro:
          It is conceptually possible for anyone to value anything. Even if the only thing that someone valued was the number of blades of grass they had stepped on, there is no way we could scientifically prove to them that they are wrong about what to value.

            1.1.11.5.4.3.1. Con:
            If it doesn't pertain to well-being, in any way, shape or form, then it's amoral, as in it doesn't have anything to do with morality.

            1.1.11.5.4.3.2. Con:
            Maximizing the well-being of conscious creatures is the only thing we can reasonably value.

              1.1.11.5.4.3.2.1. Con:
              It is reasonable for one to value the wellbeing of only some set of conscious beings, such as "humans", or "those I care about", or "myself".

                1.1.11.5.4.3.2.1.1. Con:
                Drawing such distinctions is arbitrary and hence unjustified. Valuing all conscious entities makes no arbitrary distinctions and is thus not unjustified in the same way, and is thus rationally preferable.

                  1.1.11.5.4.3.2.1.1.1. Con:
                  The self-other distinction is psychologically fundamental and as deeply seated as the idea that other beings can be conscious in the first place; it is thus the furthest thing from "arbitrary".

                  1.1.11.5.4.3.2.1.1.2. Con:
                  "Those I care about" is a group defined by whose wellbeing one cares about, and that one with a consequentialist perspective would choose to maximise the wellbeing of that group follows naturally. It is not at all arbitrary.

                  1.1.11.5.4.3.2.1.1.3. Con:
                  The preference to avoid "arbitrary" distinctions is itself arbitrary here.

              1.1.11.5.4.3.2.2. Con:
              Some people reasonably value adherence to the Will of God, without regard to the wellbeing of anyone or even against their wellbeing (e.g. fasting).

                1.1.11.5.4.3.2.2.1. Con:
                Religious or spiritual fulfillment is an aspect of wellbeing.

                1.1.11.5.4.3.2.2.2. Con:
                Belief in a god is inherently unreasonable, as no gods have ever been proven to exist.

                1.1.11.5.4.3.2.2.3. Con:
                Fasting has several health benefits [16], not least of which being weight loss [17] in the overweight.

              1.1.11.5.4.3.2.3. Con:
              We value art, and an artist might care little for their well being in order to fully dedicate themselves to their art.

                1.1.11.5.4.3.2.3.1. Con:
                If it truly harms the artist's wellbeing, and doesn't have a concomitant redeeming factor, then under this paradigm this kind of act is immoral.

              1.1.11.5.4.3.2.4. Con:
              Some people value the wellbeing of abstractions, like "humanity" or "the environment", in ways that are not completely reducible to the well-being of creatures.

                1.1.11.5.4.3.2.4.1. Con:
                The wellbeing of 'humanity' is subsumed by caring about the wellbeing of creatures (so long as you consider humans to be creatures).

                1.1.11.5.4.3.2.4.2. Con:
                Valuing the wellbeing of abstractions can only be done by a conscious creature. Actions that affect the abstraction also affect the creature that values it.

                  1.1.11.5.4.3.2.4.2.1. Pro:
                  If someone values the environment and someone else does something to harm the environment, then that action hurts the person who values the environment.

                  1.1.11.5.4.3.2.4.2.2. Pro:
                  E.g. There is a star out there that is exploding right now, and no one knows or cares about it. It doesn't care about itself either, it has no capacity to do so. If that star turned out to be our own, however, we would definitely care, and the wellbeing of our own start would become valuable because of the creatures that rely on it.

              1.1.11.5.4.3.2.5. Pro:
              There is no alternative source of value beyond the wellbeing, or potential wellbeing, of conscious creatures. All other philosophical efforts to describe morality in terms of duty, fairness, justice, or some other principle indirectly draw upon wellbeing.

                1.1.11.5.4.3.2.5.1. Con:
                This assumes that conscious creatures are inherently valuable. This is not self-evident. It is beneficial to the survival of conscious creatures to value their well-being, but that does not make our well being the only conceivable source of value in existence.

                  1.1.11.5.4.3.2.5.1.1. Con:
                  The inherent value of conscious life is widely regarded as self-evident [18].

          1.1.11.5.4.4. Pro:
          Not all values have to do with the well-being of conscious creatures. It is possible to, and some do, value things that are not directly related to anyone's well-being.

            1.1.11.5.4.4.1. Con:
            Values (judgements on goodness or badness) are only relevant to conscious (capable of experience/preference) beings.

            1.1.11.5.4.4.2. Con:
            If one values something it is obviously directly related to one's (perceived and possibly ill-perceived) well-being, otherwise they wouldn't value it.

              1.1.11.5.4.4.2.1. Con:
              One can value the wellbeing of others.

                1.1.11.5.4.4.2.1.1. Con:
                Selfless acts can be ends on themselves as they offer pleasure or satisfaction as reward to the doer.

                  1.1.11.5.4.4.2.1.1.1. Pro:
                  This is called instrumental kindness. And the same reward centers are activated in the brain when someone acts out of instrumental kindness as true altruism. [19]

          1.1.11.5.4.5. Pro:
          If someone does not care about either their own well-being or that of others, there is no way to argue that they are wrong from the point of view of science.

            1.1.11.5.4.5.1. Pro:
            Just because science can measure objective facts such as the well-being of people or creatures or that we can help or do good things for others, it does not necessarily provide objective truths as to whether we should care for others and do good things. We can conjur our moral values from objective facts or truths, but ultimately they are subjective by nature.

            1.1.11.5.4.5.2. Con:
            There are several arguments, made over the course of the last 3,000 years, that egoism [20] is false or incoherent or otherwise self-defeating. The claim that there is literally no way to argue against egoism is an argument from ignorance.

          1.1.11.5.4.6. Con:
          Well-being is necessary for our survival and consciousness, and therefore is pragmatically valuable.

            1.1.11.5.4.6.1. Con:
            There is not objective value to our survival and consciousness.

              1.1.11.5.4.6.1.1. Pro:
              The universe will be just as well regardless of whether humans live on a planet in it.

        1.1.11.5.5. Con:
        Even if some concepts about morality could be based on science or facts we should remember that science works with dynamic paradigms and it could change with time.

          1.1.11.5.5.1. Con:
          Dynamic paradigms in science don't necessarily conflict with objectivity of science or objectivity of morality. The scientific method is a way to approximate reality. Observable, reproduceable facts don't change. Interpretation of these facts may change, but with every change scientists get closer to reality.

        1.1.11.5.6. Con:
        Wellbeing is a subjective value in itself. It might be the highest common denominator, but defining morality by refering to wellbeing is inherently subjective.

          1.1.11.5.6.1. Con:

          1.1.11.5.6.2. Pro:
          The standards for wellbeing are subjective. Even if basing morality on wellbeing were objective, conclusions would still be subjective.

            1.1.11.5.6.2.1. Con:

Objective in this case must mean: universally applicable (to all people in similar circumstances) to the best of our understanding (at that point in time), but since our understanding improves, so should our moral intuitions.

              1.1.11.5.6.2.1.1. Con:

                1.1.11.5.6.2.1.1.1. Pro:

          1.1.11.5.6.3. Pro:

        1.1.11.5.7. Pro:

        1.1.11.5.8. Pro:
