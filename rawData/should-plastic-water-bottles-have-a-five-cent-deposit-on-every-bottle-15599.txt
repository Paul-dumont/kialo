Discussion Name:
Should plastic water bottles have a five-cent deposit on every bottle?

1. Thesis:
Plastic water bottles should have a five-cent deposit on every bottle.

  1.1. Pro:
  A five-cent deposit would increase the percentage of plastic bottles recycled and thus reduce plastic pollution.

    1.1.1. Pro:
    A deposit on water bottles helps to drive a circular economy by making plastic bottle recycling simple and rewarding [1].

      1.1.1.1. Pro:
      Citizens can generate an extra income by collecting plastic bottles.

        1.1.1.1.1. Con:
        This takes away an incentive from the government and society as a whole to help poor people to escape poverty. After all, it can always be pointed out that they can survive on collecting bottles and returning them.

        1.1.1.1.2. Pro:
        In addition, this helps to keep poor citizens busy collecting bottles. This is a better solution than having them sitting around idle which is physically and mentally unhealthy.

      1.1.1.2. Con:
      Plastic bottle recycling is only made easier for local customer; since it becomes impossible for customer-on-the-go.

    1.1.2. Con:
    Instead of focusing on plastic recycling, plastic production should be reduced [2] to solve the plastic pollution problem.

      1.1.2.1. Pro:
      Plastic production generates large volumes of greenhouse gas emissions [3] and perpetuates plastic pollution.

      1.1.2.2. Con:
      Since plastic has infiltrated every facet of our lives, swapping out plastic packaging would have knock-on environmental effects [4] and might not be beneficial for the environment.

    1.1.3. Pro:
    Having a five-cent deposit on a water bottle gives the empty water bottle a monetary value [5]. If something has a monetary value, it is unlikely to be discarded.

      1.1.3.1. Con:
      Five cent is not much, only the poorest people will mind about that money.

        1.1.3.1.1. Pro:
        Five cent deposit won't be likely to create an impact in household's economies.

        1.1.3.1.2. Con:
        The monetary value of the water bottle is not going to be the sole motivation for recycling for most people. People must still have a common desire to do the right thing when it comes to environmental sustainability [6].

      1.1.3.2. Pro:
      Giving the water bottles monetary value will keep the bottles out of the environment and into the recycling facility where they belong.

    1.1.4. Con:
    There are better policies and practices than a refundable deposit to encourage plastics' recycling.

      1.1.4.1. Con:
      Policies might be more efficient to hold business responsible, however, a refundable deposit system has the ability of directly incentivizing responsible consumer behavior [7] by creating a tangible benefit for people to return their plastic products for recycling.

      1.1.4.2. Pro:
      The EU Packaging and Packaging Waste Directive (PPWD) [8] has set out targets for recycling packaging waste for 2025 and 2030.

        1.1.4.2.1. Con:
        European plastics producers have been pushing for a mandatory EU recycled content target for plastics packaging of 30% by 2030 since they believe final targets for recycled plastics should be set collaboratively with the institutions and the value chain partners. [9]

        1.1.4.2.2. Pro:
        According to this directive, at least 65% of all packaging waste must be recycled by 31 December, 2025, and at least 70% by 31, December 2030 [10].

    1.1.5. Pro:
    Deposit refund schemes (DRS) [11] to incentivize consumers to recycle plastic beverage bottles have positively reduced the amount of plastic bottles recycled in some countries.

      1.1.5.1. Con:
      There's still a divide and a big gap between countries in sustainable practices regarding DRS [12], which won't help with worldwide sustainability.

      1.1.5.2. Pro:
      In South Australia, a plastic container deposit scheme involving a refundable deposit of AUD 0.10resulted in a threefold decrease in the quantity of beverage containers becoming litter on beaches [13].

      1.1.5.3. Con:
      Some of the most polluting countries [14], such as the United States, lack of federal regulations regarding bottle refund programs.

        1.1.5.3.1. Pro:
        In 2016, the United States generated the largest amount of plastic waste of any country in the world (42.0 Mt). [15]

        1.1.5.3.2. Con:
        Numerous US states have implemented what are commonly known as 'bottle bills [16]'.

          1.1.5.3.2.1. Con:
          Many other states such as Illinois, have unsuccessfully tried several times over the past decade to adopt a bottle bill [17], but the opposition from retailers, distributors and some recyclers has been stronger.

          1.1.5.3.2.2. Pro:
          In California, the California Redemption Value (CRV) stands at USD 0.05 [18] for containers under 0.7 liters and USD 0.10 for larger ones.

            1.1.5.3.2.2.1. Pro:
            Since its inception in 1987, it is approximated that around 300 billion beverage containers made of aluminum, glass, and plastic have been successfully recycled [19].

            1.1.5.3.2.2.2. Con:
            The California Redemption Value is struggling since in 2022 almost half of its 2,448 certified beverage container redemption sites had gone out of business over the past decade. [20]

      1.1.5.4. Pro:
      In 2001, Israel implemented a deposit refund legislation, introducing a deposit of ILS 0.25 on glass and plastic containers under 1.5 liters [21]. The law yielded positive outcomes, with overall benefits surpassing total costs by approximately 35%. (p.32)

      1.1.5.5. Pro:
      Ecuador saw a notable upswing in PET bottle recycling due to a refundable deposit of USD 0.02 per PET beverage bottle [22], established in 2011. Recycling rates escalated from 30% in 2011 to 80% in 2012. (p.32)

    1.1.6. Con:
    Not all types of plastic can be recycled.

      1.1.6.1. Pro:
      Non-recyclable plastics [23] include cling film, blister packaging, plastic-coated wrapping paper, composite plastic, bioplastics, and polycarbonate.

      1.1.6.2. Con:
      It is estimated that about 75% of global plastics produced are thermoplastics and can therefore, be recycled [24].

  1.2. Pro:
  A five-cent deposit would create a disincentive for purchasing plastic products.

    1.2.1. Con:
    In societies with a prevalent disposable culture [25], consumers might prioritize convenience over the deposit cost.

      1.2.1.1. Pro:
      Consumers might perceive this deposit as insignificant and not substantial enough to deter them from purchasing the product, especially since water is a basic necessity. [26]

      1.2.1.2. Con:
      Reusable water bottles are increasingly becoming popular [27], which shows the willingness to avoid throw-away plastic bottles.

    1.2.2. Con:
    Unless similar alternatives to plastic bottles are available, a disincentive to purchase plastic products is unlikely to happen.

      1.2.2.1. Pro:
      Retailers and producers should be disincentive to use plastic packaging in their products.

      1.2.2.2. Con:
      There's been an increase in plastic packaging avoidance around the world [28].

    1.2.3. Pro:
    This added cost at the time of purchase serves as a financial deterrent and as a type of "penalty", making plastic products relatively less attractive compared to alternatives.

      1.2.3.1. Con:
      Eco-friendly, plastic-free products tend to be more expensive [29] than plastic-based products, therefore the "penalty" can be found in both alternatives.

      1.2.3.2. Pro:
      The principle of a five-cent deposit relies on the idea that the individuals or entities responsible for generating pollution should shoulder the expenses of its control in order to avert harm to both human health and the environment. [30]

    1.2.4. Pro:
    Consumers might actually appreciate this incentive as it rewards behavior that is good for society and environment [31].

      1.2.4.1. Pro:
      Other recycling incentive schemes such as the one in Hong Kong or in Southern England [32] have worked really well.

      1.2.4.2. Con:
      Discounts and deals [33], instead of an added tax would be better to incentive consumers to recycle.

  1.3. Pro:
  The funds collected could be used to address environmental problems.

    1.3.1. Pro:
    The funds could support projects aimed at restoring and preserving natural ecosystems affected by plastic pollution. [34]

      1.3.1.1. Con:
      Unless citizens can also participate in choosing which projects are chosen and in the follow-up, this solution could lead to a lack of transparency and corruption.

      1.3.1.2. Pro:
      The UN has targeted many different interesting projects with the aim of restoring the ecosystems [35].

      1.3.1.3. Pro:
      Restoring the ecosystem could be one of the best solutions to climate change and plastic pollution since it halts pollution, reduces waste, and revives vegetation above and below the surface [36].

    1.3.2. Pro:
    The collected deposits could also be used to subsidize businesses that adopt more sustainable practices [37] which would incentivize businesses to reduce their use of single-use plastic bottles.

      1.3.2.1. Con:
      Subsidies for business that decide to go green [38] already exist all over the world.

      1.3.2.2. Pro:
      Big corporations are still being given money to produce cheap plastic [39], therefore businesses should be fined for using plastic and rewarded for choosing alternatives to single-use plastic.

    1.3.3. Pro:
    Investing in research and innovation could lead to the development of new, eco-friendly materials to replace water bottles, [40] as well as more efficient recycling processes.

      1.3.3.1. Pro:
      Funds could be directed towards research grants for scientists and entrepreneurs working on sustainable solutions for packaging and waste management. [41]

      1.3.3.2. Con:
      There are already alternative eco-friendly materials to plastic [42].

    1.3.4. Con:
    Industries involved in producing single-use plastic bottles might resist the implementation of a deposit system by lobbying efforts to undermine or delay its enforcement [43].

      1.3.4.1. Pro:
      Plastic lobby has been delaying disposable plastic directives. [44]

      1.3.4.2. Con:
      Many new directives are banning the use of single-use plastic items [45], such as the EU’s Directive on single-use plastics [46].

    1.3.5. Con:
    Relying on funds collected through a deposit system is not a sustainable solution in the long run. True environmental progress requires systemic changes. [47]

      1.3.5.1. Pro:
      Plastic production and distribution should be completely banned [48] to avoid any further plastic pollution.

      1.3.5.2. Con:
      Any solution that increases recycling and deals with plastic waste is better than not doing anything.

    1.3.6. Con:
    If consumers start to view the deposit as a source of funds, they might be less inclined to donate to charitable environmental causes. [49]

      1.3.6.1. Con:
      Money doesn't fix all the problems [50] and it is probably preferable to improve consumer's recycling habits.

      1.3.6.2. Pro:
      If consumers feel they are participating to strengthen the corporate social responsibility (CSR) [51] of a retailer, especially if the retailer is local [52], they might not extend their support somewhere else.

    1.3.7. Con:
    The introduction of a deposit system could incentivize counterfeit operations, where individuals might produce counterfeit water bottles to claim deposits, leading to fraud and further environmental damage.

      1.3.7.1. Con:
      Measures such as assigning specific bar codes to recycled bottles [53] have already be put into place to avoid fraudulent actions.

      1.3.7.2. Pro:
      They've already been cases of fraud related to plastic DRS, such as the case of the alleged fraudster that made €1m by 'recycling' bottles [54].

    1.3.8. Pro:
    The deposit collected could fund water bottle refill stations [55] at public places, encouraging people to use reusable water bottles instead of disposable ones.

      1.3.8.1. Pro:
      Water bottle refill stations can directly improve children's health [56].

      1.3.8.2. Pro:
      Water bottle refill stations are an effective way to stop plastic bottle pollution [57].

      1.3.8.3. Con:
      Since providing clean water is an essential service, governments should be responsible for funding water bottle refill stations without having to had an extra tax to citizens [58].

  1.4. Con:
  It unfairly shifts the responsibility of covering the expenses related to environmental pollution onto consumers.

    1.4.1. Con:
    Some existing programs, such as the "extended producer responsibility" (EPR) [59] require companies to cover the costs of recycling programs.

      1.4.1.1. Con:
      EPR bills don't incorporate specific reduction requirements and standards for recycled content to avoid “unsustainable waste disposal [60].”

      1.4.1.2. Pro:
      This would encourage companies to prioritize non plastic materials if they don't want to cover the costs of recycling programs.

    1.4.2. Pro:
    A deposit refund scheme (DRS) on plastic bottles is a type of tax that may punish those who are already recycling [61].

      1.4.2.1. Pro:
      It is estimated than 32% of the global population currently recycles. [62]

      1.4.2.2. Con:
      It is considered that 91% of plastic isn't recycled [63]; therefore not many people would feel punished.

    1.4.3. Pro:
    DRS encourages individual recycling, but it doesn't directly address the root cause of the production and disposal of single-use plastic bottles by manufacturers. [64]

      1.4.3.1. Pro:
      It is estimated that only 20 companies are accountable for manufacturing more than half of the disposable single-use plastic that eventually becomes global waste [65].

      1.4.3.2. Con:
      DRS does encourage accountability in retailers' actions since they will be responsible for setting systems to recycle the bottles returned so they can eventually re-circulate.

    1.4.4. Con:
    Plastic waste management responsibility is shared between consumers and producers.

      1.4.4.1. Pro:
      In Europe, consumers generate approximately 40% of plastic waste while producers are responsible for the remaining 60% [66].

      1.4.4.2. Con:
      Producers should be hold accountable for their plastic products and packaging’s end-of-life impacts [67].

    1.4.5. Pro:
    Having a 5-cent deposit on bottles puts the burden of environmental behavior on consumers while adding less pressure on companies to use more environmental alternatives to plastic bottles.

      1.4.5.1. Con:
      Environmental taxation is an effective way to give the flexibility to consumers and businesses to determine how best to reduce their environmental “footprint” [68]. (p.1)

      1.4.5.2. Pro:
      By adding an environmental taxation to the purchase of plastic, it falsely targets the blame for the problematic plastic pollution on the consumers [69].

    1.4.6. Con:
    As people would be encouraged to care more about the environment with this responsibility, they'd be incentivised to put pressure on companies to be more environmentally friendly.

      1.4.6.1. Con:
      Assuming that citizens need an environmental tax in order to care more about the environment is patronizing and falls under paternalism [70].

        1.4.6.1.1. Pro:
        According to a global survey, 9 in 10 people support a plastic pollution treaty [71].

        1.4.6.1.2. Con:
        The earth is facing a major threat with climate change, [72] it is therefore fair to assume that citizens need to be pushed to care about the environment.

      1.4.6.2. Pro:
      Studies have shown that consumers are willing to pay more for sustainability [73].

  1.5. Con:
  This policy would impose an excessive load on retailers, demanding their time and financial resources.

    1.5.1. Con:
    A deposit system can foster consumer loyalty to specific retailers.

      1.5.1.1. Pro:
      Once customers have become used to how a retailer's recycling scheme works, they would be more likely to repeat. [74]

      1.5.1.2. Con:
      Chaos has also been linked to installing DRS in stores [75], which would have a negative effect on customer's loyalty.

    1.5.2. Con:
    Retailers that participate in environmentally friendly initiatives, such as a bottle deposit systems, can enhance their brand image and reputation.

      1.5.2.1. Con:
      This will only be possible for big retailers, since small retailers might be put out of business because of DRS. [76]

      1.5.2.2. Pro:
      Retailers directly benefit from increased sales and consumer foot traffic [77] as a result of a better reputation thanks to the implementation of DRS.

    1.5.3. Con:
    This policy can lead to an increased revenue growth for retailers.

      1.5.3.1. Pro:
      While retailers initially provide deposits to customers, they can often receive reimbursements from the system's governing body. Over time, the deposits collected can help offset the costs associated with managing the deposit system.

      1.5.3.2. Con:
      The handling fees paid to retailers by the central system for each returned DRS container fully cover the cost of handling and processing, resulting in no financial impact on the retailers [78]. (p.14)

    1.5.4. Pro:
    There is a cost the retailer [79] will incur to facilitate the return of the bottles within its retail locations.

      1.5.4.1. Con:
      Retailers would supposedly be able to sell the discarded bottles on, thereby making a profit after the initial investment into the necessary infrastructure.

      1.5.4.2. Pro:
      Bottle and can deposit return schemes were estimated to cost £1bn in the UK [80].

    1.5.5. Pro:
    Depending upon the number of water bottles returned, retailers may have to hire additional staff to manage the returned bottles.

      1.5.5.1. Con:
      Hiring additional staffs could economically activate local [81] communities.

      1.5.5.2. Pro:
      DRSs require 1.5 to 4.0 times more employees. [82] (p.2)

    1.5.6. Pro:
    The retailers may have to deal with sanitation issues [83] depending upon the condition of the water bottles when returned.

      1.5.6.1. Con:
      Bottles can easily be cleaned through an automated process. [84]

      1.5.6.2. Pro:
      Cleaning PET bottles requires the use of hot water [85]; retailers may need to increase their usage of water and electricity in order to respond to the increased demand of recycled bottles.

  1.6. Con:
  This method might not effectively tackle the actual issues of plastic pollution [86].

    1.6.1. Pro:
    Microplastics and plastic-derived chemicals have been found in drinking water, [87] seafood, and other food sources. There are concerns about potential health risks from consuming these particles and chemicals.

      1.6.1.1. Pro:
      According to research, chopping, shredding and washing of plastic in recycling facilities may turn as much as six to 13% of incoming waste into microplastics [88].

      1.6.1.2. Con:
      There are no relevant solutions to this day to fight the problem of microplastics. [89]

      1.6.1.3. Pro:
      It would be better to ban plastic-bottled water in countries where tap-water is readily available since prevention is always better than cure.

    1.6.2. Pro:
    Plastic pollution in oceans disrupts marine ecosystems and habitats. It affects coral reefs, seagrass beds [90], and other crucial habitats, leading to a decline in biodiversity and ecosystem health.

      1.6.2.1. Pro:
      Plastic pollution has now entered the marine food web [91] which can cause many negative consequences.

      1.6.2.2. Con:
      Recycling helps keep plastics out of the ocean and reduces the amount of “new” plastic in circulation [92].

    1.6.3. Con:
    By reusing existing plastic materials, we can conserve valuable natural resources and decrease the environmental impact of plastic production.

      1.6.3.1. Pro:
      The production of plastic relies on fossil fuels, contributing to resource depletion and greenhouse gas emissions. [93]

      1.6.3.2. Pro:
      The production of plastics generates greenhouse gas emissions, contributing to climate change [94]. Additionally, plastic pollution can affect the planet's ability to absorb carbon dioxide, as plastic debris can hinder the growth of carbon-absorbing vegetation.

      1.6.3.3. Pro:
      Reusing plastic rather than producing it from new resources each time, contributes to alleviating the pressure on fossil fuels like coal, natural gas, petroleum, and others [95].

      1.6.3.4. Con:
      Plastic cannot always be safely recycled so increasing the recycling of plastic can actually be more damaging to the planet. [96]

    1.6.4. Con:
    If consumers have to modify their behavior to collect and store plastic water bottles, the action could inspire many consumers to start recycling other items.

      1.6.4.1. Con:
      To the contrary, it might educate consumers to the effect that they do not recycle anything that does not come with a monetary reward.

      1.6.4.2. Pro:
      Deposit Return Systems (DRS) are considered to be an effective Instrument towards a Zero Waste Future. [97]
