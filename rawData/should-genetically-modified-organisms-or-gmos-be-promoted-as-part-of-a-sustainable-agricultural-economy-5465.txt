Discussion Name:
Should Genetically Modified Organisms (or GMOs) be promoted as part of a sustainable agricultural economy?

1. Thesis:
Genetically modified organisms (GMOs) should be promoted as part of a sustainable agricultural economy.

  1.1. Pro:
  GMOs can help address food shortages [1] and food insecurity [2].

    1.1.1. Pro:
    GMO crops can be nutritionally fortified [3] in order to reduce the incidence of malnutrition and attendant developmental disorders, saving and improving millions of lives.

      1.1.1.1. Pro:
      800 million [4] people in the world are undernourished, despite the fact that the current output of the world’s farms could supply over 11 billion people with 2,000 calories per day. Increasing the nutrient content of foods in impoverished regions would help to reduce this gross inequality.

      1.1.1.2. Con:
      The biofortification of GMO plants is an inefficient way to address nutritional deficiencies [5] compared to supplementation or fortification.

    1.1.2. Con:
    If GMOs trigger adverse health effects, then their potential as a long-term solution to food shortages will be limited.

      1.1.2.1. Con:
      GMOs do not have more adverse effects on people's health than normal organisms do.

      1.1.2.2. Pro:
      Despite receiving FDA approval, long-term studies on the effects of consuming GMO foods have not been adequately performed; there may be unknown health effects [6] associated with the technology that are not yet apparent.

    1.1.3. Pro:
    Since GMOs can be made more resistant to disease and climate, they offer a solution to the lack of food in certain parts of the world.

      1.1.3.1. Pro:
      In the absence of GMOs, humanity would become increasingly subject to climate-induced scarcity (p. 353 [7]).

        1.1.3.1.1. Pro:
        The climate is changing quickly. Although plants and animals will evolve to compensate, the organisms we rely on may not evolve quickly enough [8] to support the world's population. GMOs can be part of the solution.

      1.1.3.2. Pro:
      GMO strains resistant to heat, drought, and pollution can help to mitigate the effects of climate change and other environmental challenges (p. 353 [9]).

      1.1.3.3. Pro:
      GM crops can be made resistant to microbial infections, such as the potato blight that led to the Irish Potato Famine [10] in the 19th century.

      1.1.3.4. Con:
      While GMOs can be made more resistant to climate change and events, their ability to adapt is limited [11]; GMO crops only offer a short-term solution for this problem.

      1.1.3.5. Con:
      Given that GMO seed supplies are tightly controlled by a small number of companies, reliance on them creates a serious threat to food supplies in the event that a supplier's production is disrupted [12].

    1.1.4. Pro:
    GMOs can be grown using much less space than non-GMO crops, yielding a number of positive impacts.

      1.1.4.1. Pro:
      Genetic modifications such as photosynthesis improvements [13] could allow for bigger yields on the same land, this could give us a mixture of oppertunity to feed our hungry, leave room for nature and to not use certain harmfull substances.

      1.1.4.2. Con:
      Some GMO plants use soil nutrients differently [14] than conventional strains, which can have impacts on soil quality and the plants themselves.

    1.1.5. Con:
    The majority of food shortages and hunger are not caused by a lack of food or climate change, but rather economic, social, and political issues [15]; GMOs offer little opportunity to address such issues.

      1.1.5.1. Pro:
      The leading causes of food insecurity are conflict [16], conflict-related price increases, and conflict-related supply shortages.

      1.1.5.2. Con:
      Despite being unable to affect social and political causes of hunger, GMOs hold great potential to address hunger caused by climate or economic issues [17]. Even a partial solution to hunger is better than no solution at all.

  1.2. Pro:
  GMOs offer a number of economic benefits over wild-type (WT) organisms.

    1.2.1. Pro:
    GMOs can be used to create many highly useful non-food items.

      1.2.1.1. Pro:
      GMO crops, such as cotton [18], are widely used in the production of textiles.

      1.2.1.2. Pro:
      The wide availability of human insulin [19] over the past few decades is due to the use of GMO bacteria in insulin production.

        1.2.1.2.1. Pro:
        In the absence of insulin produced by GMO bacteria, the supply of human insulin would remain dependent on animal-sourced insulin, which carries risks of adverse effects [20] and supply shortages [21].

      1.2.1.3. Con:
      The potential dietary risks of GMOs are only one category of concern; non-food GMOs pose environmental and ecological risks as well.

    1.2.2. Pro:
    GMO crops are less expensive.

      1.2.2.1. Pro:
      The shelf life of GMO produce can be extended.

        1.2.2.1.1. Pro:
        Food that lasts longer on the shelf [22] is more attractive to retailers and reduces waste.

      1.2.2.2. Pro:
      GMO crops can be produced more efficiently, leading to lower prices.

        1.2.2.2.1. Pro:
        GMO plants produce a higher yield [23] than wild-type strains.

        1.2.2.2.2. Pro:
        GMO wheat can have grains that are 12.3% heavier [24], thereby producing more food from the same number of plants.

        1.2.2.2.3. Con:
        The techniques used to genetically modify many organisms, such as base editing and prime editing, are themselves inefficient and expensive [25].

      1.2.2.3. Con:
      GMO seeds cost more than conventional seeds; it is $80 more expensive [26] to raise an acre of GMO corn.

        1.2.2.3.1. Pro:
        For large-scale farms [27], this difference in cost can be substantial.

        1.2.2.3.2. Con:
        The parent claim only addresses seed cost and ignores the decreased need for pesticides and herbicides and differences in yield associated with producing GMOs.

    1.2.3. Pro:
    Since specific traits can be genetically conferred on GMOs, they can be made more attractive to consumers, improving sales.

      1.2.3.1. Pro:
      The appearance and texture of GMOs can be matched to consumer preferences.

        1.2.3.1.1. Pro:
        GMO Arctic apples are resistant to bruising [28] and discoloration.

        1.2.3.1.2. Con:
        Consumers who become conditioned to GMOs may come to hold unreasonable expectations about the appearance of produce and reject foods that do not appear like GMOs.

      1.2.3.2. Pro:
      The flavor of GMO foods can be improved.

        1.2.3.2.1. Pro:
        Sixty percent of testers preferred the taste of GMO tomatoes [29] over traditional ones.

        1.2.3.2.2. Con:
        In many cases, the modifications made to GMO foods do not affect the taste; many GMO foods taste the same [30] as their conventional counterparts.

      1.2.3.3. Con:
      Fifty-one percent of American consumers [31] believe that GMO foods are less healthful, which may limit the economic potential of genetically engineered products.

        1.2.3.3.1. Pro:
        Research on the effects of pro-GMO advertising has shown that despite holding more positive perceptions of GMOs, younger consumers remain reluctant to purchase them (p. 79 [32]).

      1.2.3.4. Con:
      Some research suggests that attractiveness and healthfulness may be perceived as opposing qualities in food [33] by many consumers.

    1.2.4. Con:
    The economic advantages of GMOs will benefit those of higher socioeconomic status more, and will therefore contribute to increased disparity [34].

      1.2.4.1. Pro:
      The higher capital investment required to produce GMO products is outside the means of many smaller producers, so the economic benefits of GMOs will primarily be realized by wealthy producers (p. 10 [35]).

        1.2.4.1.1. Pro:
        The cost of producing GMOs is increased by government regulations [36] on genetically modified crops.

      1.2.4.2. Con:
      GMO crops offer a number of distinct advantages [37] for smaller and less wealthy farms.

      1.2.4.3. Con:
      The lower food prices [38] offered by GMOs directly benefit those of lower socioeconomic status, for whom food constitutes a higher proportion of their budget.

      1.2.4.4. Pro:
      The lower price of GMOs could hurt smaller producers, who may be unable to scale up production to compensate.

    1.2.5. Con:
    The market for non-GMO products and seeds [39] is continuing to grow in Europe and Asia; non-GMO products may well turn out to be more profitable for farmers.

      1.2.5.1. Pro:
      The non-GMO food market is anticipated to grow by over $1 billion [40] between 2020 and 2025.

      1.2.5.2. Con:
      GMOs are the best option for many farmers in developing countries with hot and dry climates, who rely on GMOs to make a living where they would not otherwise be able.

        1.2.5.2.1. Pro:
        Over a five-year period, a total of 2.9 million farmers [41] in 13 African countries saw their yields increase by 20% to 30% after sowing drought-tolerant GMO seeds.

    1.2.6. Con:
    Because GMOs can be patented [42], farmers who use GMOs become economically dependent on seed companies.

      1.2.6.1. Con:
      This is not unique to farming; many businesses and industries are dependent on suppliers of raw materials.

      1.2.6.2. Con:
      This is also true for non-GMO seeds [43] in many parts of the world.

      1.2.6.3. Pro:
      GMO seed contracts do not allow farmers to save their seeds for use in planting future crops.

        1.2.6.3.1. Pro:
        Monsanto won a $23m lawsuit against farmers and small businesses [44] who saved GMO seeds for planting, highlighting the vulnerability of farmers to seed distributors.

          1.2.6.3.1.1. Con:
          The practice of suing is very marginal in the context of the overall market. Suing is done to enforce contract law and proceeds go to charity. Over decades of operation, selling seeds to over 300,000 farms per year, Monsanto have sued approximately 100 farmers and settled 700 cases out of court. [45]

        1.2.6.3.2. Con:
        Buying seeds is economically convenient for many kinds of cultivations, and producing seeds autonomously is not a widely spread practice in modern farming.

          1.2.6.3.2.1. Pro:
          Many farmers do not wish to save seeds [46] and would prefer the stability of a new seed each year, saving on management and cleaning costs, and benefitting from high performance hybrids.

        1.2.6.3.3. Con:
        Seed contracts cover a single annual purchase [47] and do not force farmers to renew; a farm can return to alternative crops or saved seeds if they choose.

          1.2.6.3.3.1. Pro:
          The high uptake of GE crops suggests that most farmers are not opposed to the terms of seed contracts.

      1.2.6.4. Pro:
      Large biotech companies have invested heavily in genetic patents, which are problematic insofar as they make no distinction [48] between naturally occurring and human-engineered genetic traits.

  1.3. Con:
  Consumption of GMOs is associated with a number of health risks.

    1.3.1. Con:
    Genetic modification has been in existence for millennia, and almost all the foods we eat today are far removed from their wild counterparts. It is thus illogical to fear GMOs.

      1.3.1.1. Pro:
      Even foods picked in the wild may be genetically modified, due to hybridization with GMO crops. This makes it nearly impossible to avoid GMO foods.

      1.3.1.2. Pro:
      The traditional methods of genetic modification are too random and have greater risks and costs.

      1.3.1.3. Con:
      Modern genetic modification techniques could have dramatically different side effects than traditional methods.

        1.3.1.3.1. Con:
        New technologies such as CRISPR [49] merely enhance the mechanism of mutagenesis, not the end result.

    1.3.2. Con:
    The perception of health risks from GMOs is due to a lack of understanding as to what the term "GMO" means; organisms that have been genetically modified by breeding and genetically engineered (GE) or transgenic organisms are distinct and must be considered independently.

      1.3.2.1. Con:
      Transgenic crops created via bacterial infection [50] have been discovered in nature.

      1.3.2.2. Pro:
      While GMOs can be created via pollination or other methods, GEs are made in labs; transgenic GE organisms are made using DNA from foreign organisms.

      1.3.2.3. Pro:
      Although the term is often used only in reference to modern genetic engineering methods, most foods today have been genetically modified by selective breeding.

    1.3.3. Pro:
    There are human health risks associated with the use of GMOs.

      1.3.3.1. Con:
      There is some human health risk associated with drinking water [51], yet the benefits of drinking it are clear; the same applies to GMOs.

        1.3.3.1.1. Con:
        The risks associated with water are well known; in the absence of long-term studies, GMOs carry the possibility of unknown risks that could involve rapid change.

        1.3.3.1.2. Pro:
        While certain genetic engineering techniques do carry potential risks, the consensus of authorities is that the post regulation [52] risks are equal to or lower [53] than those associated with with non-GMO food; no additional adverse impacts have been demonstrated.

      1.3.3.2. Con:
      Many studies that are used as evidence of the health risks of GMOs are not supported by mainstream science (p. 2 [54]).

        1.3.3.2.1. Con:
        The most common position among regulatory bodies, including the WHO [55], is that genetic engineering does have risks and should be regulated.

        1.3.3.2.2. Pro:
        The proponents of claims about GMO health risks include people with no expertise or training [56] and those with profit incentives [57] to dispute GMO safety.

          1.3.3.2.2.1. Con:
          The parent claim commits the genetic fallacy [58].

        1.3.3.2.3. Pro:
        After evaluating the available research literature, multiple [59] credible [60] authorities [61] have disagreed with the position that GMOs pose a health risk.

        1.3.3.2.4. Con:
        Some studies and whistleblowers have suggested that people and studies disputing the safety of GMO foods have been falsely discredited for political or financial purposes.

          1.3.3.2.4.1. Con:
          Domingo, (2011) is an oft-cited source for claims of adverse impacts outside of industry studies. A number of these studies, such as Seralini et. al. (2007) [62] and Malatesta et al. (2008) [63] have methodological problems, including failure to control with isogenic lines, inadequate sample size, and p-hacking.

            1.3.3.2.4.1.1. Pro:
            Other systematic reviews have reached different conclusions [64].

          1.3.3.2.4.2. Pro:
          Whistleblowers, such as Dr. Thierry Vrain [65], have warned that the health risks posed by GMOs have been falsely underreported.

            1.3.3.2.4.2.1. Con:
            Thierry Vrain [66]'s claims about flawed studies are not supported by evidence.

          1.3.3.2.4.3. Con:
          An analysis of industry bias in publications must be considered in the larger context of publications, where a significant proportion are non-industry aligned [67], and the results from industry and non-industry studies generally agree.

            1.3.3.2.4.3.1. Con:
            Industry bias in studies on the safety of hazardous products [68] has been well-documented.

            1.3.3.2.4.3.2. Pro:
            Large, systematic reviews and meta analyses in recent years confirm GMO safety from a weight-of-evidence perspective, including a decade of EU-funded research [69], and an overview of 10 years of studies [70].

            1.3.3.2.4.3.3. Pro:
            The majority of GMO studies are performed as part of the commercialization of crops, so any publication bias largely represents commercial reality rather than underhanded tactics [71]. Many such studies are compositional studies on the substantial equivalence of crops.

              1.3.3.2.4.3.3.1. Pro:
              These studies are part of the built-in, internal reviews within corporations to ensure that only successful products make it to late-stage assessment and publication.

      1.3.3.3. Con:
      GMOs are regulated by government bodies, such as the EFSA [72], and undergo extensive safety testing prior to commercialization. Risk assessments should consider the actual risk—post regulation and scrutiny—not the theoretical risk.

        1.3.3.3.1. Con:
        Genetic modification via other techniques, such as gene editing, is unregulated and could generate greater risks [73].

        1.3.3.3.2. Pro:
        Every GMO is unique, just as every species, so they go through the same process as normally occurring organisms.

        1.3.3.3.3. Pro:
        While genetic engineering methods can create novel risks, these are highly scrutinised, and the scientific consensus is that the post-regulation risks of GMOs are equal to or lower than their conventional counterparts.

      1.3.3.4. Pro:
      There is widespread concern that GMO foods will lead to an increase in allergies [74].

        1.3.3.4.1. Con:
        There is no evidence [75] that GMO foods cause more allergies than conventional foods.

        1.3.3.4.2. Pro:
        It is possible to create transgenic crops that incorporate the allergens [76] associated with a different organism.

          1.3.3.4.2.1. Con:
          This possibility is accounted for and actively tested [77] during the approval phase of GMO development.

      1.3.3.5. Pro:
      Eating GMO foods could lead to antibiotic-resistant bacteria [78].

        1.3.3.5.1. Pro:
        Research has shown that antibiotic-resistant genes from GMO foods [79] can be taken up by bacteria, allowing them to survive common wastewater treatments.

        1.3.3.5.2. Con:
        While studies acknowledge the possibility of antibiotic-resistant gene transfer, there are no plausible scenarios [80] in which this would produce new strains of antibiotic-resistant bacteria.

      1.3.3.6. Pro:
      The herbicides associated with GMOs, such as glyphosate, are a potential health concern for humans.

        1.3.3.6.1. Pro:
        Many pesticides carry serious health risks [81], so it is reasonable to investigate whether the herbicides that show increased usage with GMO adoption are creating new dietary health risks.

          1.3.3.6.1.1. Con:
          This is an argument in favor of reduced pesticide use rather than against the adoption of GMOs.

        1.3.3.6.2. Con:
        In developed economies, the rates of herbicide [82] and pesticide application are highly regulated, with limits set at levels that prevent residues from presenting dietary risks.

          1.3.3.6.2.1. Pro:
          Herbicide regulations are comprehensive frameworks [83] developed using low dosage and conservative risk modelling to ensure that trace levels of herbicides will not harm human health.

    1.3.4. Pro:
    There is an animal health risk attached to the use of GMOs.

      1.3.4.1. Con:
      This is not an issue for those who do not equate the value of animal life and the value of human life. For such people, if humans are not negatively affected, then there is not a valid reason to ban GMOs.

        1.3.4.1.1. Con:
        Animal consumption of GMOs leads to human consumption of GMOs via meat consumption.

      1.3.4.2. Pro:
      GMO consumption can cause cancer in animals.

        1.3.4.2.1. Pro:
        Rats that were fed diets of different GMO varieties showed symptoms of hepatorenal toxicity consistent with carcinogenesis [84].

          1.3.4.2.1.1. Con:
          The researchers noted that the observed effects could be attributable to pesticide residues [85] rather than the GMO food itself.

        1.3.4.2.2. Con:
        An analysis of trends since the introduction of GMO crops provides no evidence of increased cancer or other health risks [86] associated with their consumption.

        1.3.4.2.3. Con:
        This study was later retracted [87].

      1.3.4.3. Pro:
      It is unethical [88] to genetically engineer animals.

      1.3.4.4. Con:
      The studies that suggest this are underpowered, poorly designed, and of limited to no statistical validity [89]. Many of these studies have been published in predatory journals without peer review [90].

  1.4. Con:
  GMOs pose a number of environmental hazards.

    1.4.1. Pro:
    GMOs may reduce biodiversity by outcompeting similar plants, leading to unforeseen consequences for the environment, animals, and people.

      1.4.1.1. Con:
      The same principle applies to selective breeding; for example, the number of chicken varieties used to produce meat and eggs is very limited.

      1.4.1.2. Con:
      Anything can have unforeseen consequences for the environment, and such consequences could be positive or negative.

      1.4.1.3. Pro:
      Some GMO/wild-type hybrid organisms [91] have an advantage over both of their parents..

      1.4.1.4. Pro:
      Many GMO strains enjoy natural advantages over wild strains [92] and are likely to replace them in the wild. This can have unforeseen consequences on the environment.

        1.4.1.4.1. Con:
        A number of GMOs have competitive disadvantages [93] compared to wild-type organisms and are often outcompeted by them in the wild.

    1.4.2. Con:
    GMOs can be engineered to be pest resistant, reducing reliance on environmentally harmful pesticides and herbicides.

      1.4.2.1. Pro:
      GMO crops [94] can be engineered to produce biochemical pesticides and thereby require little to no synthetic pesticides.

        1.4.2.1.1. Con:
        The studies on whether GMO crop adoption has increased or decreased pesticide usage are inconclusive [95].

      1.4.2.2. Con:
      To date, the majority of GMOs have been herbicide-resistant, which enables more aggressive herbicide [96] use that can harm people and the environment.

        1.4.2.2.1. Con:
        Current usage is a poor predictor of future applications. New GE methods are significantly reducing costs and expanding the possible applications.

      1.4.2.3. Con:
      The only two transgenic crops engineered for herbicide (glyphosate) resistance that are produced on a large scale are corn and soybeans; the current usage of these GMO crops creates and promotes an increased use of herbicides that is not sustainable [97].

        1.4.2.3.1. Con:
        Herbicides are applied to both GMO and non-GMO crops.

      1.4.2.4. Pro:
      Adoption of glyphosate has supported a move away from more toxic and persistent herbicides [98].

        1.4.2.4.1. Con:
        In specific case studies of Roundup-ready crops, such as sugar beets, the herbicides used on the non-GMO strains are inferior [99] in terms of health, environmental impact, and cost.

        1.4.2.4.2. Pro:
        Trends in the use of herbicides are not clear cut; evidence suggests that glyphosate adoption has reduced overall toxicity, and the rate of herbicide usage increased less in GMO systems (p. 1 [100]).

        1.4.2.4.3. Pro:
        Glyphosate uptake is associated with reduced usage of more caustic [101] herbicides; systematic reviews show that GMO adoption [102] is associated with a reduction in the toxicity of the employed herbicides.

    1.4.3. Con:
    The changes brought by crop biotechnology are greener than their alternatives.

      1.4.3.1. Con:
      GMO production for use in biofuels is unsustainable and destroys land (p. 3 [103]).

        1.4.3.1.1. Con:
        Despite the challenges, GMOs offer a way for oil-dependent societies to reduce carbon output in the short term, without adversely affecting their populations or economies, while more ecologically sound technologies are developed.

        1.4.3.1.2. Pro:
        Biofuels are not an efficient method of reducing emissions, as the same amount [104] of energy is required as an input to produce the equivalent biofuel output.

      1.4.3.2. Pro:
      GMOs significantly decrease greenhouse gas emissions from agriculture. In 2012, this was the equivalent of removing 27 billion kg [105] of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere.

    1.4.4. Con:
    GMO plants can be engineered to utilize nitrogen more efficiently [106], thereby decreasing environmental pollution from nitrogen fertilizers.

      1.4.4.1. Pro:
      Nitrogen fertilizers are a substantial source of atmospheric nitrous oxide [107], which acts as a potent greenhouse gas [108].

      1.4.4.2. Con:
      Animal agriculture [109] is a greater contributor of nitrogen pollution than nitrogen fertilizers via the combination of feed production and animal waste. Any strategy to reduce atmospheric nitrous oxide must focus on animal sources.

    1.4.5. Pro:
    Lack of genetic diversity due to monoculture of GMOs increases the vulnerability of the human populations that depend on them.

      1.4.5.1. Pro:
      Increased genetic diversity [110] ensures adaptability to changing conditions and protects a population against invasive pathogens.

      1.4.5.2. Con:
      GMOs do not foster or drive broadacre commodity crop production [111] (monoculture), which is responsible for reduced genetic diversity.

        1.4.5.2.1. Pro:
        GMO investment has a high capital cost and follows rather than creates commodity markets, and is therefore not responsible for trends like broad acre corn and soy.

        1.4.5.2.2. Pro:
        Decreased diversity of crops and lower frequency of rotation in the US cannot be attributed to the adoption of GE crops [112].

      1.4.5.3. Con:
      A meta analysis of studies failed to find evidence of reduced genetic diversity [113] since the introduction of GMOs.

        1.4.5.3.1. Pro:
        The number of varieties has actually increased [114] in last 100 years.

        1.4.5.3.2. Pro:
        The myth of decreasing diversity originated with comparisons of the number of seed names in catalogs from the 1900s and recent catalogs; in older catalogs, many seed varieties were sold under multiple names [115].

  1.5. Pro:
  GMOs could be specifically formulated in order to address some environmental issues [PETase producing Bacteria, for example.] article by phys.org [116]
