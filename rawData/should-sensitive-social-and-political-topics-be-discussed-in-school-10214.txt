Discussion Name:
Should sensitive social and political topics be discussed in school?

1. Thesis:
Sensitive social and political topics should be discussed in school.

  1.1. Con:
  Schools should focus on traditional and non-controversial subjects.

    1.1.1. Con:
    Many less traditional and controversial subjects are an important part of education.

      1.1.1.1. Pro:
      Comprehensive sex education, while controversial to some, has been shown to improve student outcomes [1].

        1.1.1.1.1. Pro:
        Teens who complete a comprehensive sex education program [2] are 60% less likely to become pregnant or impregnate someone.

      1.1.1.2. Con:
      Schools can allow students to explore such topics outside of the compulsory curriculum.

        1.1.1.2.1. Pro:
        Schools can create elective or advanced placement courses for students with both the interest and maturity to engage with sensitive issues.

          1.1.1.2.1.1. Con:
          Creating such alternate pathways would reinforce the idea that such topics are not important to the average person, which is untrue. [3]

        1.1.1.2.2. Con:
        It would be unfair to other students if some are able to benefit from engaging in essential discourse on sensitive issues.

      1.1.1.3. Pro:
      Discussing controversial topics with students increases their sense of agency [4] and empowers them to drive change.

        1.1.1.3.1. Pro:
        Although discussion of identity can be a sensitive topic (pp. 3, 16 [5]), part of learning about the world is gaining an understanding of one's place within it [6].

        1.1.1.3.2. Con:
        Sensitive topics are often addressed using outdated or inherently biased instructional materials, which can be disempowering for students (p. 618 [7]).

      1.1.1.4. Con:
      It would be better to keep sensitive social and political topics as the purview of colleges.

        1.1.1.4.1. Pro:
        Adolescence is a sensitive time in a child's development, where they are particularly susceptible to outside influence [8]. It would be better to address discussion of difficult subjects once their identities are better established.

        1.1.1.4.2. Con:
        As of 2018, about 2/3 of Americans have not attained a college degree [9], so reserving discussion of sensitive topics for universities would leave out the majority of individuals.

    1.1.2. Pro:
    Discussing sensitive social and political topics in school does not meet the intended purpose of schools, which is to benefit society's general welfare.

      1.1.2.1. Pro:
      The social purpose of schooling is to promote the development of an educated workforce [10]. Time spent discussing sensitive issues could be better spent on teaching students valuable career skills.

        1.1.2.1.1. Con:
        Being able to engage with sensitive topics is becoming a relevant career skill.

          1.1.2.1.1.1. Pro:
          Modern companies [11] increasingly have roles and departments dedicated to diversity, inclusion, and equality that specialise in engaging with sensitive topics, such as identity and discrimination.

          1.1.2.1.1.2. Pro:
          Being able to effectively discuss controversial issues is essential to occupying public office.

          1.1.2.1.1.3. Con:
          More often than not, discussing controversial topics is taboo in workspaces [12].

        1.1.2.1.2. Pro:
        Having an educated workforce improves the economy [13], which is beneficial to society.

      1.1.2.2. Con:
      The aim of school is to enable the fullest possible development of each learner for living morally, creatively, and productively in a democratic society (p. 1 [14]); therefore, sensitive subjects need to be discussed.

        1.1.2.2.1. Pro:
        The whole of society is affected by the quality of education of each student both individually and in aggregate.

        1.1.2.2.2. Con:
        Students do not necessarily need to discuss sensitive topics in order to live morally, creatively, and productively.

    1.1.3. Con:
    Schools are expected to promote student mental health and well-being [15], which are directly related to sensitive social and political issues for some pupils.

      1.1.3.1. Con:
      The inflammatory nature of some sensitive topics might affect the mental well-being of vulnerable students.

        1.1.3.1.1. Pro:
        Students often feel uncomfortable and anxious discussing sensitive topics, particularly when they hold a different opinion than their peers [16].

        1.1.3.1.2. Con:
        Marginalized students can perceive the avoidance of some sensitive topics as erasure [17], which can harm their mental health.

        1.1.3.1.3. Pro:
        Students may react poorly to sensitive topics in situations where they have absorbed strong preexisting beliefs from their family or community.

      1.1.3.2. Pro:
      Perceived racial discrimination is a primary source of anxiety and mental health concerns in adolescent African Americans (pp. 1–2 [18]).

        1.1.3.2.1. Con:
        Educators are often inadequately trained in effective strategies and tactics [19] for discussing race in the classroom.

        1.1.3.2.2. Pro:
        Addressing both real and perceived racial discrimination in the school setting inherently requires discussion of sensitive topics (p. 104 [20]).

      1.1.3.3. Pro:
      School staff are expected to provide a safe space for LGBTQ+ children to speak out or share their concerns (p. 25 [21]).

        1.1.3.3.1. Con:
        Such spaces can be provided for LGBTQ+ students without requiring all students to participate in sensitive discussions on gender and sexual orientation.

        1.1.3.3.2. Pro:
        Supporting the well-being of LGBTQ+ pupils may involve discussion of recent government actions [22], on which different political parties may hold divergent views, and other topics where there is ongoing debate.

        1.1.3.3.3. Pro:
        Young LGBT people [23] are more likely to be bullied in school than heterosexual and cisgender people, and they are less likely to feel represented in sex and relationship education.

    1.1.4. Pro:
    Schools should be focused on effectively teaching core subjects.

      1.1.4.1. Con:
      Critical thinking, forming arguments, and exploring new topics should be central pillars of an education system, all of which are fostered by discussing sensitive social and political topics.

        1.1.4.1.1. Pro:
        School debates on sensitive topics could build a culture of tolerance, where people discuss things according to their rational value and understand the implications of both their own and others' arguments.

        1.1.4.1.2. Con:
        Such skills can be developed through rhetorical exercise on accepted educational subjects [24]; the topic need not be controversial.

      1.1.4.2. Pro:
      Discussing sensitive topics takes time away from core classes like science, math, English, and history.

        1.1.4.2.1. Con:
        Student debate on sensitive topics could be reserved for an extracurricular debate club, which would not interfere with core subjects.

          1.1.4.2.1.1. Con:
          Keeping sensitive topics outside the main curriculum would greatly limit the number of students with access to such education.

        1.1.4.2.2. Pro:
        Knowledge of science, math, and English is required for work, and hence the time spent teaching them should not be reduced (p. 1 [25]).

        1.1.4.2.3. Pro:
        Discussing sensitive topics will likely take a lot of time [26], as such conversations require a lot of background and can spiral out of control.

        1.1.4.2.4. Con:
        Discussion of sensitive topics can be efficiently integrated into core classes to teach students how to address sensitive issues within those fields. For example, sensitive scientific topics are best addressed while teaching science.

          1.1.4.2.4.1. Pro:
          History can discuss the origin of a sensitive issue; English can discuss the rhetorical techniques used to support opposing arguments; and science and math can discuss the empirical evidence for different positions.

        1.1.4.2.5. Con:
        Awareness and understanding of current issues are no less important than the skills taught in core classes.

          1.1.4.2.5.1. Pro:
          To be educated on a topic is not equivalent to having been taught to think critically about that topic.

      1.1.4.3. Con:
      Not allowing discussion of sensitive topics would prevent schools from exhausting the core subjects; a rich educational experience becomes impossible when students are not provided with as many facts on a given topic as possible.

        1.1.4.3.1. Con:
        Lessons on sensitive subjects can be limited to facts and objective information, leaving out the controversial elements (p. 9 [27]).

          1.1.4.3.1.1. Con:
          In many cases, the facts surrounding sensitive issues are themselves a contentious topic; discussing the purported facts can create controversy (p. 8 [28]).

          1.1.4.3.1.2. Pro:
          Exposure to the facts about sensitive topics is less likely to lead to confrontation [29] than discussing or debating them.

        1.1.4.3.2. Con:
        Students do not need to be taught every single fact about a subject. Sensitive areas of core subjects are usually the purview of specialised study or public discourse.

          1.1.4.3.2.1. Pro:
          Differentiated instruction [30] can allow students who successfully demonstrate a greater aptitude for understanding and dealing with sensitive topics to explore such issues in more depth.

        1.1.4.3.3. Pro:
        A thorough understanding of biology requires covering sexual reproduction and evolution, both of which are controversial [31] for some people.

        1.1.4.3.4. Pro:
        English classes are sometimes prevented [32] from teaching classic books because they cover controversial topics.

        1.1.4.3.5. Pro:
        History cannot be properly taught [33] without discussion of some sensitive social and political topics.

  1.2. Pro:
  Discussing sensitive topics in school would support the development of students into effective adults.

    1.2.1. Con:
    Children often struggle to take in more nuanced and complex perspectives. Attempts to discuss sensitive topics could backfire and lead them to develop extreme conclusions, fail to learn anything, or simply leave them confused.

      1.2.1.1. Con:
      If children aren't taught how to approach sensitive social and political topics in an appropriate manner, they may fail to properly handle such topics when they become adults and the topics become more pertinent.

        1.2.1.1.1. Pro:
        Children who do not discuss sensitive topics during childhood can experience social tension in the future, as they will likely remain unable to sympathize with experiences they cannot personally relate to.

        1.2.1.1.2. Con:
        Discussing sensitive social topics with children unfairly forces them to adopt adult perspectives [34] before they are sufficiently developed.

      1.2.1.2. Pro:
      Teachers sometimes tell students how to interpret difficult issues; this can lead to teachers' biases having a significant influence on students' opinions.

        1.2.1.2.1. Pro:
        Science and religious education teachers sometimes go beyond their purview when teaching students about evolution or creation myths (pp. 211–212 [35]).

      1.2.1.3. Pro:
      Sensitive political topics can only be properly approached when one has a complete understanding of civics. Since civics is generally not being taught [36] in school, sensitive political topics will only confuse students.

        1.2.1.3.1. Con:
        Many schools have civic clubs that students can join voluntarily if they have an interest in social and political issues.

      1.2.1.4. Con:
      Teenage children have the maturity [37] to engage with sensitive social and political topics, and it can be beneficial to let them form their opinions through debate before they reach voting age.

    1.2.2. Pro:
    Students should be well informed on sensitive issues since an understanding of problems is essential to participating in society.

      1.2.2.1. Con:
      Current affairs are not as important to children because they are limited in their ability to make decisions based on ongoing events.

        1.2.2.1.1. Pro:
        Having views on political issues is not essential for children since they cannot actively participate in politics.

        1.2.2.1.2. Con:
        Learning about current affairs is essential for students in developing a foundation upon which their future political decisions can be based (p. 29 [38]).

      1.2.2.2. Pro:
      Students encounter sensitive issues firsthand, regardless of whether adults talk about them. Discussing such issues in school would ensure that they are adequately prepared to successfully deal with the things that they will encounter in life.

        1.2.2.2.1. Con:
        Exposure to sensitive issues can be blocked until children are mature enough to discuss them.

          1.2.2.2.1.1. Pro:
          By limiting or eliminating social media use for children under 18 years old, it is unlikely that they would be exposed to some of these ideas.

          1.2.2.2.1.2. Con:
          Children are regularly exposed to sensitive topics via the mainstream media and conversation with other children (p. 4 [39]).

        1.2.2.2.2. Pro:
        Increased access to information in modern times makes it impossible to shield children from sensitive topics. Ordered discussions in school are the best way for them to engage these topics in a controlled and constructive fashion.

        1.2.2.2.3. Pro:
        Students will be unprepared to analyze and interpret events if they are taught to ignore them in public settings like school.

          1.2.2.2.3.1. Pro:
          If sensitive topics are not taught to students, they form the false impression that such things do not occur.

          1.2.2.2.3.2. Con:
          Students are likely to be exposed to these sensitive topics in their lives outside school, so it is doubtful that they will remain unable to understand them.

        1.2.2.2.4. Con:
        When children perceive that they have limited knowledge to understand something, they compensate by learning more [40], so they would naturally become more knowledgeable and critical as they encounter such issues.

          1.2.2.2.4.1. Pro:
          It is likely that students will turn to guardians, trusted adults, and information sources about such sensitive topics when they encounter them.

          1.2.2.2.4.2. Con:
          Children with more background knowledge, such as that cultivated in classroom discussion, are more aware of incomplete explanations and are thus more likely to think critically and seek out additional knowledge on a topic (p. 15 [41]).

    1.2.3. Con:
    Discussion of sensitive topics could make students of opposing opinions more extremist in their views [42].

      1.2.3.1. Con:
      Balanced discussions of sensitive topics should include many different perspectives; no opinion should inherently be considered as "opposing."

      1.2.3.2. Con:
      Extremist views are largely formed by a lack of exposure to different perspectives.

        1.2.3.2.1. Pro:
        Children with a comprehensive understanding of social issues are more open-minded and less susceptible to political and religious bias.

        1.2.3.2.2. Pro:
        Many extremists have never actually met the various minorities [43] they oppose, and they often change their views after meeting and befriending such people.

      1.2.3.3. Con:
      Discussing these topics in school helps to confront extremist views and false news.

        1.2.3.3.1. Pro:
        Teaching interventions can help students develop resilience to extremist views (p. 5 [44]).

      1.2.3.4. Pro:
      Discussion of sensitive topics in school would lead students to explore subjects further on the internet, which can increase the chances that they become radicalized (p. 6 [45]).

        1.2.3.4.1. Con:
        Discussing sensitive issues in school is more likely to prevent than promote youth radicalization.

          1.2.3.4.1.1. Pro:
          Open dialogue [46] and discussion are better tools for preventing religious radicalization than monitoring, which risks alienating and isolating minority pupils.

          1.2.3.4.1.2. Pro:
          Schools that encourage open and respectful discussion of values help students develop greater resilience to radicalization (p. 4 [47]).

        1.2.3.4.2. Con:
        The internet is ripe with information that can shape children's understanding of sensitive topics. Teaching such topics in school will prime students to explore [48] the internet from a more critical perspective.

      1.2.3.5. Pro:
      Classroom discussions on sensitive topics can lead to group polarization [49], where both sides of a debate become more extreme in their opinions. This can be harmful to educational outcomes.

    1.2.4. Pro:
    Politics and other relevant social concerns should be taught at school because they are highly beneficial [50] for making students knowledgeable of current affairs while they are still young.

      1.2.4.1. Pro:
      Providing children with factual, unbiased information from both sides of sensitive political issues at a young age can help them avoid future political apathy.

        1.2.4.1.1. Con:
        That may also be more likely to increase rather than decrease future political apathy because political parties usually do not fully recognize the complexity of sensitive subjects but e.g. perpetuate simplistic narratives. Political marketing aims to gather as many votes as possible usually relying on simple messages agreed to by many – elections tend to oversimplify complex issues [51].

        1.2.4.1.2. Pro:
        Political science and public policy research indicate that voting habits are formed in childhood and adolescence [52].

          1.2.4.1.2.1. Pro:
          Children who participated in the US Fast Track program, designed to help at-risk children develop social skills, had a greater likelihood of voting [53] later in life.

        1.2.4.1.3. Con:
        Educating kids on controversial topics could lead to feelings of anxiety and disenfranchisement that could result in increased apathy and disengagement from politics.

          1.2.4.1.3.1. Con:
          Evidence suggests that civic education is positively linked with voter participation (p. 4 [54]).

          1.2.4.1.3.2. Pro:
          Cambridge Analytica's "Do So [55]" campaign in Trinidad and Tobago was successful in generating voter apathy by stimulating feelings of disillusionment with the current government, resulting in decreased voter [56] turnout among certain demographics.

          1.2.4.1.3.3. Con:
          Failing to educate children about how their country works is more likely to breed feelings of apathy since they feel uninformed and powerless to enact change.

        1.2.4.1.4. Pro:
        Most research [57] supports [58] the connection between higher educational attainment [59] and greater political participation and engagement.

          1.2.4.1.4.1. Pro:
          Learning about political issues in school helps pupils become active citizens [60] who can hold their own opinions while respecting other perspectives.

        1.2.4.1.5. Con:
        Political apathy often results from bad political experiences rather than a lack of information (p. 397 [61]).

          1.2.4.1.5.1. Con:
          Politicians and political systems are subject to error [62], so bad political experiences are inevitable. Learning how to deal with this and build one's opinion in relation to sensitive issues is an essential function of the education system.

      1.2.4.2. Con:
      Children in primary school do not typically care about sensitive social and political topics.

        1.2.4.2.1. Pro:
        School-age children are more focused on issues in their own lives [63] than with society at large.

        1.2.4.2.2. Con:
        The generalization that primary school students are uninterested in sensitive topics is reductive. Each individual student's curiosity is motivated by factors unique to that child and their experiences [64].

      1.2.4.3. Pro:
      Students should be provided with discussion and facts before they decide on a particular view that they may hold for the rest of their lives.

        1.2.4.3.1. Pro:
        Teachers supervising debates can ensure that students support their arguments with evidence, leading to a more productive discussion of ideas than the students might find elsewhere.

        1.2.4.3.2. Con:
        People's views change as they meet new people, travel to new places, and have new experiences. There is no reason to believe that people decide on their views while in school and stick to them forever.

        1.2.4.3.3. Pro:
        Young people learn and change opinions more easily than adults because the prefrontal cortex of their brains, which is used for rational thinking, is still developing. [65]

    1.2.5. Pro:
    Through engaging with sensitive topics, students would learn to respond to such issues with tact and diplomacy.

      1.2.5.1. Pro:
      Discussing sensitive topics can teach students to empathise with different opinions, to see grey areas, and to understand compromise, all of which are valuable life skills in modern society.

        1.2.5.1.1. Con:
        While the ability to empathize and understand the perspectives of others is important, children must also learn to be assertive and confident in their own opinions [66] in order to be effective.

        1.2.5.1.2. Pro:
        Children are self-centered [67] and have difficulty considering the perspectives of others. Learning different perspectives on sensitive issues would help them relate better to the feelings of others.

      1.2.5.2. Con:
      Focusing on children's tact and diplomacy when they encounter sensitive topics can reduce a valuable learning opportunity [68] to a simple lesson on manners as opposed to effectively resolving sensitive issues.

        1.2.5.2.1. Con:
        Learning politeness and good manners is essential to the social development [69] of children.

        1.2.5.2.2. Pro:
        Calm and civility are of lesser use than authenticity in creating real solutions to the challenges in society.

  1.3. Con:
  Sensitive topics can be divisive, which impedes the functioning of the school.

    1.3.1. Pro:
    Exploring sensitive topics might lead children to identify and focus on their differences and expose them to harmful views concerning these differences.

      1.3.1.1. Pro:
      Younger children may remain unaware of the differences between them; discussing such issues in school would lead to children focusing on such differences before they have had a chance to develop a broader appreciation of diversity.

        1.3.1.1.1. Pro:
        Research has shown that children are often unable to understand [70] that not all families are the same.

        1.3.1.1.2. Con:
        Children become aware of many differences between people at a very young age (p. 5 [71]).

      1.3.1.2. Con:
      This depends on how the discussion is conducted. If this is a major concern, students can participate in online discussions using anonymous accounts.

        1.3.1.2.1. Pro:
        Anonymity takes away the fear of being personally attacked for expressing opinions.

        1.3.1.2.2. Con:
        Anonymity abstracts the statement from its source, which may render it contextless and thus unclear.

    1.3.2. Pro:
    Discussing issues like politics [72] in school could lead to the bullying of children who are different from their classmates.

      1.3.2.1. Pro:
      Children are frequently exposed to political bullying via media coverage, and they often adopt similar tactics [73] with other children.

        1.3.2.1.1. Con:
        Rational and unbiased discussion of such issues could counterbalance the biased perspectives [74] children learn via the media.

        1.3.2.1.2. Pro:
        It may be difficult for teachers and parents to identify bullying between students, as a great deal of such behavior has shifted online (pp. 7–8 [75]).

      1.3.2.2. Con:
      Educating children about these differences through open discussion [76] would reduce bullying.

        1.3.2.2.1. Pro:
        Bullying itself is a sensitive topic in schools. Encouraging open discussion of the issue helps improve awareness and increases the reporting of such behaviors (pp. 77–78 [77]).

        1.3.2.2.2. Con:
        Open discussion of issues may not actually decrease bullying and may shift bullying to more covert and passive-aggressive methods (p. 9 [78]).

    1.3.3. Con:
    Students and teachers are already divided by a whole host of factors; it is unlikely that discussion of sensitive topics will impact this divide meaningfully.

      1.3.3.1. Pro:
      Teachers and millennial students are deeply divided over the use of digital media [79].

      1.3.3.2. Pro:
      Adolescent students can develop a negative relationship with authority figures, such as teachers, while they are developing greater autonomy (p. 138 [80]).

      1.3.3.3. Con:
      Discussion of sensitive topics in school could easily go awry and create a wider gap between students and teachers.

      1.3.3.4. Con:
      It is impossible to generalize about the impact of such discussions on students and teachers since each individual has a unique perspective and body of experience.

    1.3.4. Con:
    Divided opinions outside and within schools already exist. Not discussing such topics would only entrench the existing divisions and prevent reflection and productive engagement.

      1.3.4.1. Con:
      Discussion of sensitive topics has the potential to further existing divisions, particularly when biased opinions are presented.

        1.3.4.1.1. Con:
        Identifying biased opinions is the first step in overcoming them.

        1.3.4.1.2. Pro:
        Bias has been shown to adversely affect student engagement (p. 2 [81]).

      1.3.4.2. Pro:
      It is possible to discuss sensitive topics with students in a way that avoids value judgments (p. 1 [82]).

        1.3.4.2.1. Con:
        Certain topics, such as sex education, are inherently tied to morality, so moral judgments are unavoidable (p. 60 [83]).

        1.3.4.2.2. Pro:
        Discussion of sensitive topics can remain focused on facts [84], allowing students to consider and determine their own values.

          1.3.4.2.2.1. Con:
          Some sensitive topics concern objective moral values [85], so exclusively focusing on facts may be impossible.

      1.3.4.3. Pro:
      Students of different ages are at different stages of moral development [86]. Topics that may be impossible to discuss with younger students can be completely appropriate for older students.

        1.3.4.3.1. Pro:
        Schools have a social obligation to impart moral education (p. 8 [87]); this inherently requires discussion of sensitive topics.

        1.3.4.3.2. Con:
        As moral values are relative [88], schools that promote particular values are inherently teaching a culturally biased perspective.

    1.3.5. Con:
    The sensitivity of a topic is a subjective matter, and therefore all topics should be discussed in our schools, including those that some may consider sensitive.

      1.3.5.1. Con:
      The subjective nature of such topics presents its own challenges; for example, students and teachers with a personal connection to a sensitive topic may be re-traumatized by discussing it (p. 6 [89]).

        1.3.5.1.1. Pro:
        Trauma-informed teaching practices must start with an acknowledgement of the school's role in causing and recreating trauma [90].

        1.3.5.1.2. Con:
        Exposure to traumatic triggers in controlled situations can help desensitize [91] individuals to traumatic situations and help them heal.

      1.3.5.2. Pro:
      Topics are not inherently sensitive, it is only personal experience and social context that make them so (pp. 127–128 [92]).

        1.3.5.2.1. Con:
        Some topics are inherently inappropriate for a particular audience; for example, graphic sexual education is inappropriate for children (p. 23 [93]).

        1.3.5.2.2. Pro:
        Discussing sensitive topics in school could help students gain more objective points of view on important issues (pp. 3, 68 [94]).

      1.3.5.3. Pro:
      Responsible and open-minded discussions will teach children that sensitive issues should be addressed instead of avoided.

        1.3.5.3.1. Pro:
        Avoiding sensitive topics in the classroom allows children's biases to become firmly ingrained [95].

        1.3.5.3.2. Con:
        Given the diverse perspectives on sensitive topics and the difficulty of addressing such differences with others, it is beneficial that students learn to refrain from such discussions to avoid unnecessary conflict (p. 2 [96]).

      1.3.5.4. Con:
      Teaching is more about providing a framework for learning [97] than merely sharing content; while a complete curriculum should make students aware of sensitive issues, they should not be forced to discuss them.

        1.3.5.4.1. Pro:
        When students learn about a subject, they will naturally have questions about sensitive topics. It is sufficient to address these questions in reference to the subject without making such concerns the focal point (p. 68 [98]).

    1.3.6. Pro:
    Some sensitive and political topics can make children feel ostracised based on their social and political background [99].

      1.3.6.1. Pro:
      Children are often insufficiently competent in intercultural communication, so it is likely that they will fail to understand and respect those of other cultures and ethnicities (pp. 39–40 [100]).

        1.3.6.1.1. Con:
        Younger school-age children exhibit a number of skills and attitudes that contribute to their intercultural communicative competence (pp. 4–5 [101]).

        1.3.6.1.2. Pro:
        Many children have little real-world experience [102] with cultural diversity, so they may be insensitive to cultural differences or negatively affected by the attitudes of children from other backgrounds.

      1.3.6.2. Con:
      The inability to understand alternative points of view leads to alienation on both sides of an issue, which can worsen the conflict among students or between students and teachers.

        1.3.6.2.1. Pro:
        By discussing topics that people feel sensitive about and weighing different perspectives on them, students may actually become less sensitive and irritable about those topics and feel more at ease with controversy and ambivalence in their lives.

      1.3.6.3. Con:
      School may be the only place students meet people with experiences and concerns vastly different from their own.

        1.3.6.3.1. Pro:
        Families and other social groupings are often little more than echo chambers for ideas that a student has grown up with. Discussions at school present young people with personal perspectives beyond those of family, friends, and their religious congregations.

        1.3.6.3.2. Pro:
        Discussing these topics in school could raise awareness; that way, people may become more sensitive and caring to hear other's experiences and opinions.

  1.4. Pro:
  Schools are an ideal setting for children to explore sensitive topics.

    1.4.1. Pro:
    In a school setting, teachers could appropriately guide and moderate discussion of sensitive issues.

      1.4.1.1. Con:
      Teachers may be uncomfortable with discussing sensitive issues, which could negatively impact the quality of education on such topics.

        1.4.1.1.1. Pro:
        Teachers who are uncomfortable with providing sex education [103] can unwittingly present the topic as taboo.

        1.4.1.1.2. Con:
        Teachers can take steps to prepare themselves [104] to effectively discuss sensitive topics with students.

      1.4.1.2. Pro:
      When teachers discuss politics with children, they do so in a professional and monitored environment where they can be held accountable.

        1.4.1.2.1. Con:
        Tenure, which prevents bad teachers from being eliminated, increases the risk that biased teachers [105] will influence young minds.

        1.4.1.2.2. Pro:
        It is against the law for teachers to talk about their religion or their political opinion to their students.

          1.4.1.2.2.1. Pro:
          The Establishment Clause of the First Amendment [106] of the US Constitution restricts teachers regarding personal religious opinions [107] in the school environment.

          1.4.1.2.2.2. Con:
          In Canada, the only restrictions on teachers' freedom of speech are prohibitions against harassment, violent threats, insults, and hate speech [108].

          1.4.1.2.2.3. Pro:
          In the UK, the Education Act of 1996 prohibits political indoctrination [109].

            1.4.1.2.2.3.1. Pro:
            Teachers and staff can share their personal views, but they must ensure that they are not presented as fact and point out that there are opposing views that the students might wish to consider.

            1.4.1.2.2.3.2. Con:
            In February 2022, the UK Government released guidance on political impartiality in schools [110], which stated that there is no blanket prohibition on school faculty expressing their personal views on subjects being taught to pupils.

      1.4.1.3. Con:
      Sensitive topics shouldn't be discussed in schools because teachers may bias their students [111].

        1.4.1.3.1. Con:
        Just because a teacher might include bias when discussing a political subject is not a valid reason to ban all discussion of politics.

          1.4.1.3.1.1. Pro:
          If teachers wanted to bias their students, they could do so without explicitly mentioning topics and just alluding to them in a skewed way. Open discussions where all sides are heard are less likely to generate bias.

            1.4.1.3.1.1.1. Pro:
            It is likely that some sensitive topics will come up in class regardless. If teachers only discuss such sensitive topics as an aside to the main lesson, the discussions are likely to be brief, and there wouldn't be time or an established process to present both sides fairly.

          1.4.1.3.1.2. Pro:
          Bias is not inherently bad.

            1.4.1.3.1.2.1. Con:
            Biased thinking is inherently opposed to critical thinking, as it restricts the exploration and appreciation of the merits of other possible views.

            1.4.1.3.1.2.2. Pro:
            Entirely unbiased teaching is not ideal; education is intended to contribute to the spiritual, moral, social, and cultural development of students and promote the fundamental values of society (p. 18 [112]).

        1.4.1.3.2. Con:
        Teachers could be required to undergo training [113] in recognising their own biases and mitigating [114] their effects. For example, The National Board for Professional Teaching Standards offers multi-day bias training for teachers seeking National Board Certification (p. 3 [115]).

          1.4.1.3.2.1. Pro:
          Teachers can learn to be open about their own political bias, remind students that they are allowed to disagree, and identify when they are leaving the realm of purely factual information to express their own opinion. Such practices would also help students recognize bias outside of the classroom.

          1.4.1.3.2.2. Con:
          Personal bias can be notoriously difficult to identify and can be extremely subtle. This is why it has been evident in every profession. Therefore, it is unlikely that teachers are better placed to avoid it.

        1.4.1.3.3. Pro:
        According to surveys, US teachers tend to be more supportive of the Democratic Party [116]; in one survey, 79% of surveyed US school teachers with partisan political beliefs identified as Democrats [117].

          1.4.1.3.3.1. Con:
          Either the teacher has a different bias than their parents or peer group, or the same one. So either they are confronted with a different perspective, which is always good, or it does not matter.

            1.4.1.3.3.1.1. Con:
            Biased teaching that unfairly favors family and community beliefs is equally damaging to the integrity of a child.

            1.4.1.3.3.1.2. Pro:
            Most children are likely to take on their parents' social and political views [118]. Learning different perspectives in school would support them in making up their own minds.

          1.4.1.3.3.2. Con:
          A tendency for a party does not imply bias or vice versa a 50-50 share does not imply neutrality.

          1.4.1.3.3.3. Pro:
          Half of the surveyed educators reported voting for Hillary Clinton in the 2016 presidential election, while less than a third voted for Donald Trump (p. 3 [119]).

          1.4.1.3.3.4. Pro:
          A majority of polled students with conservative political views [120] feel that their views are not represented, and many of them are hesitant to share their views for fear of being judged.

        1.4.1.3.4. Pro:
        Teachers are regarded as an authority on the subjects that they teach, especially by younger pupils. As such, views supported by teachers could be seen as more valid by the pupils, thereby clouding their judgement.

          1.4.1.3.4.1. Pro:
          Because students often think teachers are all-knowing and always right [121], they are likely to take what teachers say as facts, even if they are biassed opinions.

          1.4.1.3.4.2. Con:
          Research suggests that, when assessing reality status, children are equally likely to believe or doubt (p. 1 [122]).

      1.4.1.4. Pro:
      Teachers are trained to address sensitive topics with their students in an educational and constructive way, and convey accurate, objective, and unbiased information [123] (p. 5 [124]).

        1.4.1.4.1. Pro:
        Teachers are trained to convey accurate historical information, objective science, and purely informational or unbiased news reports.

          1.4.1.4.1.1. Con:
          Teachers may express their own opinions on a topic, even if unintentionally.

          1.4.1.4.1.2. Con:
          Teachers do not necessarily follow their training.

          1.4.1.4.1.3. Pro:
          If all sides of an issue, including the pros and cons of each argument, must be discussed and the students must develop their own conclusions using critical thought, then there is little opportunity for teachers to impose bias.

        1.4.1.4.2. Pro:
        Teachers can facilitate discussion among students, helping to separate facts from opinions and reminding students to be respectful.

          1.4.1.4.2.1. Pro:
          Ideally, the role of a teacher would be to motivate students to debate among themselves and form their own opinions without any input from the teacher whatsoever.

    1.4.2. Con:
    Schools lack the diversity to ensure that such discussions would reflect real-world opinions and experiences (p. 686 [125]).

      1.4.2.1. Con:
      Students will be more likely to believe stereotypes if they are not taught to tolerate and appreciate differences.

      1.4.2.2. Con:
      Schools are significantly more likely than students' families to have diversity in thought, race, ethnicity, and socioeconomic class.

      1.4.2.3. Pro:
      Despite the US population becoming increasingly diverse, more than 33% of US K–12 students attend schools where a single ethnicity makes up 75% or more of the student body [126].

      1.4.2.4. Pro:
      The ethnicity of teachers is likely to match the majority ethnicity [127] of a school's students.

    1.4.3. Con:
    Students are not currently taught [128] the critical thinking skills that would be necessary to properly interrogate and debate sensitive issues.

      1.4.3.1. Con:
      Discussion of these subjects could be utilized as a pathway through which to learn critical thinking skills.

        1.4.3.1.1. Pro:
        Critical thinking skills could be included in the curricula on sensitive topics, ensuring that students are made aware of the subjective role of the teacher and taught to weigh the pros and cons of views presented to them.

          1.4.3.1.1.1. Pro:
          Such topics could be preceded by direct critical thinking skill education.

        1.4.3.1.2. Con:
        It is better for students to develop critical thinking skills prior to discussing sensitive topics, as they will be better prepared to engage with such topics.

      1.4.3.2. Pro:
      Most schools don't start teaching how to identify bias [129] until the last few years, if even then.

        1.4.3.2.1. Pro:
        Without prior tutelage in critical thinking skills, students are not equipped to identify presuppositions and fallacies that may be presented by teachers.

          1.4.3.2.1.1. Con:
          Once a student can read and write [130], they can think critically and communicate.

        1.4.3.2.2. Con:
        Children are already taught critical thinking skills in age-appropriate ways, even if not through overt critical thinking modules.

          1.4.3.2.2.1. Pro:
          Children are taught the difference between fiction and fact in the early years, even as early as the age of three [131], both at home and in school. They are led to question the reality of stories and consider what moral lessons they teach.

    1.4.4. Con:
    The fact that most schools are publicly funded makes discussing sensitive political issues in schools inappropriate and ineffective.

      1.4.4.1. Pro:
      Since public schools are reliant upon funding from the state, they are unlikely to adequately address topics that are critical of government policy.

        1.4.4.1.1. Pro:
        Textbooks are generally purchased directly by the state on a large scale, so their contents are often skewed [132] towards the ideologies of majority populations.

        1.4.4.1.2. Con:
        While teachers may technically be government employees, they are sufficiently removed from administrative bodies to not preclude questioning government policy in the classroom.

        1.4.4.1.3. Con:
        Constitutionally guaranteed rights protect discussion that is critical of the government and can provide a remedy on the rare occasions that such speech is discouraged.

      1.4.4.2. Con:
      As publicly funded institutions, schools bear responsibility for protecting the essential rights guaranteed by the state, including freedom of speech and religion.

        1.4.4.2.1. Pro:
        To ensure religious freedom and freedom of education, European countries offer the teaching of religious facts in other subjects (France); non-confessional religious education (England, Sweden); optional confessional religious education (Spain, Netherlands); or, when mandatory, the possibility to opt out (Romania) (p. 19 [133]).

          1.4.4.2.1.1. Pro:
          The Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe has condemned [134] creationism in education, viewing it as an attack on two fundamental European principals: the separation of church and state and the freedom of education.

    1.4.5. Pro:
    The internal and external regulation of schools provides transparency [135] and accountability [136], which would ensure that curricula and instructional methods address sensitive topics in a way that is appropriate for children.

      1.4.5.1. Pro:
      School curricula are generally developed at a broader level, by the state or a school board, rather than by individual schools.

        1.4.5.1.1. Con:
        State education boards are not less likely than school districts or individual schools to develop biased curricula [137] on sensitive topics.

          1.4.5.1.1.1. Con:
          Education is inherently political [138]; forgoing discussion of sensitive topics due to fears of bias upholds the status quo.

          1.4.5.1.1.2. Pro:
          It is impossible to sanitize a curriculum [139] from all possible sources of bias.

            1.4.5.1.1.2.1. Con:
            A society where nobody can express independent thought about anything controversial would be utterly dehumanizing.

        1.4.5.1.2. Pro:
        Any curriculum that addresses sensitive topics could be developed by a team that represents various points of view. People can work together to find a way to satisfactorily address sensitive issues for all parties.

      1.4.5.2. Con:
      Some teachers have spoken out against inappropriate sexual education curricula that encourages open discussion of students' private sexual preferences [140].

        1.4.5.2.1. Pro:
        More than 12 US states have introduced legislation banning teachers from inappropriately discussing gender and sexuality [141] with students.

        1.4.5.2.2. Con:
        It is essential that students are able to discuss topics like sexual preference in an open forum to address the omnipresent sexualization and social pressures [142] they encounter in society.

      1.4.5.3. Con:
      Schools with strict, top-down accountability measures are unlikely to institute discussion of sensitive topics, as they tend to make use of narrower curricula that are more easily assessed (pp. 10–11 [143]).

        1.4.5.3.1. Con:
        There are other forms of school accountability that are more conducive to the inclusion of sensitive topics, such as those driven by student or community needs (p. 3 [144]).

        1.4.5.3.2. Pro:
        Schools are judged by the success of their students on academic assessments [145]. Until such assessments include sensitive topics, schools have little incentive to spend valuable class time discussing such topics.

    1.4.6. Pro:
    Young people should have the opportunity to discuss issues that are important to them in a safe, respectful, and ordered environment.

      1.4.6.1. Pro:
      Schools are a safe environment, where students who are affected by discussion of sensitive topics can receive support.

        1.4.6.1.1. Con:
        Discussion of sensitive sexual topics in school could be used by pedophile teachers [146] to have inappropriate conversations with children and groom them. [147]

        1.4.6.1.2. Pro:
        Trigger warnings [148], safe spaces, and procedural civility can and should be normalised in schools.

      1.4.6.2. Pro:
      An educational setting allows students to be provided with factual information, appropriate context, lessons on argumentation, and connections to real-life opinions and scenarios.

        1.4.6.2.1. Pro:
        Family environments are the most common alternative for such discourse; unlike schools, they are often hierarchical [149], and therefore children may not feel free to express themselves there.

      1.4.6.3. Con:
      If sensitive and political topics are discussed in school, then students must be afforded the right to free speech, which includes opinions that some people might consider hate speech.

        1.4.6.3.1. Con:
        There would be no reason; indeed, it would be counter-productive to allow ad hominem attacks between students during a discussion of sensitive social and political subjects.

        1.4.6.3.2. Con:
        Speaking freely does not imply that "hate speech" is used.

        1.4.6.3.3. Pro:
        Children often inherit views that they believe are aligned with their parents' beliefs [150], so classroom discussions may function as a platform for problematic opinions.

        1.4.6.3.4. Pro:
        Classrooms are likely to be more politically correct than other public spaces, so students run the risk of being ostracised for expressing politically incorrect views.

      1.4.6.4. Con:
      The school environment is not necessarily safe; peers may still gang up on those with fringe opinions.

        1.4.6.4.1. Con:
        School is safer than the real world, as teachers are present to moderate any conflicts that may arise.

      1.4.6.5. Con:
      Black-and-white thinking is common in immature thinkers. As such, children are likely to have more extreme views [151] and be unable to express them in a sensitive way.

    1.4.7. Con:
    Social and political issues cannot be effectively discussed in schools because students would have an incentive to present views that would get them good grades instead of having an open, honest debate.

      1.4.7.1. Pro:
      Most elementary and secondary education systems rely on grades as a key indicator of student attainment (p. 79 [152]).

      1.4.7.2. Con:
      Students need not be graded on their views; students could be graded on participation in sensitive discussions, which would likely increase their participation as well (p. 1 [153]).

  1.5. Pro:
  Discussing sensitive topics promotes cognitive development in children.

    1.5.1. Pro:
    Discussing difficult topics would provide intellectual exercise for students.

      1.5.1.1. Pro:
      Understanding sensitive issues often requires significant historical knowledge [154], which trains students to expand their recollection and application of information.

        1.5.1.1.1. Pro:
        Expanding children's historical knowledge supports the development of memory [155], a key element of cognitive development.

        1.5.1.1.2. Con:
        The history taught in many curricula is written from a biased perspective [156] and may not support accurate discussion of sensitive topics.

      1.5.1.2. Pro:
      In order to address controversial topics, students must consider multiple perspectives and arguments, which trains their brains to deal with complex issues.

        1.5.1.2.1. Pro:
        Research indicates that Level 2 perspective taking (the ability to understand the difference between one's own perspective and that of another) increased rapidly between ages 6 and 8 (pp. 2, 5 [157]). Discussion of sensitive topics could bolster such cognitive growth.

        1.5.1.2.2. Con:
        In dealing with sensitive topics, students will inevitably be exposed to harmful perspectives [158] that need to be addressed and contrasted with other views. This would require a complete overhaul of the curriculum.

      1.5.1.3. Con:
      The intellectual exercise gained from such discussions would come at the expense of developing other essential skills in the classroom.

        1.5.1.3.1. Pro:
        Classroom discussions on sensitive topics are often difficult to guide and can easily drift away from a teacher's learning goals [159] for the class.

        1.5.1.3.2. Con:
        Discussions about sensitive topics can be structured in such a way as to simultaneously support the development of other skills [160].

      1.5.1.4. Con:
      There are more direct and less controversial ways of developing the mental abilities of children, such as math drills [161], debates [162], and quizzes. [163]

        1.5.1.4.1. Pro:
        The cognitive development of younger children is primarily concerned with the acquisition of basic cognitive skills and abilities; discussion of relational concepts [164] should be reserved for older children and young adults who have already established the foundational skills.

        1.5.1.4.2. Con:
        A teaching practice being controversial does not imply that it is ineffective or undesirable; many commonly accepted practices today were controversial [165] at one point.

    1.5.2. Con:
    Being exposed to certain complex concepts at a young age can negatively affect the self-perception and perspectives of children.

      1.5.2.1. Pro:
      If topics such as discrimination are not discussed well, it could lead to children from vulnerable or minority groups developing a victimhood [166] or inferiority complex. [167]

        1.5.2.1.1. Con:
        Avoiding discussion of ethnicity is associated with a position of privilege (p. 10 [168]). Children that belong to minority ethnicities are more likely to experience discrimination, and open discussions can empower them to identify and address it.

        1.5.2.1.2. Pro:
        Evidence suggests that discussion of racial bias with the children of caregivers who have experienced racism can result in greater damage to children's self-esteem by confirming their expectations (p. 11 [169]).

      1.5.2.2. Con:
      Avoiding discussion of topics that are considered too sensitive, such as gender, shields children from the events happening in the real world.

        1.5.2.2.1. Pro:
        It is often ignorance regarding such topics that leads to them becoming controversial in the first place.

        1.5.2.2.2. Con:
        Ensuring that children are capable of comprehending a topic before it is taught is not the equivalent of avoiding the topic entirely. Teaching subjects that are beyond a student's comprehension may ingrain poor ideas and thinking.

    1.5.3. Pro:
    Learning about sensitive topics in school would open up new thought processes for children.

      1.5.3.1. Pro:
      The ability to consider alternative viewpoints and opinions is associated with successful cognitive development [170] in children.

        1.5.3.1.1. Pro:
        Children first become capable of identifying the perspective of others at around age seven (pp. 4–5 [171]); discussing age-appropriate sensitive topics could help them develop such skills further.

        1.5.3.1.2. Con:
        Children's cognitive development would increase more from interacting with knowledgeable authorities [172], such as teachers, than from discussions with other students, who lack knowledge about the nuances of many sensitive issues.

        1.5.3.1.3. Pro:
        Only 35% of high school graduates successfully achieve the formal operational stage [173] of cognitive development, where they are able to truly appreciate different perspectives. Discussing sensitive issues in school could support wider attainment of this developmental stage.

        1.5.3.1.4. Con:
        The ability to consider alternative viewpoints can also result in indecision and analysis paralysis [174], which can be retrogressive traits.

      1.5.3.2. Con:
      Being exposed to sensitive issues can also negatively skew children's perspectives of the world.

        1.5.3.2.1. Con:
        Learning about such topics in school can help children with extremist parents to develop healthier perspectives and contextualize their parents' beliefs (p. 8 [175]).

        1.5.3.2.2. Pro:
        Discussion of sensitive issues often conflates related issues, such as socioeconomic class and ethnocultural identity, in ways that can be confusing to younger students (p. 126, 132 [176]).

      1.5.3.3. Pro:
      In-school discussion of sensitive topics would help students form their own conclusions rather than relying on those of others.

        1.5.3.3.1. Pro:
        Considering evidence and forming their own conclusions would make students more confident in their opinions.

        1.5.3.3.2. Con:
        Since classroom discussions are often dominated by a few people, such as teachers or bolder students, they may still end up creating reliance on these figures for opinions.

        1.5.3.3.3. Pro:
        Learning how to discuss and evaluate sensitive issues to form an opinion would prevent students from blindly accepting politically or religiously motivated information.

      1.5.3.4. Con:
      In most lessons, there is usually only one teacher, and at least two people are required to authentically discuss differing perspectives.

        1.5.3.4.1. Pro:
        Many teachers do not effectively teach issues from multiple perspectives [177].

        1.5.3.4.2. Con:
        Students can present their perspectives via in-class debate and out-of-class homework, with the teacher acting as a moderator.

    1.5.4. Con:
    Children may not have the emotional or intellectual maturity to deal with some complex and nuanced topics.

      1.5.4.1. Pro:
      Children are often not given the tools to appreciate and effectively respond to some of the topics in political matters.

        1.5.4.1.1. Pro:
        The vocabulary of such issues is often beyond that of many children.

          1.5.4.1.1.1. Pro:
          Addressing the use of slurs and aggressive language is an important part of discussion on sensitive issues (p. 2880 [178]); however, these should not be taught to children.

          1.5.4.1.1.2. Con:
          The essence of these issues can be engaged in language acceptable for younger audiences.

        1.5.4.1.2. Con:
        It is possible to simplify these issues so children can understand and engage with them.

          1.5.4.1.2.1. Pro:
          There are many books [179] that address race, gender, and sexuality in ways children can understand.

      1.5.4.2. Con:
      Discussing sensitive social and political topics will help children to mature.

        1.5.4.2.1. Pro:
        Such topics can be thoughtfully framed and introduced [180] in the school setting, which helps students learn a more mature approach to discussions.

        1.5.4.2.2. Con:
        Being exposed to sensitive topics can desensitize [181] children to such issues.

          1.5.4.2.2.1. Pro:
          Exposing children to sensitive topics at a formative age normalises such issues for them, making them less likely to be overwhelmed by related tragedies in the future.

            1.5.4.2.2.1.1. Con:
            In the future they are more prepared to form views on them and engage with them. For example, it is more likely that by then they have gained more critical thinking skills, web search literacy, and media literacy.

          1.5.4.2.2.2. Con:
          It is difficult to desensitise children to sensitive topics because of how complex and varied they are. The topics that children confront in their childhood are likely to have evolved [182] by the time they reach adulthood.

      1.5.4.3. Pro:
      The book "The Political Classroom: Evidence and Ethics in Democratic Education [183]" describes how some students cried while discussing sensitive political matters, which demonstrates their inability to handle different worldviews.

        1.5.4.3.1. Con:
        Crying is a normal and healthy reaction to learning about the tragedies associated with many sensitive topics, such as racial discrimination, hate crimes, etc. [184]

      1.5.4.4. Con:
      The ignorance of educators about the capacity of students to engage in important discussions leads to a lack of concerned and engaged citizens.

        1.5.4.4.1. Con:
        Children are often shielded from exposure to difficult topics by teachers and caregivers alike (p. 34–36 [185]); educator concerns about children engaging with sensitive subjects may be due to an awareness of children's emotional vulnerability rather than ignorance about their capacities.

        1.5.4.4.2. Pro:
        Many adults also lack the ability to engage in sensitive topics in a mature manner, which likely stems from a lack of exposure to such conversations at a younger age.

        1.5.4.4.3. Pro:
        Children are known to possess high memory skills and a strong sense of empathy, suggesting that they do have the intellectual capacity to deal with sensitive topics.

  1.6. Con:
  Parents may not want their children discussing sensitive topics in school.

    1.6.1. Pro:
    School discussions could lead to children developing beliefs on sensitive issues contrary to those of their parents.

      1.6.1.1. Pro:
      Sixty percent [186] of surveyed UK residents want political education to replace religious education, so as to prevent children from adopting contrary religious beliefs.

        1.6.1.1.1. Pro:
        Although it is not part of the national curriculum, religious education has remained a compulsory subject in the UK [187].

        1.6.1.1.2. Con:
        Religious education prepares students [188] for life in a pluralistic society and increases their awareness of world affairs.

      1.6.1.2. Con:
      Schools can inform students and parents in advance about upcoming sensitive discussions, allowing them to opt in or out as they see fit.

        1.6.1.2.1. Con:
        Allowing parents to opt their children out of sensitive topics would widen existing political rifts and pressure teachers and schools [189] to avoid certain topics altogether.

        1.6.1.2.2. Pro:
        Parents are more familiar [190] with the knowledge and maturity levels of their children and are thus better positioned to make decisions than teachers and administrators.

      1.6.1.3. Con:
      With the greater access to information [191] that has been enabled by technology, children today are more likely to develop ideologies that differ from those of their parents [192], so they should have the opportunity to express and support these ideologies independently of their parents or parental influence.

        1.6.1.3.1. Con:
        Discussing such issues in school would require teachers to take greater responsibility for the moral education of their students; a task for which they may not be adequately prepared (p. 3 [193]).

        1.6.1.3.2. Pro:
        Children should not be completely sheltered from reality. While it is beneficial to shield them from shocks and defer some knowledge and details until appropriate for their state of development, censorship and the banning of subjects are wrong.

      1.6.1.4. Con:
      Only providing education in line with parents' beliefs entrenches the ideology of the parents and creates an echo chamber [194], leaving no room for different ideologies that may be more appealing to the youth or even more correct.

        1.6.1.4.1. Con:
        Moral values are relative [195]; the ideologies and values taught by schools are not necessarily better.

        1.6.1.4.2. Pro:
        Every adult that a child meets exerts some effect on them. All adults, parents, and teachers should be able to cope well with questions about sensitive topics, which many teenagers encounter during school, at home, and in the media.

      1.6.1.5. Pro:
      The opinions of teachers on sensitive topics may be at odds with those of parents.

        1.6.1.5.1. Con:
        Teachers do not need to impart their opinions to students in order to teach about sensitive topics.

        1.6.1.5.2. Pro:
        The majority of US parents [196] do not believe that their child's teachers are capable of appropriately addressing sensitive topics.

    1.6.2. Con:
    The public school system guarantees that a child's education is completely independent of their parents.

      1.6.2.1. Con:
      Parental involvement in a child's education is a key factor in their academic success (pp. 1–2 [197]).

        1.6.2.1.1. Pro:
        Children rely upon the support of their parents and are inherently dependent [198] on them.

        1.6.2.1.2. Con:
        Parents can be involved in and support their child's education without exerting undue ideological control over the topics and concepts they study.

      1.6.2.2. Pro:
      Parents' opinions should be weighed against the costs of allowing them to keep their children ignorant.

        1.6.2.2.1. Pro:
        Regardless of parents' opinions, failing to educate children about child abuse prevention [199] places them at unnecessary risk.

        1.6.2.2.2. Con:
        In many cases, it is impossible to foresee the benefits and costs of children's exposure, or lack thereof, to a given topic.

      1.6.2.3. Con:
      Imbuing schools with the responsibility for educating students on moral issues undermines the moral authority [200] and responsibility [201] of parents.

        1.6.2.3.1. Pro:
        Parents have an obligation to oversee the moral development of their children [202], and are better positioned to do so than schools.

        1.6.2.3.2. Con:
        Engaging with different moral perspectives supports children in developing moral autonomy [203].

      1.6.2.4. Pro:
      Most countries [204] have compulsory education [205] laws, which means the state can decide what and when children should learn.

        1.6.2.4.1. Pro:
        Compulsory education supports the right of children to equal opportunity [206].

        1.6.2.4.2. Con:
        Many countries allow homeschooling [207] as an option for parents who want to circumvent this.

          1.6.2.4.2.1. Con:
          Homeschooling outside of medical necessity remains illegal [208] in many countries.

          1.6.2.4.2.2. Pro:
          Homeschooling allows parents more control [209] over what their children learn.

    1.6.3. Pro:
    Parents ultimately have both the right and the responsibility to determine what their children should know and when.

      1.6.3.1. Con:
      Even if parents have the right to control what their children learn, they do not have the right to dictate what schools teach.

        1.6.3.1.1. Con:
        As a primary source of school funding via taxation [210], parents, who are tax-paying citizens, should have the right to help determine school curricula [211].

        1.6.3.1.2. Pro:
        Since education is a public right, the responsibility for developing curricula lies with school, regional, and national authorities [212] rather than parents.
