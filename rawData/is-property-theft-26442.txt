Discussion Name:
Is Property Theft?

1. Thesis:
Property is theft.

  1.1. Con:
  If property is theft, that implies that the property owner somehow stole the material from a previous owner. Thus, the claim contradicts itself. That is, the term "theft" implies some kind of material entitlement to the material in question.

    1.1.1. Con:
    There is an alternate implication here. Property is theft implies that all property belongs to all people of a region and buying a particular part and limiting other people's access to it is theft.

  1.2. Con:
  A society cannot function properly without propriety. Property enables citizens to enjoy the comfort of their life without worrying about losing what they have on the ground that it is not theirs to begin with.

    1.2.1. Con:
    Air and water are acknowledged as shared resources we all depend on, and should anyone claim ownership we would all worry about losing valuable necessities.

      1.2.1.1. Con:
      Air and water are owned by the government, who inflict violence upon those who trespass. It is by government violence in defence of its property that these valuable necessities are preserved for our use.

        1.2.1.1.1. Con:
        While governments may lay claim to the air and water of the lands they control, their doing so does not typically serve the purpose of protecting those resources for everybody's use; environmental protection laws are a recent and relatively small phenomenon. Rather, state ownership of land, water, and air enables the differential and precise control of economic, social, and political activity of people, along lines of citizenship, ethnicity, rurality, etc.

          1.2.1.1.1.1. Con:
          What the predominant use the owners of property have for it isn't relevant to whether said owner actually owns said property.

    1.2.2. Con:
    One need not worry about losing a rental (more than a non-rental) object, despite it not being theirs to being with.

      1.2.2.1. Con:
      Rented property is just another form of property; there must be a landlord to whom the rent must be paid, even if that landlord is a government standing in for "the common good".

    1.2.3. Pro:
    If property was a theft then the whole world would be killing each other over where to live. In my opinion, this is a ridiculous and pointless discussion

  1.3. Con:
  "A is A." Property is property. Theft is theft. To define property as theft is simply not logical.

  1.4. Con:
  Implicit in the statement "property is theft" is the notion that everyone in society owns everything that is ownable, such that taking any exclusive usage of something is to deprive someone (indeed, everyone) of a right to use that property in that particular way. There are moral and practical reasons as to why this view is intuitively problematic.

  1.5. Pro:
  Property is theft only if it doesn’t go entirely back to society after the owner passes away.

  1.6. Pro:
  The air does not and should not belong to anyone. The same applies to everything else.

    1.6.1. Con:
    Some people actually own various gases like helium, hydrogen, oxygen, neon, argon, etc. They store such gases in containers, and there are businesses that deal in such gases.

      1.6.1.1. Con:
      "Air" and "gasses" aren't equivalent. Air is understood to be the mixture of atmospheric gasses that surrounds the earth and inundates the biosphere.

    1.6.2. Pro:
    The air moves according to forces that are not under the human control.

    1.6.3. Con:
    Rights to all types of matter or resource cannot be derived from the belief in the property status of one type of matter or resource.

      1.6.3.1. Pro:
      If nothing outside of person's body can belong to them, then nothing one possesses can belong to one. Such law would be socially undesirable.

    1.6.4. Con:
    "The same applies to everything else" is a non sequitur. Even if the air doesn't and shouldn't belong to anyone, that does not logically imply that the same would be true of everything else. For instance, I breathe air, but I don't breathe cement. Cement and air have different properties and play different roles in human civilization.

    1.6.5. Con:
    In practice, the air belongs to governments [1]. A government may forbid trespass on its airspace, and control who can dump which substances into the atmosphere. If you attempt to trespass on this property without permission - e.g. fly a plane without a pilot's license - you may expect violent coercion against you.

    1.6.6. Con:
    Surely it's not the case that this logic applies to luxury goods like yachts or Rembrandt paintings as well, or to something like a hamburger which can only be consumed by one person.

  1.7. Pro:
  More private property equals less freedom in society.

    1.7.1. Con:
    The Soviet Union, in which owning private property for commercial purposes was forbidden [2], was a totalitarian one-party dictatorship [3].

    1.7.2. Con:
    Even if that's true, it doesn't mean that private property is theft. It merely means that private property amounts to less freedom in society.

    1.7.3. Con:
    Perhaps less private property results in less freedom in society. After all, if the State owns everything, as in communism, the individual isn't particularly free.

    1.7.4. Con:
    The creation of new property doesn't require freedom but labor and resources.

    1.7.5. Pro:
    The existence of private property restricts other people's mobility. People who do not own a particular place can not utilize it.

      1.7.5.1. Con:
      The existence of private property gives people security. People who do not own it can't displace the residents.

    1.7.6. Pro:
    More private property means more capital accumulation and more concentration of economic power. This leads to more social inequalities and less freedom for workers.

  1.8. Pro:
  Property requires appropriation, and nothing but power can justify appropriation. Property thus relies on pure power.

    1.8.1. Con:
    Appropriation can be justifiable without power. What's wrong with picking up an unowned rock at the beach and taking possession of it? What's wrong with finding some unowned wild berries in the woods and eating them? What's wrong with trading for or buying something from someone else if both parties accept the deal?

    1.8.2. Con:
    Even if property relies on pure power, that doesn't logically make it theft. It would just mean that property is a function of power, and that the current owner somehow overpowered the previous owner or simply is the first person to use power to take possession of the property.

    1.8.3. Con:
    Appropriation by legitimate power is itself legitimate.

      1.8.3.1. Con:
      Such legitimacy can only ever be circularly derived.

        1.8.3.1.1. Con:
        The legitimacy of the power of an absolute monarch comes by the grace of God; the derivation is not circular.

          1.8.3.1.1.1. Con:
          The legitimacy of God's grace comes from the institutions that support and benefit from monarchical power.

            1.8.3.1.1.1.1. Con:
            The grace of God cannot be legitimised or delegitimised by mortal men. God (assuming he exists) is the ultimate arbiter of the universe, and does not need support from any institution.

        1.8.3.1.2. Con:
        The legitimacy of the power of a body of elected representatives flows from the consent of the governed as expressed in their votes; its derivation is not circular.

          1.8.3.1.2.1. Con:
          The government grants and delineates the legitimacy of a citizenry's vote.

            1.8.3.1.2.1.1. Con:
            If a government attempts to control the citizenry's vote, by making its elections less free and fair, it loses popular legitimacy.

    1.8.4. Con:
    In societies where land or similar forms of property are expected to have an owner but ceases to have one by some non-violent means (e.g. last owner dies without an heir), the "appropriation" of that property is not a confiscation from the common good by pure power, but a resolution of ambiguity.

    1.8.5. Con:
    Property isn't appropriated. It is purchased as the fruits of individual labor.

      1.8.5.1. Con:
      Only that which is already property can be bought and sold; appropriation is required to create property first.

  1.9. Con:
  One's self, mind and body are also their property, but it does not mean they stole it.

    1.9.1. Con:
    Private property [4] "may consist of real estate, buildings, objects, [or] intellectual property (for example, copyrights or patents)." Your mind and body are part of yourself. You are an individual and cannot legally be owned.

  1.10. Pro:
  There is no property without violence. If I own something, whoever uses it without my permission exposes themselves to physical coercion.

    1.10.1. Con:
    Property can be obtained through non-violent means.

    1.10.2. Con:
    This alone cannot indicate that property is immoral - the same could be said about self-ownership, and yet violence in defence of that ownership is regarded as legitimate.

      1.10.2.1. Con:
      This assumes both that one owns themselves (itself dubious), and that personal self-defense is best understood as protection of that property. This fails to account for the defensive behavior of those who could have not possibly have such a conception of property, for instance, little children and animals, and likewise locates the value of one's life in its being some sort of "auto-investment", a notion that is as alien to day-to-day lived experiences as it is cynical.

        1.10.2.1.1. Con:
        As an explanation for its legitimacy, personal self-defense is best understood as protection of the property one has in one's self-ownership. Questioning whether self-defence is legitimate and where its legitimacy comes from is similarly impossible for small children and animals.

          1.10.2.1.1.1. Con:
          It's only impossible if you assume that self-defense is best understood as a protection of property, which you again assert with no evidence. In any case, a successful model of behavior here is not one that derives "legitimacy", but rather meaningfully predicts people's actions; if you have to ignore children and animals for your analysis to make sense, this is a flaw.

            1.10.2.1.1.1.1. Con:
            The only relevance of self-defence to the discussion here is the question of why self-defence is legitimate, so a model that predicts behaviour but does not derive legitimacy may be an effective model for predicting behavious, but is perfectly irrelevant to the question at hand.

        1.10.2.1.2. Con:
        Capitalist investment is not the only use and purpose for owning things, so the claim that self-ownership reduces the value of one's life to an investment is a non sequitur.

  1.11. Con:
  Purchasing or owning things are more intrinsic thoughts, because the society in which we belong to has assigned value to everything and so we must possess to exist.

    1.11.1. Pro:
    If you purchase something, then you have stolen your time to earn money to buy the thing.

      1.11.1.1. Con:
      I can't steal my own time and space.

        1.11.1.1.1. Con:
        Work is not something that you want to do it is something that you have to do. Work is a theft of your personal time so that you can afford to buy the things that you want. So anytime you make a purchase you are stealing more personal time away from yourself because you will have to work longer.

    1.11.2. Pro:
    If you own several things you need a place to store them so they are also stealing your space. They also cause you to worry about their protection from actual thieves so they steal your calm.

  1.12. Con:
  There is no alternative.

    1.12.1. Con:
    Alternatives are both easily imagined (as in any number of anticapitalist ideologies) and attested throughout history—both in terms of historical societies operating without anything we'd call property, and in terms of many specific material relations conducted every day without regard to propriety.

    1.12.2. Pro:
    Property is not exclusive to humans. Many social species are territorial [5].

      1.12.2.1. Pro:
      Territoriality [6] evolved as a mechanism, along with others such as dominance hierarchies and ritualised aggression, that minimizes the need for mortal combat any time two individuals want the same limited resource on an area of land.

  1.13. Con:
  Property originally comes about by the exertion of labor upon natural resources (labor theory of property [7]).

    1.13.1. Pro:
    Except soil and commodities, usually goods are not natural but Artificial, hence with human work. Human work is individually owned by each person. The assembling of any product is obtained through the human work (physical or intellectual) which is not publicly available.

    1.13.2. Con:
    If this were true, then the fact that the vast majority of property is not owned by those who themselves exerted their labor upon natural resources would lead us to conclude that this property, though perhaps not in of itself theft, is nonetheless thieved.

  1.14. Con:
  Property is established through official or unofficial agreement.

    1.14.1. Con:
    It is no one's place to sell the commons. Property is established through violence: violent accumulation of resources; violent occupation of lands; coercion of labor to produce capital. Indeed, this violence is often "official", that is, legitimized by the laws of those who would hold property.

      1.14.1.1. Con:
      Acquisition of property generally occurs through an exchange of (finite) items, whether this is money that has been awarded to you in exchange for your (finite) time or otherwise. This constitutes a moral (labour, gifts) exchange. The existence of immoral (war, conquest, theft) ways of acquiring property does not invalidate the moral ways of acquisition.

    1.14.2. Pro:
    Everybody lays claim to something, however small; when everybody has ownership over something, it is a tremendous equaliser.

  1.15. Con:
  Property is the main incentive driving human progress.

    1.15.1. Con:
    This does not refute the original claim; perhaps it is the case that human progress is motivated by thievery.

    1.15.2. Con:
    While many of the material changes of the last few centuries were driven by ideologies of property-accumulation (namely, capitalism and mercantilism), these institutions (and their supporting notions of property) were not present during many other moments of "progress", for instance, the neolithic and agricultural revolutions.

      1.15.2.1. Con:
      In comparison to the timescale of human progress since the industrial revolution, the first agricultural revolution - a single technology spreading over thousands of years - was astoundingly slow.

    1.15.3. Con:
    Culture is the main incentive [8] driving human progress.
