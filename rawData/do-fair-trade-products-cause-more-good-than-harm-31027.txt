Discussion Name:
Do fair trade products cause more good than harm?

1. Thesis:
Fair trade products cause more good than harm.

  1.1. Pro:
  Fair trade products have helped create more ethical consumers by increasing awareness regarding ethical trade practices [1].

    1.1.1. Con:
    Purchasing fair trade products can lead consumers to think that they have done enough good for the world, making them complacent on other ethical issues.

      1.1.1.1. Pro:
      Though fair trade is not the only factor to consider in ethical consumption, some people consider it to be [2].

        1.1.1.1.1. Con:
        The Fairtrade movement itself is regularly accused [3] of working with companies that are either considered historically unethical, or symbols of the unfairness of the international trading system.

        1.1.1.1.2. Pro:
        Everyday decisions [4] - such as deciding to use public transport or walk, to shop locally, or to buy second hand clothes - are all examples of ethical consumerism that people could implement in their lives.

      1.1.1.2. Pro:
      The reduction of waste is an important part [5] of ethical consumption. The fair trade movement encourages consumerism, which distracts consumers from reducing the waste that they create.

        1.1.1.2.1. Con:
        Consumers would still buy coffee or chocolate if a fair trade option wasn't available. Fair trade offers a green alternative to goods people would buy no matter what.

        1.1.1.2.2. Pro:
        Buying local is better for waste reduction [6] than buying fair trade products, which some people may not be aware of due to the lack of consolidated branding for locally-produced goods.

      1.1.1.3. Con:
      Ethically-minded individuals deliberately choose to buy fair trade products for this reason and, as such, are likely to be more mindful in general of the many ways they can lessen their consumer impact on the world.

        1.1.1.3.1. Pro:
        Those who show a preference for purchasing fair trade products are more likely to be altruistic overall (p. 10 [7]).

        1.1.1.3.2. Pro:
        Market research has shown that general awareness and preference for sustainability [8] in consumers has increased increased in recent years.

        1.1.1.3.3. Con:
        Ethically-minded individuals [9] are more likely to participate in and support greenwashing (pp. 227-228 [10]). In general, they attempt to convince their own ego [11] that they're lessening their carbon footprint when in reality, their very existence [12] has a harmful impact [13] on the planet.

    1.1.2. Pro:
    Fair trade products help promote sustainable farming and have a positive impact on the environment.

      1.1.2.1. Con:
      Fairtrade does not assist [14] the poorest of the poor, and in some cases, discriminates against them, making them worse off [15] than uncertified, conventional producers.

        1.1.2.1.1. Pro:
        Fair trade's high certification fees have been shown to decrease the average income [16] of many of the world's poorest workers, especially coffee farmers [17].

        1.1.2.1.2. Pro:
        The benefits received from fair trade often assist only the most skilled [18] coffee growers, and tend to exclude the absolute poorest farmers.

        1.1.2.1.3. Con:
        The Fairtrade Foundation actively targets the improvement and sustainability of the world's poorest [19] traders.

      1.1.2.2. Pro:
      Fairtrade certification requires producers to follow Fairtrade Standards [20], which cover key areas of environmental protection and agricultural practice [21].

      1.1.2.3. Con:
      Fairtrade rules hinder agricultural development by limiting the size of eligible farms and actively discourage [22] more modern farming techniques [23] that might give workers an opportunity to break out of the poverty cycle.

        1.1.2.3.1. Pro:
        Mechanisation increases [24] efficiency and individual productivity, as well as overall farm income.

        1.1.2.3.2. Con:
        Mechanisation displaces [25] labour on farms and pushes farm workers into poverty.

        1.1.2.3.3. Pro:
        Drum-seeding technology on a Bangladeshi farm was shown to increase farmers' profits (p. 77 [26]).

      1.1.2.4. Con:
      Given that fair trade products cannot be grown in the UK or EU [27], they have to travel a great distance to get to their end consumer; shipping cargo [28] has an inherent negative impact on the environment.

        1.1.2.4.1. Con:
        Goods made or grown overseas will be shipped to the UK and EU regardless of their fair trade status; thus, goods with fair trade status are still an overall net benefit.

        1.1.2.4.2. Pro:
        Having the option of fair trade products could dissuade consumers from buying locally [29].

      1.1.2.5. Pro:
      Some Fairtrade co-operatives [30] invest their Fairtrade Premium [31] in reforestation [32] projects.

        1.1.2.5.1. Con:
        Reforestation projects are not the same [33] as forest restoration; the latter is more important for the long-term sustainability of local communities.

        1.1.2.5.2. Pro:
        As many fair trade farmers are threatened by climate change [34], replacing depleted forests is in their best interests.

    1.1.3. Con:
    There is no such thing [35] as being an ethical consumer in the global capitalist trade economy.

      1.1.3.1. Con:
      Ethics are ultimately relative [36]. Given that the global capitalist system is unlikely to be dismantled, individuals should be allowed to make ethical decisions – to the extent of their desire – within it.

        1.1.3.1.1. Pro:
        Fairtrade allows both producers [37] and consumers [38] a degree of control over the ethical decisions they make.

        1.1.3.1.2. Con:
        Economists [39] have warned that capitalism, in its current form, is unsustainable [40].

      1.1.3.2. Pro:
      Under a capitalist system, all profits [41] are wages not paid to workers at the production level.

        1.1.3.2.1. Pro:
        Even with fair trade systems in place, governments and large corporations profit substantially more than primary producers (p. 21 [42]).

        1.1.3.2.2. Con:
        Fair trade systems ensure that producers recieve more of the profits from their efforts than they ordinarily would; while a capitalist society prevails, ensuring a higher profit to primary producers in this manner can help alleviate some of the damage it causes.

        1.1.3.2.3. Con:
        The fact that shoppers are increasingly willing [43] to pay a premium for fair trade produce indicates a willingness to deviate from a traditional capitalist structure.

    1.1.4. Pro:
    Fairtrade organisations have highlighted the ethical problems [44] attached to some popular consumable items.

      1.1.4.1. Pro:
      Once consumers are a part of the fair trade regime, they challenge [45] manufacturers and retailers to guarantee the ethical claims they are making about their products.

      1.1.4.2. Pro:
      Fair Trade certifications provide information that creates transparency within supply chains, allowing consumers to choose between ethical and unethical businesses (p. 233 [46]).

        1.1.4.2.1. Pro:
        The transparency created by Fairtrade even extends into problematic sectors like gold mining [47], creating awareness beyond typical farming applications.

        1.1.4.2.2. Con:
        A company making a fair trade product does not automatically make said company ethical. Oftentimes, ethical brands are bought out and acquired [48] by larger corporations with dubious ethical backgrounds.

      1.1.4.3. Con:
      Fairtrade products are not always entirely ethical themselves: companies have been able to sell products as 'Fairtrade [49]' when they feature only one fair trade ingredient or constituent part.

        1.1.4.3.1. Pro:
        Entire brands can claim to be Fair Trade whilst only using a nominal amount [50] of fairly traded ingredients.

        1.1.4.3.2. Pro:
        Due to product storage and processing methods, items that claim to be Fairtrade are not actually guaranteed [51] to be.

        1.1.4.3.3. Con:
        The inclusion of at least one fair trade ingredient is more ethical than the inclusion of none.

  1.2. Con:
  The Fairtrade business model creates unsustainable business relationships [52] that undermine local economies and market systems.

    1.2.1. Con:
    Fair trade organisations help producers by increasing their knowledge [53] about the market and teaching them business skills to help producers to build, improve, and maintain profitable businesses.

      1.2.1.1. Con:
      The Fairtrade model doesn't encourage producers to focus on the quality [54] of the goods they produce. Making quality products creates better relationships with consumers.

        1.2.1.1.1. Con:
        Fairtrade encourages farmers to reinvest [55] the premiums they receive in incentives that will improve product quality and productivity.

        1.2.1.1.2. Pro:
        Because Fair Trade premiums are usually a fixed amount, there is little incentive for farmers to create higher quality products (p. 225 [56]).

      1.2.1.2. Con:
      The Fairtrade model doesn't teach producers how to evaluate and grade [57] their own coffee, skills necessary for running a successful coffee-growing business.

        1.2.1.2.1. Pro:
        Coffee grading enables farmers to identify the quality [58] of their beans and the standard of coffee it will produce; higher grade quality coffee allows for greater profits and subsequent reinvestment [59] at the farm level.

        1.2.1.2.2. Con:
        Coffee grading is an arbitrary system [60] that has no international standard.

      1.2.1.3. Pro:
      Organisations operating under fair trade practices commit to long-term trading partnerships [61], which creates secure and reliable business relationships with producers.

      1.2.1.4. Pro:
      Fairtrade allows producers' to improve their business and negotiation skills, allowing them to reduce their dependency on singular buyers and thus expand their client base (p. 6 [62]).

        1.2.1.4.1. Pro:
        Farmers in poorer nations often do not have access to the business skills necessary to increase their own and their communities' wellfare (p. 804 [63]).

        1.2.1.4.2. Con:
        A number of Farmers' Organizations across developing nations also widely offer such training in an efficient and cost-effective manner (p. 626 [64]).

    1.2.2. Pro:
    Fairtrade locks [65] developing countries in the primary sector commodities market, which limits how much the country can develop and grow.

      1.2.2.1. Con:
      Fairtrade certification helps stabilise the incomes [66] of farmers, allowing many families to keep their children in school, stimulating both present and future economic growth [67].

        1.2.2.1.1. Con:
        Only a certain percentage of products are sold in Fairtrade markets (p. 54 [68]), leaving producers without any guarantees [69] that they will successfully sell all of their stock.

        1.2.2.1.2. Pro:
        When the average number of years of schooling in a country increases, the economy grows at a higher annual rate [70] over subsequent decades.

        1.2.2.1.3. Con:
        Obtaining Fairtrade certification is extremely harmful to small-scale farmers in the developing world.

          1.2.2.1.3.1. Pro:
          In some years, the cost of certification [71] is more than the revenue earned by farmers; obtaining Fairtrade certification can actually result in a decrease in small-scale farmers' income (p. 3 [72]).

          1.2.2.1.3.2. Con:
          Small farmers in Latin America and Africa benefit [73] from the minimum price guaranteed by Fairtrade and its co-operatives.

      1.2.2.2. Pro:
      Western countries manufacture goods using the raw materials produced by fair trade workers in developing countries; the profits from those products stay in the Western world [74].

        1.2.2.2.1. Pro:
        Despite 90% of cocoa being grown [75] in developing countries, just 4% of chocolate is produced there. This means that the West receives the largest profit from their value-added conversion of the goods originally produced by Fairtrade farmers.

        1.2.2.2.2. Pro:
        Fairtrade actively discourages farms from investing in mechanisation and industrialisation, both of which would increase [76] manufacturing capabilities and subsequently lead to increased profits from the sale of manufactured goods compared to basic commodities.

        1.2.2.2.3. Con:
        Some successful joint ventures have allowed the majority of the profits to remain in the developing countries producing the raw goods (p. 24 [77]).

      1.2.2.3. Pro:
      An economy dependent on basic commodities is much more vulnerable [78] to international market prices and shocks than diversified economies.

        1.2.2.3.1. Pro:
        Volatility in primary product markets can have long-term, detrimental effect [79] on the stability of producing economies.

        1.2.2.3.2. Con:
        Fairtrade actively works towards stabilising product prices globally to negotiate market fluctuations (p. 8 [80]).

        1.2.2.3.3. Con:
        Western consumers are willing to pay a premium for fair trade goods (p. 2 [81]). Their openness to paying more can support such economies, even during tough economic times.

      1.2.2.4. Con:
      Advancement into secondary and tertiary markets is linked to per capita income [82], reducing the competitiveness of the majority of the countries producing fair trade products due to their relative poverty and low rates of industrialisation.

        1.2.2.4.1. Pro:
        Fairtrade has been shown to increase per capita income, which would help facilitate a move into other economies (p. 34 [83]).

      1.2.2.5. Con:
      Most of these countries were locked in primary sector markets long before the concept of Fairtrade was developed. Therefore, their lack of growth cannot be blamed on Fairtrade.

        1.2.2.5.1. Con:
        Fairtrade could lay down standards and minimum pricing for secondary products in order to alleviate the problem. However, it has yet to do so (p. 1 [84]).

        1.2.2.5.2. Pro:
        Western consumerism locked [85] many developing countries into primary sector markets as early as the nineteenth century. In contrast, the concept of Fairtrade [86] was developed in the late twentieth century.

    1.2.3. Con:
    By improving and stabilising the revenues of producers in developing countries, fair trade directly complements efforts towards community development and social welfare infrastructure [87] within developing countries.

      1.2.3.1. Pro:
      Fair trade products and profits have helped rebuild conflict-ridden countries all over the world.

        1.2.3.1.1. Pro:
        Fair trade premiums in Palestine have helped pay for olive groves [88] that were destroyed by the Israeli army.

        1.2.3.1.2. Pro:
        The production of fair trade raisins in post-war Afghanistan [89] has helped local communities build schools and rebuild infrastructure that was destroyed during the war period.

        1.2.3.1.3. Con:
        Conflict-ridden zones need to be rebuilt by governments and non-profits focused on repairing infrastructure, while individuals can only do so much; the profits from fair trade, if kept by the workers, are secondary.

      1.2.3.2. Con:
      Fairtrade maintains developing countries in a disadvantageous [90] economic specialisation. It would be more beneficial to them to export manufactured goods instead of agricultural products [91].

        1.2.3.2.1. Pro:
        Research has shown that developing economies that rely on agricultural production develop at a slower rate compared to those that rely on manufacturing (p. 98 [92]).

        1.2.3.2.2. Con:
        Developing nations largely have inefficient manufacturing [93] sectors. In contrast, agriculture is an established sector in many developing nations and its continued development [94] is effective in both poverty reduction and economic growth.

      1.2.3.3. Pro:
      Fair trade producers reinvest [95] some of the profits in their local communities in order to build schools and support educational purposes.

        1.2.3.3.1. Pro:
        Around one-quarter of Fairtrade premiums on plantations have been used for educational purposes (p. 4 [96]).

        1.2.3.3.2. Con:
        The production of Fairtrade goods in Costa Rica had negative impacts on the education of unskilled coffee workers’ children (p. 3 [97]).

        1.2.3.3.3. Con:
        A significant amount of funding is invested [98] in coffee cooperatives’ buildings and salaries, rather than in schools.

        1.2.3.3.4. Pro:
        In several countries, Fairtrade premiums have been reinvested to fund [99] new schools [100], repair existing ones, purchase school supplies, as well as supporting scholarship programs to ensure the workers and their families continue their education.

    1.2.4. Pro:
    Fair trade distorts the free market system [101] by enabling singular entities [102] to influence and control the market, increasing inefficiency, corruption, and unfair business practices [103].

      1.2.4.1. Pro:
      Fair trade buyer associations are incentivised to keep the price of fair trade coffee artificially inflated, they therefore intentionally restrict the supply [104] of coffee that can be labeled as certified, limiting [105] fair trade farmers' ability to chose buyers who offer them the best price for their product.

        1.2.4.1.1. Pro:
        The minimum price [106] guaranteed by Fairtrade encourages producers to create an unsustainable oversupply [107] of goods, resulting in a depression of global commodity prices and harming the rate of pay for non-Fairtrade suppliers.

        1.2.4.1.2. Con:
        Fair trade coffee growers sell lower quality [108] coffee to co-operatives, and sell higher quality coffee directly to private traders for higher prices.

      1.2.4.2. Pro:
      Resistance to unfair business practices is growing (pp. 345-346 [109]); as more people realize how these issues affect small producers, people may become less willing to purchase goods with Fairtrade branding.

        1.2.4.2.1. Pro:
        Exploitative monopolies risk losing market share [110] as consumer preference, especially among the vast majority of millennials [111], has shifted to responsibly-sourced products that are obtained through equitable means.

        1.2.4.2.2. Con:
        Fairtrade has committed to continually identify and counter unfair trade practices (pp. 1-2 [112]).

  1.3. Pro:
  Fair trade products lead to more equitable global trade.

    1.3.1. Pro:
    Fair trade helps establish more humane labour norms in the developing world.

      1.3.1.1. Pro:
      Fairtrade can support [113] workers in learning to realize their rights and negotiating the terms and conditions of their work.

        1.3.1.1.1. Pro:
        A Fairtrade buyer and their allies in the labour movement at a banana plantation in Cameroon came together to support the creation [114] of a joint union platform, strengthening the union workers' negotiating position.

        1.3.1.1.2. Con:
        Many workers on Fairtrade plantations are migrants struggling to obtain [115] legal status and therefore do not have significant employment rights.

      1.3.1.2. Pro:
      Fair trade prohibits [116] the use of children for cheap labor.

        1.3.1.2.1. Con:
        There is evidence [117] that children have been paid to work for Fairtrade producers.

        1.3.1.2.2. Pro:
        Child labor is a big problem [118] within the developing [119] world. Fair trade practices can help eliminate [120] this problem over time.

      1.3.1.3. Con:
      Fair Trade is based on private rules or standards that are concluded between parties, whereas equitable trade targets global standards. It is unlikely that a private trade regime will ensure equity for the entire arena of global trade (pp. 14-15 [121]).

        1.3.1.3.1. Pro:
        Countries that are already a part of an existing trade regime are unlikely to agree on the reforms in tariffs, market access, and liberalization that are required under fair trade policies to reach an equitable trading system.

          1.3.1.3.1.1. Pro:
          The European Union imposes [122] mandatory tariffs on goods that are imported from developing countries. Countries that are long established trading partners of the Union are unlikely to introduce Fair Trade reformations in their trading policies (p. 15 [123]).

      1.3.1.4. Con:
      There is no guarantee [124] that fair trade will improve working conditions compared to what individuals would have had otherwise.

        1.3.1.4.1. Pro:
        Fair trade often applies only to the production of raw materials [125] rather than to finished goods. For example, fair trade certified cotton could end up in a t-shirt manufactured in a sweatshop.

        1.3.1.4.2. Pro:
        Revised Fairtrade Standards in 2016 failed to benefit garment workers [126].

        1.3.1.4.3. Con:
        Given that improving working conditions is one of the main pillars [127] of the Fairtrade Standards, it is unlikely that they would worsen working conditions for individuals, especially compared to their non-Fairtrade counterparts.

        1.3.1.4.4. Con:
        Fairtrade employs current and former agricultural workers on their Board of Executives [128], who can voice their opinions and stick up for the rights of their fellow workers.

    1.3.2. Pro:
    Fairtrade International empowers [129] communities to organise into co-operatives and improve their negotiating position within the supply chain.

      1.3.2.1. Pro:
      Fair trade products and initiatives have helped empower female labourers around the world.

        1.3.2.1.1. Con:
        In developing countries, Fairtrade does not do enough to empower women laborers.

          1.3.2.1.1.1. Pro:
          Fairtrade co-operatives usually require [130] their members to own the land that they farm on. In many countries, women face barriers toward land ownership, and so end up losing out on Fairtrade certifications.

            1.3.2.1.1.1.1. Pro:
            In Ghana [131], cultural and social norms make it difficult for women to own property, and in many cases exclude women from truly benefiting from Fairtrade-certified operations.

            1.3.2.1.1.1.2. Pro:
            Research into the impacts of fair trade has not found any evidence [132] of Fairtrade improving women’s access to land (p. 5).

          1.3.2.1.1.2. Con:
          Even if Fairtrade certification is not currently working for female labourers, it at least sets out the empowerment of women as one of its goals [133]. This means it is more likely to adapt to overcome these problems than regular global trade, which does not [134] usually consider the empowerment of women as important.

          1.3.2.1.1.3. Pro:
          Women in developing countries have to face several barriers to work - such as being restricted to working in the day [135], or needing their husbands permission [136] - which fair trade has not been able to help them overcome.

            1.3.2.1.1.3.1. Pro:
            Fairtrade certifications often require women to commit to working a certain number of hours. This ignores the fact that women in developing countries often face the triple burden [137] of working while also running households and looking after children.

            1.3.2.1.1.3.2. Con:
            There have been many improvements [138] recently, and many countries which previously did not allow women to work without their husband's permission have progressively granted rights to women.

        1.3.2.1.2. Pro:
        Fair trade has helped reduce male dominance in different sectors across developing countries by empowering women to overcome historical and current barriers to work.

          1.3.2.1.2.1. Pro:
          The Fairtrade premium [139] is often utilised as a source of seed-funding for gender equality initiatives [140] and programs.

            1.3.2.1.2.1.1. Pro:
            Many Fairtrade farms use their premiums on Women’s Empowerment Programs [141] in areas like family planning, health, education, career development [142], and housing to assist women in need.

            1.3.2.1.2.1.2. Pro:
            Fairtrade has helped to restore some of the historical lack of access of women to agricultural resources [143] and assets [144].

            1.3.2.1.2.1.3. Con:
            Research shows that women are often underrepresented in decision-making in relation to the use of fair trade premiums, which may often result in projects being designed that are more beneficial to men than to women (p. 5 [145]).

          1.3.2.1.2.2. Pro:
          Fairtrade can help set up rules and provisions [146] that help combat gender-based discrimination and violence within the agricultural sector.

          1.3.2.1.2.3. Con:
          There remains a lack of explicit gender policies and implementation strategies among most fair trade producers and organisations (p. 7 [147]).

          1.3.2.1.2.4. Con:
          Social norms and the gendered division of labour in agriculture constrain women’s ability [148] to benefit from Fairtrade to the same extent as men.

            1.3.2.1.2.4.1. Pro:
            Within Chile [149]'s fair trade fruit-picking sector 75% of women work more than 60 hours a week in season, on temporary contracts, and a third do not earn the minimum wage.

          1.3.2.1.2.5. Con:
          Fair trade organizations do not do enough to directly attack the root causes [150] of gender inequality [151] in agriculture.

        1.3.2.1.3. Pro:
        In many developing countries [152], fair trade has enabled women producers to organize in groups for greater access to markets, which has improved their economic and social situations (p. 80 [153]).

        1.3.2.1.4. Con:
        The impact of fair trade on female workers is largely dependent on existing local arrangements such as the sexual division of labour, attitudes on gender, and the ability of women to own and control the means of production (p. 763 [154]).

      1.3.2.2. Con:
      Farmers who do not participate in fair trade practices are also able to successfully negotiate higher prices for their products.

        1.3.2.2.1. Pro:
        Non-Fairtrade Bolivian coffee producers [155] were able to negotiate higher prices—often more than what Fairtrade farmers received.

        1.3.2.2.2. Con:
        Without the power of the collective [156], negotiating prices is more difficult. In this sense, Fairtrade is more useful for individual producers.

    1.3.3. Con:
    The problem of inequitable global trade is more concerned with politics [157] than trade relations.

      1.3.3.1. Con:
      Economics, including trade relations, and politics are strongly related [158].

        1.3.3.1.1. Pro:
        Fairtrade recognises and supports [159] governmental efforts to make international trade more equitable.

      1.3.3.2. Pro:
      At its core [160], inequitable trade is the excessive concentration of markets in the hands of powerful transnational corporations, which dictate the terms of trade and absorb most of the profits. In many countries, these exploitative corporations are supported and subsidized [161] by the state.

        1.3.3.2.1. Pro:
        More than half the world's trade takes place between G8 countries [162], with developing economies struggling to match them.

        1.3.3.2.2. Con:
        The presence and popularity of Fairtrade sends a powerful message to politicians [163] that consumers demand reform of inequitable trade practices.

    1.3.4. Con:
    Fairtrade is mainly profitable [164] for traders in rich countries and richer farmers.

      1.3.4.1. Con:
      Economic inequality is largely driven by unequal ownership of capital (p. 14 [165]), which is rectified to a large degree by Fair Trade.

        1.3.4.1.1. Con:
        In recent years [166], Fairtrade International began to allow major multinational corporations to join its trade regime. This means that the competition between poor producers and large multinational corporations in international markets is still likely to continue being unfair.

        1.3.4.1.2. Con:
        Fairtrade coffee farms are prohibited [167] from employing any full-time workers and Fairtrade certifiers do not monitor or enforce how much farmers pay [168] temporary workers employed on short-term contracts to harvest the farm produce in developing countries. This is detrimental to job and wage growth and stability in the long term.

          1.3.4.1.2.1. Pro:
          A study in Ethiopia and Uganda [169] showed that the wages of workers in fair trade dominated sites were much lower than those in other locations.

          1.3.4.1.2.2. Pro:
          When comparing larger, commercial, non-certified farms, as well as smallholder sites, wages within the same sectors were generally lower [170] in the areas dominated by Fairtrade producer organisations.

          1.3.4.1.2.3. Con:
          Without a job in the short term, there cannot be a focus on the long term. As long as people are employed and making a livable wage, that's all that matters in the short term (pp. 189-190 [171]).

        1.3.4.1.3. Con:
        Fair trade has failed to produce a successful or meaningful reallocation of global capital.

        1.3.4.1.4. Pro:
        Fair trade practices cut out the exploitative middleman [172] between producers and distributors in order to make global trade more equitable.

          1.3.4.1.4.1. Con:
          Fairtrade does not cap or monitor the amount of money that retailers charge for Fairtrade goods, allowing most of the profits to be kept by middlemen [173] or large corporations (p. 5 [174]).

          1.3.4.1.4.2. Con:
          Fairtrade funds meant for community development and support have allegedly [175] been illegally distributed as bribes, further exploiting local markets with already-thin resources.

          1.3.4.1.4.3. Pro:
          Fair trade organisations trade directly with producers, which keeps prices low for consumers whilst maximising producer profit (p. 2 [176]).

        1.3.4.1.5. Pro:
        Fairtrade provides producers with a minimum price guarantee [177], which means that farmers are able to have more secure and stable incomes.

          1.3.4.1.5.1. Con:
          Fair trade producers have significantly higher costs [178], such as paying for the certification and meeting necessary standards (pp. 11, 20 [179]). This means that, even though producers are guaranteed a higher wage, they don't necessarily financially benefit from producing fair trade goods.

          1.3.4.1.5.2. Pro:
          Fair trade allows farmers' co-operatives to attain higher prices [180] and retain access to markets.

          1.3.4.1.5.3. Con:
          Fairtrade standards only guarantee the minimum price to farming co-operatives [181], which has been diverted [182] from poorer individual farmers who would have otherwise received the extra support.

          1.3.4.1.5.4. Pro:
          Stable and secure incomes increase investments, which increase the efficiency and productivity [183] of producers (p. 14 [184]).

      1.3.4.2. Pro:
      Fairtrade co-operatives are required to meet costly quality [185] and certification [186] standards, which means their farmers must be relatively skillful, educated, and generally well-funded.

        1.3.4.2.1. Pro:
        Countries ranked [187] by the World Bank as upper middle-income contain 54% of all producer organisations that have Fairtrade certification. In comparison, low-income countries contain only 21% of Fairtrade producers.

        1.3.4.2.2. Con:
        Fairtrade allows for farmers to improve equipment [188] so as to be able to compete with large corporations.

        1.3.4.2.3. Pro:
        Fair trade standards are either met mostly by suppliers that have a higher income or middle income in developing countries, or by nations that have a high average GDP per capita (pp. 63-64 [189]).

        1.3.4.2.4. Con:
