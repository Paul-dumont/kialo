Discussion Name:
Can evolutionary ethics provide a sufficient explanation of our moral experience?

1. Thesis:
Evolution does not explain the existence of morals.

  1.1. Pro:
  Morality involving evolution is a stolen principle from theism [1].

    1.1.1. Con:
    Morality can be found in non-human species [2], and we have no good reasons to believe these species are theistic.

      1.1.1.1. Pro:
      No non-human species are recognized to hold any beliefs in god(s).

      1.1.1.2. Pro:
      Non-human species can be observed acting in altruistic ways [3] for both their kin and other species.

      1.1.1.3. Pro:
      Evolutionary ethics predicts non-human species could develop similar ethical behaviors as humans.

    1.1.2. Con:
    Morals developed first, and religion [4] seems to be a by-product of our moral evolution.

      1.1.2.1. Pro:
      Certain ethical behaviors are found to be far older than even the earliest proto-religions.

      1.1.2.2. Pro:
      Religions are an attempt by humans to try and standardize certain perceived moral actions.

      1.1.2.3. Pro:
      Proto-religions are found to be formed from beliefs in ancestor worship, seeking guidance on ethical ideas and more from dead elders.

      1.1.2.4. Pro:
      Religion is one of many social constructs that develop in order to apply pressure to certain parameters of decisions made by individuals within a large population.

    1.1.3. Con:
    Theism pretends to "own" naturally evolved morals.

      1.1.3.1. Pro:
      Theist claim without evidence that morals are dictated by a god.

    1.1.4. Con:
    All things aside, this is a genetic fallacy [5], as it argues that an idea "stealing" its basis from another source renders that idea wrong.

    1.1.5. Con:
    "Stolen Principle" does not seem to be a relevant observation to the value of claims relating to ethics and morality in the context of evolution. Claims should be evaluated logically, independent of their sources.

    1.1.6. Con:
    If there was no sense of good and bad, the image of God or the Gods could not have been refined. Therefore some form of ethics must predate theism.

      1.1.6.1. Con:
      If a God existed as described in the Bible, there would be no need for refinement as the ethics would be given by the God for us to interpret, not create.

        1.1.6.1.1. Con:
        There is no good reason to conclude the God as described in the Bible to be true.

        1.1.6.1.2. Con:
        The God described in the Bible shows contradictory and convoluted ethical actions and judgments.

        1.1.6.1.3. Con:
        There seems philosophically no good reason for any God to have ethics, as such is determined by the pressures on an organism via the conditions by which it survives.

        1.1.6.1.4. Con:
        The God described in the Bible prescribes ethical actions that humans have determined to be unethical.

        1.1.6.1.5. Con:
        The ethical teachings as prescribed in the Bible such as Psalms 2:8-9 [6], John 8:44 [7], Exodus 22:18 [8], Mark 7:2 [9] etc. are crude and unrefined moral teachings.

  1.2. Con:
  Evolutionary ethics is by far the best explanation for ethics, the development of ethics, and why there are similar and different standards of ethics across all species.

    1.2.1. Pro:
    Several studies [10] suggest that we share evolutionary [11] roots for morality [12] and ethics [13] that extend far back along the phylogenetic tree.

  1.3. Pro:
  Morality/ethics is about how one aught to act. Any evolutionary explanation would inherently make it a description of why we act, and a description of what we feel we aught to do. This is a categorical change from morals/ethics actually are making them incompatible and contradictory and thus incoherent. Once a delusion's source is explained, it is then evidently not reality.

    1.3.1. Con:
    Explaining away is still explaining, so the fact that morality would be "a delusion" if explained by evolution does not mean evolution can't explain it.

    1.3.2. Con:
    We all feel that we ought to act in certain ways. Evolution can help explain those feelings and thus help give an account of de facto ethics.

  1.4. Con:
  Humanity is morally flawed, and those flaws are best explained by the fact that our moral experience was crafted by an amoral process of evolution.

    1.4.1. Pro:
    Humans are often hypocritical. From an evolutionary perspective, it is best to be outwardly "good" to accrue allies and respect and rise in society, but to act selfishly for personal benefit whenever one can get away with it.

    1.4.2. Pro:
    Human moral feeling is often parochial - we care much more about our friends and neighbours than distant strangers. This makes perfect sense evolutionarily, as our friends and neighbours are often genetically similar so helping them helps our genes; a more abstract and universal morality would consider this a flaw, however, as everyone is equally a sentient person.

  1.5. Pro:
  Only human beings can be moral despite evolution effecting all living creatures. Therefore, morality and evolution have nothing to do with each other.

    1.5.1. Con:
    Insofar as "only humans can be moral" has any validity, it is in our ability to philosophise about what the ethical thing to do is - i.e. in our species' exceptional intelligence. As our intelligence is an evolved trait [14], this renders the argument incoherent.

  1.6. Pro:
  Evolution seems to not account for why when we contemplate doing something immoral, we developed strong intuitions that the act is immoral rather than developing a brain system that physically prevents us from doing the act.

    1.6.1. Pro:
    If immoral behaviors harm the reproductive fitness of a population, evolution would favor traits that are most effective at curbing immoral behavior. A physical compulsion to not do the act would be more effective than a mere moral intuition, and thus should have been favored.

    1.6.2. Con:
    Even if the complete abolition of certain immoral actions was favoured by evolution, such a mechanism would be needlessly complex compared to just making the intuitions strong enough that people never encounter temptations strong enough to overcome them.

      1.6.2.1. Con:
      We have already evolved strong emotional and physical responses to outside stimuli and events, such as vomiting when we see something disgusting. It is not too great a leap to imagine an evolved bodily response to certain immoral actions.

    1.6.3. Con:
    We consider doing immoral things only when there is a benefit to it, something that tempts us. Our desires are also evolved, so this equates to a tradeoff between two benefits. Evolution only cares about morality insofar as it is pragmatically beneficial, so has no reason to make us always take the moral side of such tradeoffs.

      1.6.3.1. Con:
      Murderers often commit murder without receiving any benefit near the evolutionary value of a human life.

        1.6.3.1.1. Pro:
        Murders are frequently [15] motivated merely by personal conflict.

        1.6.3.1.2. Pro:
        The negative consequences of murder are not innate or certain, but rather are a result of the context of the killing and the judgement of the society the crime took place in.

        1.6.3.1.3. Con:
        The life of an unrelated stranger has little to no evolutionary value to an individual.

    1.6.4. Con:
    The most that evolution could possibly do is offer strong intuitions to choose or not to choose to do an act. Evolution could not evolve a brain that made it impossible to do an act.

      1.6.4.1. Pro:
      Whilst evolution could make it physically impossible to do an act (just as humans have no evolved to fly), it cannot create a total moral or mental block on doing something.

        1.6.4.1.1. Con:
        Our brain did evolve [16] a system that completely paralyzes most of the body during REM sleep. This shows that organisms can evolve a system that completely prevents action in a specific context.

  1.7. Pro:
  Evolution fails to explain why we have certain specific moral intuitions.

    1.7.1. Pro:
    Evolutionary ethics struggles to explain altruism.

      1.7.1.1. Pro:
      An organism that sacrifices itself to help others jeopardizes the chance of reproduction.

        1.7.1.1.1. Con:
        Being part of a group that would possibly do the same for you and your offspring dramatically increases the incentive [17] to help others and is re-enforced on many levels by the feedback-loops accompanied with group cooperation.

          1.7.1.1.1.1. Pro:
          Genetic analysis shows that indeed sacrifice for relatives can be adaptive even if it results in death.

        1.7.1.1.2. Con:
        This would not be true if the helper were post-breeding age and the rescued were pre-breeding age. Suggesting adults will always have an incentive to rescue children.

      1.7.1.2. Pro:
      The existence of truly altruistic behaviors is difficult to reconcile with evolutionary theory since individuals would be unwilling to sacrifice their own fitness for the sake of others [18].

        1.7.1.2.1. Con:
        Its existence can be explained by large numbers of individuals and variation in behavior within the group. If there is a natural limit (would directly lead to extinction somehow), anything up to that limit would mean some individuals could act with truly altruistic behavior.

          1.7.1.2.1.1. Pro:
          This would likely come with both positive and negative re-enforcements to the group which could heavily depend on many other variables in biological and environmental conditions / pressures.

        1.7.1.2.2. Con:
        Observation shows that humans are often willing to risk their lives for strangers.

      1.7.1.3. Con:
      -> See 1.7.1.1.1.

      1.7.1.4. Con:
      Kin selection [19] can explain the evolutionary development of altruistic tendencies.

        1.7.1.4.1. Con:
        Kin selection fails to explain why people are sometimes altruistic towards strangers.

          1.7.1.4.1.1. Con:
          Strangers are still human. They are kin relative to all other species.

          1.7.1.4.1.2. Con:
          Altruism toward strangers could be a spandrel [20].

      1.7.1.5. Con:
      The mechanism whereby altruistic behaviour can evolve by natural selection was worked out by Hamilton about 50 years ago [21]. Briefly, it is because those individuals are some of your genes, therefore it can be a net gain for those genes even if you die.

    1.7.2. Pro:
    Evolutionary ethics can not explain the moral of "love your enemy".

      1.7.2.1. Pro:
      Love your enemy is in direct contravention with how animals have involved, which requires them to identify their enemy correctly and defend against them. Therefore, evolution can not explain this moral.

        1.7.2.1.1. Con:
        A trait is not precluded from being a product of evolution just because it is not seen in other species.

        1.7.2.1.2. Con:
        This is not a valid comparison. Animals cannot afford to 'love their enemies' as their enemies are typically in direct competition for limited resources. In contrast, humans live in relative abundance due to cooperative technological societies, where loving one's enemy offers a benefit in achieving that relative abundance.

      1.7.2.2. Con:
      To love one's enemies is counterintuitive, and while viewed as moral by some philosophies or religions, is not common in human behaviour in practice. Evolutionary explanations should not try to explain a tendency among humans that does not actually exist, such as a tendency to love one's neighbour.

        1.7.2.2.1. Con:
        Increasing the moral circle is a perfectly natural process found within evolution, especially between groups where both can seek and find positive outcomes. Evolution predicts such to be a possible course of action.

          1.7.2.2.1.1. Pro:
          When successful it translates into less threats and more long-term potential benefits if me and my neighbor can agree to care for each other -VS- being worried constantly about their actions and what expenses I must dedicate in preventing and in preparing actions against them.

          1.7.2.2.1.2. Con:
          This is a false equivalence: To love one's enemies is not the same thing as coming to mutually beneficial arrangements with neighbours who might have been your enemies.

            1.7.2.2.1.2.1. Con:
            First off, to "love one's enemies" is ambiguous, but assuming it has any tangle impact, it would be when two or more sides can find benefit in exercising such to some degree.

              1.7.2.2.1.2.1.1. Con:
              "Love your enemies" is biblical, and Matthew 5:44 [22] is not at all ambiguous, especially in context.

            1.7.2.2.1.2.2. Con:
            For one group to exclusively practice such (assuming love means to not take up arms against), would mean the elimination of said group (assuming others find no benefit and thus exterminate the pacifist), and so for such a practice to hold any relevance it would come from two or more groups finding benefit in practicing it.

            1.7.2.2.1.2.3. Con:
            You can label it whatever you want, it is but a parameter that does or does not meet conditions successfully.

        1.7.2.2.2. Con:
        What morals are, is one thing. What people do is another. The original objection is that evolution can only select from what people do, not what people believe. This "con" is basically a concession that the original objection is in fact true and valid.

      1.7.2.3. Con:
      Expanding the moral circle to work with and include a greater sphere of potential agents is a net benefit when and if it succeeds. Not needing to dedicate resources to protecting and/or attacking enemies can result in multiple benefits to one's own expenses, and equally beneficial to the enemy that recognizes the same.

      1.7.2.4. Con:
      An enemy one day may become a friend the next, and with time and numbers some combinations will benefit greatly, although most will not, this is the truth of nature and reality. "love your enemy" is the ideologization of a concept that under the right conditions can allow greater benefits than "battle thy enemy".

      1.7.2.5. Con:
      The aphorism can be interpreted to mean "make peace with your enemies" or "try not to have enemies". These have evolutionary advantages when followed as general rules.

        1.7.2.5.1. Pro:
        Conflict consumes resources, therefore any intent, decision, or action to limit conflict can be considered evolutionarily advantageous.

    1.7.3. Pro:
    Evolution fails to explain why we have moral intuitions opposing rape.

      1.7.3.1. Pro:
      Since rape sometimes leads to reproduction, and evolution tends to propagate behaviors that promote reproduction, it seems evolution would have given us moral intuitions promoting rape rather than condemning it. However, we have moral intuitions condemning rape.

      1.7.3.2. Con:
      Insofar as a woman chooses mates so as to maximise genetic fitness, for a man to override that choice and impose himself correspondingly decreases it.

        1.7.3.2.1. Con:
        Even in this case, it fails to explain why we have moral intuitions condemning rape even when committed against otherwise sexually inactive women.

        1.7.3.2.2. Con:
        If this was the explanation for how we evolved moral intuitions condemning rape, rape would be seen as much less serious if the man was genetically fit. However, we do not have such intuitions.

      1.7.3.3. Con:
      Our moral intuitions are to some extent socially constructed - insofar as we changed our minds about something in the past thousand years, our current opinion doesn't call for an evolutionary explanation - and many past societies did not define rape the way we do (or, equivalently, forbid that which we call rape).

        1.7.3.3.1. Pro:
        Historically, in many societies, it was impossible for a man to rape his wife [23].

          1.7.3.3.1.1. Con:
          Even in these societies, there were moral intuitions condemning the rape of other women, which would be unexpected under naturalism given that such acts often produce more human offspring.

      1.7.3.4. Con:
      Rape is violent and harmful to a woman, and the chance of viable offspring per sex act is low, so it is possible that the net offspring produced due to an act of rape is not even positive.

      1.7.3.5. Con:
      Insofar as we have general moral intuitions about violence, they should apply to rape, as rape is violent.

    1.7.4. Con:
    Morality is in our instincts.

      1.7.4.1. Con:
      Morality is unnatural [24] to the evolutionary make-up of humans, instead, our instincts are consumed by self-interest and survival [25].

      1.7.4.2. Pro:
      Because humans possess the intellectual powers [26] that are needed to repress natural impulses, it is argued that morality in their instincts.

        1.7.4.2.1. Con:
        Simply having the intellectual power to control our own emotions, thoughts, and impusles does not make having morality an instinct.

      1.7.4.3. Con:
      Humans are predisposed to be moral, but still require certain experiences to develop moral behavior.

      1.7.4.4. Con:
      "Morality" is a term used by humans to explain the reasons we should do what might not be the most beneficially to the individual. It is not "objective" in of itself, it only means something to creatures that develop groups that are co-dependent who have natural limits which create positive and negative results. If there were no negative results from indiscriminately punching people, it would not be considered morally wrong to freely do this whenever someone wanted.

  1.8. Pro:
  Evolutionary ethics doesn't seem to account for how we can have a reliable knowledge of basic moral axioms (e.g. "human life has value"), even if they exist.

    1.8.1. Pro:
    Evolution would tend to promote the moral beliefs and intuitions that facilitate survival and reproduction, even if these intuitions differ radically from our true moral obligations. Thus, if our moral intuitions merely came about from unguided evolution, they are not trustworthy.

    1.9.1. Con:

      1.9.7.2. Pro:

    1.9.8. Con:

    1.9.9. Pro:
    Experimental evidence [34] suggests that people’s opinions of what God thinks is right and wrong tracks what they believe is right and wrong.

      1.9.9.1. Con:

      1.9.9.5. Con:

      1.9.9.6. Pro:
      Social psychologist Nicholas Epley and his colleagues surveyed [35] religious believers about their moral beliefs and the moral beliefs of God. Not surprisingly, what people thought was right and wrong matched up pretty well with what they felt God’s morality was like.

      1.9.9.7. Con:
      God might as well have evolved as a framework to structure or teach moral understanding.

  1.10. Con:
  Simply because an explanation is not currently available does not mean there isn't one. The concept of evolutionary ethics develops as our understanding of evolution does and is therefore subject to changes and cannot be discounted until its irrelevancy is proven beyond a reasonable doubt.

    1.10.1. Con:
    This is an argument from ignorance. It contends that because we are ignorant of possible future advances in evolutionary science, it is impossible to disprove evolutionary ethics as an explanation of our moral experience.

  1.11. Con:
  Morality emerged through natural selection due to it enhancing genetic fitness.

    1.11.1. Con:
    Natural selection can not select motivations, only outcomes. Morality is about motivations and regulating motivations. Thus, morality is permanently and categorically outside the realm or reach of natural selection and evolutionary mechanisms.

      1.11.1.1. Con:
      An animal that did not experience hunger (a motivation to eat) would allow itself to starve to death even if food was abundant, and by dying remove itself from the gene pool. Evolution therefore clearly can and does select motivations.

    1.11.2. Pro:
    If morality was not created during natural selection, then the strong members of the society would instantly eliminate the weak to conserve resources and societies would not have formed.

      1.11.2.1. Con:
      Those strong members/leaders were more powerful surrounded by a larger group worshiping them than surviving or fighting other clans on their own.

      1.11.2.2. Con:
      The most numerous categories of "the weak" are women and children (who are generally weaker than adult men), which it would be suicidally stupid to want to eliminate, society or no society.

      1.11.2.3. Con:
      This is only true in cases where scarcity demands such actions. Often, when such circumstances do arise, in-fighting within groups is observed.

        1.11.2.3.1. Pro:
        When faced with food shortages, human beings have gone to war. For instance, the first Indochina war [36] was motivated by widespread famine.

        1.11.2.3.2. Pro:
        Scientists believe the next big war [37] will be fought over water shortages.

        1.11.2.3.3. Pro:
        Many cases of individuals resorting to murder [38] over water scarcity have been documented.

      1.11.2.4. Con:
      The formation of society is not a moral phenomenon. Instead, it could have been guided by self-interest alone.

        1.11.2.4.1. Pro:
        Surviving on one's own is difficult as a human when predators possess greater strength, speed, and agility. In order to be able to hunt effectively, humans needed to be able to travel in groups.

    1.11.3. Pro:
    Group selection [39] breeds morality because that enables groups to survive when other groups don't or less so.

    1.11.4. Pro:
    Moral behavior is the individual behavior that often causes the least harm and the greatest benefit to a population over multiple generations.

      1.11.4.1. Con:
      This is adhoc reasoning, and circular in nature. There are plenty of instances in nature where actions that are considered immoral would be "beneficial" to the group. such as killing of the leader, socially accepted rape, theft of resources. To call these actions "moral" in any setting is to destroy the concept of morality.

        1.11.4.1.1. Con:
        Subscribing to a different meta-ethical principle [40] cannot "destroy the concept of morality"; we must merely acknowledge that what is "considered immoral" is not universal.

          1.11.4.1.1.1. Con:
          Subscribing to a different meta-ethical principle does destroy the concept of morality, because you are no longer appealing to the same concept of morality. Which is by definition a destruction of the medium of the discussion.

            1.11.4.1.1.1.1. Con:
            Disagreeing about how a concept is defined - which is what subscribing to a different meta-ethical principle amounts to - is a normal part of any discussion about any particular concept, it does not at all "[destroy] the medium of discussion".

        1.11.4.1.2. Con:
        It could be argued that if a greater understanding of actions could be explained and understood by non-humans, that their "morals" would more closely match human moral standards.

        1.11.4.1.3. Con:
        Knowledge is power, and with greater understanding of our actions (including recognizing how we make others feel) would adjust how a species views correct and incorrect actions.

        1.11.4.1.4. Con:
        Different cognitive processes and different environmental conditions/pressures produce different types of morals.

          1.11.4.1.4.1. Con:
          Such process can not produce moral obligations, and are thus irrelevant to morality. At best they create a sense of morality, not morality itself.

            1.11.4.1.4.1.1. Con:
            A sense of morality is a form of morality.

              1.11.4.1.4.1.1.1. Con:
              Such a form of morality is as much an illusion, as the sense of sight which creates images that are not a reflection of an external reality.

            1.11.4.1.4.1.2. Con:
            "Moral obligation" is dependent on the mind that interprets said morals.

              1.11.4.1.4.1.2.1. Con:
              Moral obligations are inherently external to the interpretations of the mind to which they apply.

                1.11.4.1.4.1.2.1.1. Pro:
                If moral obligations are just a product of the mind, then "morally bound" simply means "we must do what we do". However, moral obligation inherently implies that we can do what we ought not to do. Thus, the rejection of the claim "moral obligations are inherently external" would lead to an inherently nonsense position, which we should reject.

                  1.11.4.1.4.1.2.1.1.1. Con:
                  The "ought" is formed from the conditions, in how the agents involved are impacted by the choices they and others make. If being punched doesn't create enough positive outcomes, *and we agree*, then we "ought" not punch each other. A limit in knowledge of outcomes can skew the "ought" and a world of scarcity and unknowns would constrain the ability for the agents involved to align what they "ought" to do.

                1.11.4.1.4.1.2.1.2. Con:
                Morals are meaningless without a mind to interpret and apply them.

            1.11.4.1.4.1.3. Con:
            Cognitive processes and environmental conditions (that create positive and negative impacts) are the only known things that can produce moral "obligations". If I cannot cognitively comprehend how the environmental pressures impact me and those I depend on, then I cannot develop a moral sense.

          1.11.4.1.4.2. Pro:
          Morals are a product of minds, minds are only found so far contained with in environments, and with minds of varying degrees of cognitive process.

      1.11.4.2. Pro:
      Moral behavior could prevent waste when a momentary surplus of food is discovered by one member of a population. It might be mercifully and charitably shared with those who need it.

      1.11.4.3. Pro:
      Moral behavior could decrease the chance that covetousness and theft would halt the successful employment of new techniques such as those which produce and maintain healthy pack animals (asses).

    1.11.5. Pro:
    Kin selection [41] is based on 'inclusive fitness'. According to inclusive fitness, certain organisms such as, say, sterile workers can accrue reproductive benefits by helping their relatives. In doing so, they help shared genes to survive and get passed on to the next generation, thereby increasing the survival of the species.

      1.11.5.1. Con:
      A study [42] found that inclusive fitness and natural selection produced the same results, thereby undermining how important inclusive fitness is to the survival of a species.

    1.11.6. Con:
    According to evolutionary theory [43], natural selection entails that only the fittest individuals in any given biological population will survive and reproduce, not the most moral one. Therefore, when confronted with a choice between something that furthers an individual's interests but is immoral and something that is moral but is not accompanied by any real benefits, the individual ought to select the former.

      1.11.6.1. Con:
      Instinct is messy; our moral instincts are not fine-tuned enough to distinguish between situations where moral behaviour can ultimately be justified by self-interest and situations where it cannot, and are especially likely to miss in the modern environment, which we were not adapted for.

      1.11.6.2. Con:
      The "fittest" does not always necessitate individual benefits.

      1.11.6.3. Con:
      Evolution creates variation. Each that is good enough to survive and reproduce will do so regardless of what we might think it "ought" to do.

      1.11.6.4. Con:
      Evolution is blind to what it might "ought" to do. Either what develops can, or it cannot survive and reproduce - and what does, passes on what was possibly successful without a need for the individuals to decide what ought they do.

      1.11.6.5. Con:
      For a social animal, as humans are, abstract things like reputation are at least as valuable as direct material gains, and this permits a self-interested explanation for much behaviour without "real" benefit.

      1.11.6.6. Con:
      Evolution not only works on an individual but also on a species level [44]. In order for a species to be more ‘fit’ than another, empathetic behavior towards your kind will be beneficial.

        1.11.6.6.1. Con:
        This assumes that moral action will inherently be more beneficial to the species than the immoral counterpart, which is not justified.

          1.11.6.6.1.1. Con:
          There are very few evolutionary successes with a species. So some degree of moral behavior (aware or not) is inherently beneficial.

            1.11.6.6.1.1.1. Con:
            Non sequitur. That there are few successes with a species, does not imply that moral behavior is involved. Its not as though immoral behavior has been shown to exert a relevant evolutionary pressure on any species.

              1.11.6.6.1.1.1.1. Con:
              All social species, species that depend on some degree of social interaction will develop good and bad behaviors that more so benefit the group than the individual.

          1.11.6.6.1.2. Con:
          Morals must be overall (more so than not, but still not always) more beneficial to the group than the individual.

            1.11.6.6.1.2.1. Pro:
            Groups that have too many individuals with selfish morals will succeed less against other competitive groups that develop more group-focused morals.

        1.11.6.6.2. Con:
        This assumes that the selective pressure for the species as a whole will be greater than the selective pressure for the benefit of the individual.

          1.11.6.6.2.1. Con:
          Selective pressures on the whole would need to be "greater" than for the individual to some degree in order for groups to be successful.

            1.11.6.6.2.1.1. Con:
            Evolution does not get to compare non existent traits. If lying were to help the individual over other individuals, then it would be selected within that group. The entire group may go extinct as a result, but that would not necessarily blunt the evolutionary pressure of the individual over the group.

              1.11.6.6.2.1.1.1. Con:
              Unless immortal and/or self-reproducing, an individual to some degree needs to care for other individuals at a cost to themselves.

        1.11.6.6.3. Con:
        Evolution operates on the levels of replicators, i.e DNA, not species. The key variable is whether cooperation leads to differential success that enhances the accumulation of genetic traits solidifying the aforementioned behavior.

    1.11.7. Pro:
    If the capacity for moral behavior did not exist among humans, then they would not be as successful as they are at increasing the human population in areas where food is abundant enough to support a lot of people. So there is evolutionary pressure for treating each other as we want to be treated.

      1.11.7.1. Con:
      Any point about evolutionary pressure is meaningless without quantification. it is not possible to quantify the pressure being referenced here, thus it is not reasonable to assume it had any effect on evolutionary path.

      1.11.7.2. Con:
      The species' overall population has little effect on the genetic fitness of the individual, so any such evolutionary pressure is marginal at most.
