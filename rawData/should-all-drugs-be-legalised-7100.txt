Discussion Name:
Should all drugs be legalised?

1. Thesis:
All drugs [1] should be legalised.

  1.1. Pro:
  The enforcement of the criminalisation of drugs has harmed communities around the world and should be stopped.

    1.1.1. Pro:
    Criminalising the activity of drug users has negative effects on individuals, their families, and society.

      1.1.1.1. Pro:
      The incarceration of drug users negatively impacts their families.

        1.1.1.1.1. Pro:
        Research has found that the children of incarcerated adults are more likely to develop emotional and behavioural problems (p. 117 [2]).

          1.1.1.1.1.1. Pro:
          Some studies indicate that the children of incarcerated parents are around six times more likely [3] to eventually become incarcerated themselves.

        1.1.1.1.2. Pro:
        Incarceration tends to cost governments significant amounts of money that may otherwise be used to fund foster care, adoption agencies, and educational services that could support the children of drug users (p. 117 [4]).

          1.1.1.1.2.1. Pro:
          The incarceration of drug offenders is highly expensive (p. 3 [5]).

          1.1.1.1.2.2. Pro:
          Corrections expenditures constitute the second-fastest growing expense in US state budgets (p. 7 [6]).

        1.1.1.1.3. Con:
        In some cases, the forced removal of a substance abuser from their family environment via incarceration may have a net positive impact [7] on other members of that family.

      1.1.1.2. Pro:
      If drugs were legalised, prison systems would be less overcrowded, which is a significant social issue [8] in the US.

        1.1.1.2.1. Pro:
        Prison overcrowding often means that inmates do not receive the resources and care they need. Hunger, health issues and a lack of access to medicine often plague overcrowded prisons [9].

        1.1.1.2.2. Pro:
        As of 2020, there were over 450,000 [10] people behind bars for drug-related crimes in the US, representing 1 in 5 inmates.

        1.1.1.2.3. Con:
        Given that this is mostly a problem in the US, it implies a problem with the US legal system rather than with the banning of drugs.

          1.1.1.2.3.1. Con:
          Many other countries [11] have significantly more overcrowded prisons than the US does. Legalisation could help resolve this serious issue in those countries, too.

          1.1.1.2.3.2. Pro:
          It is too easy to convict suspects [12] in the US. This is not as true in most other countries, meaning that drug legalisation would have little impact on prison overcrowding elsewhere.

          1.1.1.2.3.3. Con:
          In any legal system where drug use is prohibited by law, these otherwise innocent drug users will always be criminalised.

      1.1.1.3. Pro:
      The methods required for policing drugs are often dangerous and invasive.

        1.1.1.3.1. Pro:
        In the US, police officers use 'no-knock raids' to surprise potentially dangerous drug offenders, which frequently leads to the deaths [13] of innocent civilians [14].

          1.1.1.3.1.1. Con:
          Since the high-profile killing of innocent civilian Breonna Taylor in a drugs raid in 2020, many US states and districts have introduced or considered proposals to completely or partially ban [15] no-knock warrants.

        1.1.1.3.2. Pro:
        Almost 90% [16] of wiretaps are for drug trafficking in the US. This constitutes a major violation of citizens' privacy.

        1.1.1.3.3. Con:
        It is not necessary to end drug prohibition in order to mitigate cruel and dangerous policing practices: adapting [17] the criminal justice system with regards to drug law enforcement would be sufficient.

      1.1.1.4. Pro:
      Imprisonment has negative long-term repercussions for individuals.

        1.1.1.4.1. Pro:
        Incarceration often creates, or else fails to treat, substance use disorders or other mental illnesses.

          1.1.1.4.1.1. Pro:
          Studies have found that, compared with the overall population, former inmates are 40 times more likely [18] to die from an opioid overdose in the two-week period following release, and 10-18 times more likely [19] in the year following release.

          1.1.1.4.1.2. Pro:
          Many prisons around the world force prisoners into inhumane conditions, thereby damaging their mental health. (p. 1 [20])

          1.1.1.4.1.3. Con:
          Poor mental health in prison has become a major focus of prison reform [21], so strategies to mitigate this issue will likely be prioritised when prison policy changes are implemented.

        1.1.1.4.2. Pro:
        Those who have been to prison find it very hard to find employment upon their release (p. 3 [22]).

          1.1.1.4.2.1. Pro:
          Due to this, many who leave prison often continue to commit crime [23] to make money.

          1.1.1.4.2.2. Pro:
          Many who go to prison suffer from stigma [24], affecting their employment opportunities and their daily life.

          1.1.1.4.2.3. Con:
          More governments and corporations have recognized the stigma surrounding former prison inmates and begun hiring [25] a greater number of them, which at least partially mitigates this issue.

          1.1.1.4.2.4. Con:
          Some countries have started expunging [26] marijuana convictions from the records of former criminals, making it easier for them to find jobs.

        1.1.1.4.3. Con:
        Time spent in prison can have a lasting positive impact on an offender's life by deterring them from future crime and rehabilitating them via training and well-being programmes.

          1.1.1.4.3.1. Pro:
          Imprisonment significantly increases [27] employment rates among former convicts due to the increased likelihood of their participation in training programmes while in prison, which in turn has a highly positive impact on recidivism rates and thus on the overall amount of time spent out of prison.

          1.1.1.4.3.2. Con:
          Recidivism rates are often very high [28], indicating that prisons are not effective deterrents of crime.

      1.1.1.5. Con:
      The number of arrests for drug-related crimes are already plateauing [29] or slightly declining [30] in many parts of the world, indicating a less aggressive approach to policing drugs.

        1.1.1.5.1. Pro:
        Due to widescale controversies regarding aggressive and discriminatory policing, many police forces and governments have felt pressure [31] to change their styles of policing.

        1.1.1.5.2. Con:
        In 2018-19, 59% of all stop-and-searches conducted in the UK were drug-related (p. 34 [32]). This indicates that, even while drug arrests have decreased, heavy drug policing is still highly prevalent.

        1.1.1.5.3. Con:
        Drug offenses still made up almost 15% [33] of all arrests in the US in 2019, and drug arrests have begun to increase again [34] since 2015.

          1.1.1.5.3.1. Con:
          It is likely that many of those arrests are not solely for drug use or possession but are also for violence or gang activity, which would still be policed under legalisation.

        1.1.1.5.4. Pro:
        Many police forces have explicitly changed their strategies for drug policing, focusing less on marijuana use or individual dealers and more on diverting users to treatment (pp. 2-4 [35]).

      1.1.1.6. Con:
      -> See 1.1.1.3.3.

    1.1.2. Pro:
    Drug prohibition has been used as a racist policy for the mass incarceration of people of color.

      1.1.2.1. Con:
      Other countries, like Japan [36], have strict drug laws which are not used as instruments of social oppression.

      1.1.2.2. Con:
      Drug legalisation would not necessarily improve the conditions of those discriminated against: there are still many other forms of systemic racism unrelated to drug prohibition.

        1.1.2.2.1. Con:
        The fact that other systematic racial issues exist does not mean this particular racist law should not be challenged.

        1.1.2.2.2. Pro:
        Supremacist or racist governments will always find ways to criminalise minority groups. For example, 'loitering [37]' was considered a criminal offence for which black men could be sent to unpaid labour camps for years until the early 20th century.

        1.1.2.2.3. Pro:
        Racist policing has been observed in many other forms [38] of law enforcement, such as in police traffic surveillance.

      1.1.2.3. Pro:
      In the US, the War on Drugs has led to the mass incarceration [39] of African Americans and other minority groups.

        1.1.2.3.1. Pro:
        Incarceration rates for selling or possessing drugs are higher for black youths than white youths, despite the fact that white youths are more likely [40] to sell drugs than their black peers.

        1.1.2.3.2. Con:
        Racial disparities in US prisons have decreased [41]. While a gap still exists, its reduction is an indication that other efforts to make policing less racist can be successful.

      1.1.2.4. Con:
      Drug legalisation will not remove the underlying incentives that governments have for pursuing mass incarceration. Stopping those causes is a more effective way to lessen these racist arrests.

        1.1.2.4.1. Pro:
        The prevalence of racist mass incarceration in the US is in part due to the large influence of private prisons [42], who profit from - and thus are incentivised to fuel - growing numbers of arrests.

          1.1.2.4.1.1. Con:
          Only 8% [43] of American prisoners are in private prisons and many states do not use them at all. Causes of the mass incarceration problem, therefore, go far beyond the influence of private prisons.

          1.1.2.4.1.2. Pro:
          Private prison companies often bribe judges [44] to give longer sentences to those on trial. This will continue to happen regardless of drug legalisation.

          1.1.2.4.1.3. Con:
          Often, states have had to rely on private prisons to deal with an exploding number [45] of arrests. If significantly fewer people are being charged and arrested for drug crimes in the first place, private prisons may have less influence.

        1.1.2.4.2. Con:
        Many other strategies to target the sources of racist policing are often not as popular. Drug legalisation may be an important first step to solving the problem without triggering large sociopolitical backlash.

          1.1.2.4.2.1. Pro:
          More radical [46] solutions to systemic racism within the police force are often very unpopular.

          1.1.2.4.2.2. Pro:
          Drug legalisation has become a popular policy [47] in many countries.

          1.1.2.4.2.3. Con:
          Such a strategy may breed political fatigue [48], where voters are less responsive to criminal justice reform once they feel as though important changes have already been made.

        1.1.2.4.3. Pro:
        Mass incarceration in the US has largely been caused by [49] a lingering commitment to the judicial priorities of the Founding Fathers and a reluctance to use other modes of punishment.

      1.1.2.5. Pro:
      Historically, drug laws have been explicitly crafted as a means of oppressing minorities. They cannot be separated from that legacy.

        1.1.2.5.1. Pro:
        The former US president Richard Nixon's War on Drugs was started [50] to target blacks and hippies.

        1.1.2.5.2. Pro:
        The first drug laws [51] in the US were intended to discriminate against people of colour, including African-Americans and Mexican and Chinese migrants.

        1.1.2.5.3. Con:
        The fact that drug prohibition has been used as a racist tool in the past does not mean that this will continue to be the case in the future.

          1.1.2.5.3.1. Con:
          The tools used to fight drugs are intrinsically ones that control and discriminate against minorities. As long as there is a war on drugs, this subjugation will remain.

            1.1.2.5.3.1.1. Pro:
            Drug policing intrinsically requires a significant amount of invasive policing and surveillance. Given this, it is hard to enforce drug laws against the most privileged in society.

              1.1.2.5.3.1.1.1. Pro:
              It is largely impossible to know if a suspect possesses drugs without searching them [52]. Police officers may be apprehensive to conduct these searches on those who may be able to complain about or sue them.

              1.1.2.5.3.1.1.2. Pro:
              Communities with influence and money are often not seen as dangerous or crime-ridden even if drug use within them is rampant. This means that drug policing is concentrated [53] in poorer places.

            1.1.2.5.3.1.2. Pro:
            Many of those who are involved in drug trafficking come from poverty [54]. Criminalizing drugs, then, often means targetting those in poverty.

            1.1.2.5.3.1.3. Con:
            Police should try to engage in discussion with the communities [55] they enter on how best to police drugs without causing societal harms, creating a more democratic form of policing.

              1.1.2.5.3.1.3.1. Con:
              Community policing is often used as a justification to expand [56] police forces, which in the long-term leads to more drug arrests and surveillance.

          1.1.2.5.3.2. Pro:
          Many places have invested more in sensitivity training [57] for the police, hoping to change the internal culture of police forces to make them less racist.

            1.1.2.5.3.2.1. Con:
            Such training most often fails [58] to change the actions of police officers.

    1.1.3. Con:
    Such harms could be avoided through decriminalisation rather than through full legalisation.

      1.1.3.1. Con:
      Non-carceral drug punishment can be incredibly debilitating for people as well.

        1.1.3.1.1. Pro:
        Even misdemeanor charges [59] on someone's permanent record can cause them great difficulties when trying to find jobs, housing and even loans.

        1.1.3.1.2. Pro:
        Fines for the possession or sale of drugs in decriminalized systems can be between hundreds and thousands of dollars [60].

          1.1.3.1.2.1. Con:
          The average fine for drug possession is likely to have a more temporarily negative impact on an individual than long-term detention would.

          1.1.3.1.2.2. Pro:
          Individuals may be issued a warrant [61] and face incarceration if they cannot pay fines or miss court dates relating to the possession or sale of drugs in a decriminalised context.

        1.1.3.1.3. Con:
        'Defelonization [62]' is merely one possible part of complete decriminalisation. Other methods [63] to help people avoid fines and non-carceral punishments can also be used.

      1.1.3.2. Pro:
      Decriminalisation would likely lead to police targeting poor or minority communities to a much lesser extent.

        1.1.3.2.1. Pro:
        88% [64] of marijuana-related arrests in the US between 2001 and 2010 were simply for possession, with people of colour accounting for a disproportionately high number [65] of such arrests. Under decriminalisation, many of these people would not be arrested.

        1.1.3.2.2. Con:
        Under decriminalisation, there often still exist restrictions and thus arrests surrounding the public use and trade of drugs. These mean that problematic policing practices can still continue.

          1.1.3.2.2.1. Pro:
          When the penalties for drug-related offenses decrease, there is often less of an administrative burden on police for arresting people, meaning they are more likely to do it (p. 3 [66]).

            1.1.3.2.2.1.1. Pro:
            In the 1970s when New York and California partially decriminalized drugs, they found the number of drug-possession arrests actually increased as police officers took advantage of legal loopholes (p. 2 [67]).

          1.1.3.2.2.2. Pro:
          In many countries, there exist limits on publicly consuming alcohol [68]. Even under full legalisation, it is likely that similar restrictions will be applied to drugs.

            1.1.3.2.2.2.1. Con:
            Public display laws are typically not enforced as heavily when the substance being consumed is legal.

              1.1.3.2.2.2.1.1. Pro:
              If drinkers can find ways to hide their public alcohol use [69], they are typically not bothered by police. A similar norm may emerge for drug use.

              1.1.3.2.2.2.1.2. Con:
              Given the stigma surrounding drugs and the racism [70] that often informs their policing, it is likely that police will enforce these rules more strictly.

            1.1.3.2.2.2.2. Pro:
            In several US states that have decriminalised or legalised marijuana, it is still illegal [71] to use it publicly.

          1.1.3.2.2.3. Pro:
          Drug-dealing is still a criminal offense [72] under decriminalisation, so police will likely still heavily monitor, and make arrests in, areas where they suspect it is happening.

            1.1.3.2.2.3.1. Con:
            It may become easier for dealers to circumvent or else abide by persisting laws under decriminalisation, as there will be clear limits on how much one is allowed to possess without facing consequences.

            1.1.3.2.2.3.2. Pro:
            Many decriminalisation proposals, including the law passed by New York in 2021 [73], still treat possession of large quantities of drugs as felonies.

            1.1.3.2.2.3.3. Pro:
            Given that countries such as the US [74] have dedicated drug-specific law enforcement agencies, it is likely that any illegal drug-dealing will continue to be met with exaggerated force under decriminalisation.

          1.1.3.2.2.4. Con:
          Given that problematic law enforcement is currently such a focal issue [75] in the discussion of drug legalisation, if police continue to abuse their power, further measures may be taken to stop this.

            1.1.3.2.2.4.1. Pro:
            Police reforms are already popular and being proposed around the world [76], meaning that there will be significant attention paid to potential continued abuses of power under decriminalisation.

            1.1.3.2.2.4.2. Con:
            If police abuse and arrests do not diminish under decriminalisation, it may lead to more support for a return to prohibition. As such, sustainable drug policy reform should legalise all drugs.

          1.1.3.2.2.5. Con:
          Even if police action continues, fewer people will be arrested overall and more of those who do face legal actions will have to face less severe consequences.

        1.1.3.2.3. Pro:
        Under decriminalisation, there would be less need for police officers to monitor street-level drug interactions [77], which may alleviate a source of great tension between the police and communities.

          1.1.3.2.3.1. Pro:
          Such police practices have led to mistrust and enmity between police officers and youth [78] in minority communities (p. 5 [79]).

          1.1.3.2.3.2. Con:
          If it becomes harder to justify street-level policing, this may motivate police to use even more invasive methods [80] to find evidence of illegal drug-dealing.

      1.1.3.3. Pro:
      Most proposals for decriminalisation replace criminal penalties with civil ones that often require users to seek treatment [81] or refer them to support services, which may not automatically happen in a context of complete legalisation. Decriminalisation may thus be more effective than legalisation at reducing problematic drug use.

        1.1.3.3.1. Con:
        Many states would be unable to fund treatment services under a decriminalisation model, given they would not have additional tax revenue from legalised drug commerce.

          1.1.3.3.1.1. Con:
          Governments could strike deals with privately-owned facilities, allowing those found in possession of or dealing drugs to access high-quality treatment without the need for significant additional state funding.

      1.1.3.4. Con:
      Decriminalisation is unlikely to stop many forms of structural discrimination against drug users.

        1.1.3.4.1. Pro:
        In countries where individuals must rely on private health coverage, insurers often create barriers [82] to avoid covering drug-related treatments when they are decriminalised.

        1.1.3.4.2. Con:
        While structural discrimination against drug users may still exist under decriminalisation, it will be easier for activists to raise awareness of this phenomenon in a context where drug use is not criminally chargeable.

          1.1.3.4.2.1. Pro:
          When drug use is decriminalised, drug users may feel more comfortable and confident speaking out about the ways in which the stigma they face has been institutionalised.

          1.1.3.4.2.2. Con:
          Under decriminalisation, drug use will likely still be seen as 'taboo' and illegal, meaning drug users will still face prejudice and discrimination. Only under legalisation would this not be the case.

            1.1.3.4.2.2.1. Con:
            Given the existing stigma [83], fully legalising drugs may cause backlash, making it harder to address other issues surrounding stigma. Decriminalisation may be a useful incremental step.

              1.1.3.4.2.2.1.1. Pro:
              Legalisation may be seen by many as 'too quick' of a change and a large challenge to the status quo (p. 6 [84]). This may make it harder to combat stigma.

            1.1.3.4.2.2.2. Pro:
            It is easier for those refusing services [85] to drug users to justify their actions by claiming that users are still engaging in illegal behavior.

            1.1.3.4.2.2.3. Pro:
            Decriminalisation may not prevent people from seeing drug users as 'criminals', because their acts will still be illegal.

    1.1.4. Pro:
    The international War on Drugs has led to rich countries, such as the US, inflicting severe harm [86] on less powerful nations and their citizens.

      1.1.4.1. Pro:
      The US-fuelled War on Drugs has had a devastating and deadly impact on Latin American countries (p. 7 [87]).

        1.1.4.1.1. Con:
        The US government has given Colombia millions of dollars annually in aid as part of its international anti-drug strategy, with a focus placed on assisting those living in regions particularly affected by drugs (p. 1 [88]).

        1.1.4.1.2. Pro:
        The War on Drugs has worsened conditions in Mexico, causing thousands of deaths [89].

      1.1.4.2. Con:
      Legalising drugs would simply lead to another form of Western global control, wherein governments and large corporations begin to take ownership of foreign land for drug production.

        1.1.4.2.1. Con:
        Legalisation could help end [90] the drug wars in countries such as Mexico, eliminating the need for US interventions.

          1.1.4.2.1.1. Pro:
          Legalisation could lead to middle- and low-level employees of drug cartels moving to legal work, if allowed, given the greater safety and security associated with the latter. This could hollow out cartels.

          1.1.4.2.1.2. Con:
          The replacement of drug cartels with legal companies in areas like South America may simply further entrench these countries' roles as drug suppliers for affluent nations.

        1.1.4.2.2. Pro:
        Legalisation may lead to a rush for Western corporations to take ownership of the fields where drug crops are grown, giving them increased power over many communities around the world.

          1.1.4.2.2.1. Pro:
          In other industries, such as the oil industry, large multinational companies have bribed [91] powerful actors so as to be able to continue profiting from exploitative activities, fuelling corruption and human rights violations.

          1.1.4.2.2.2. Pro:
          This may similarly destabilize areas in South America where drug fields are currently being run by cartels.

          1.1.4.2.2.3. Con:
          Many drugs do not rely on such fields. Amphetamines can be produced in laboratories [92] and plants like marijuana can be grown indoors [93] in a variety of environments.

      1.1.4.3. Pro:
      The War on Drugs has often been used as a pretext for the US to gain control over policymaking [94] in other countries, often through corruption and violence.

        1.1.4.3.1. Pro:
        The US used drug-eradication strategies to prop up violent military forces [95] in the fight against drug production in the Andes.

        1.1.4.3.2. Con:
        The most egregious acts committed by the US in the War on Drugs were part of their broader Cold War foreign policy. Those strategies are much less prominent today.

          1.1.4.3.2.1. Pro:
          The US used anti-drug rhetoric to justify support for violent insurgent groups in Colombia [96] attempting to attack left-wing insurgents, especially during the 1980s.

          1.1.4.3.2.2. Pro:
          The US used drug crimes as a justification to depose leaders who challenged them, most notably Panamanian leader Manuel Noriega [97].

          1.1.4.3.2.3. Con:
          The US continues today to support and implement policies that impoverish communities elsewhere and that cause violence [98], often committed by government forces.

    1.1.5. Con:
    The legalisation of drugs would not likely stop the over-policing [99] of affected communities in countries like the US.

      1.1.5.1. Pro:
      Crime and gang activity may initially continue in many areas after legalisation if there is a delay in the drug market shift, so police are likely to continue their current behavior.

        1.1.5.1.1. Con:
        While it may not happen immediately, eventually the reduced need to police these areas will become apparent to police as more people buy drugs legally and criminal activity decreases.

        1.1.5.1.2. Pro:
        Stubborn crime rates may lead to increased public support for more aggressive policing if some start to believe that the 'treating crime as a disease [100]' approach is not working.

      1.1.5.2. Pro:
      Legalisation will not change many of the structural reasons for which excessive policing occurs in certain areas.

        1.1.5.2.1. Con:
        No type of drug reform will be able to stop racist and aggressive policing. Drug legalisation would simply remove one pernicious avenue through which it is justified.

        1.1.5.2.2. Pro:
        Local governments sometimes raise funds from fines [101], giving police an incentive to target vulnerable communities, who are unlikely to be able to appeal them. This will not change with legalisation.

        1.1.5.2.3. Pro:
        What police define as 'dangerous' or 'gang' behavior is often subjective and influenced by implicit racism [102].

  1.2. Con:
  Legalisation would increase overall drug use.

    1.2.1. Pro:
    Many people currently avoid using drugs primarily because they are illegal. If legalised, many of them may start.

      1.2.1.1. Con:
      More people may start to use drugs if they are kept illegal, as their illegality gives them 'cool' and 'forbidden fruit [103]' status.

        1.2.1.1.1. Con:
        Drug use would likely become more enticing to a wider demographic if companies were able to create marketing campaigns for drugs as they do for legal products such as cigarettes, alcohol, and fast food.

          1.2.1.1.1.1. Pro:
          Private companies have a history of funding advertisements encouraging the use of products, such as tobacco [104], among young people.

          1.2.1.1.1.2. Con:
          In a number of countries [105], the advertisement of cigarettes, alcohol, and fast food [106] is - or will soon be - restricted. The same model could be adopted for legalised drugs.

            1.2.1.1.1.2.1. Con:
            Contemporary legalization proposals and campaigns typically or in many cases [107] do not [108] include such restrictions [109] with at least one health organization calling for such [110].

          1.2.1.1.1.3. Con:
          For those who use drugs as a means to rebel against the status quo, doing what corporations advertise would be seen as unattractive.

          1.2.1.1.1.4. Pro:
          Alcohol advertisements have increased the number of young people who drink (p. 11 [111]). If advertising successfully promotes alcohol consumption, there is reason to believe it will do so for drugs, too.

          1.2.1.1.1.5. Con:
          Corporations are often seen as disconnected and inauthentic [112] when they try to appeal to young people through 'trendy' advertising. Therefore, such marketing may not succeed.

            1.2.1.1.1.5.1. Con:
            Many corporations are seen as 'cool' [113]. If drug companies can market themselves in such a way, they should be able to stimulate demand even among skeptical users.

        1.2.1.1.2. Pro:
        Without dealers whose flashy lifestyles [114] may look appealing to naive young people, drugs would lose some of their appeal.

        1.2.1.1.3. Con:
        The forbidden fruit effect only applies to a small subset of all drug users; many users take drugs [115] for reasons unrelated to the allure of prohibited or dangerous substances.

          1.2.1.1.3.1. Pro:
          The bandwagon effect [116] is another form of cognitive bias that means there could be many more new drug users if drugs are legalised, because their use will be popularised and normalised.

            1.2.1.1.3.1.1. Pro:
            Where marijuana use has been legalised, smoking games [117] have grown in popularity, indicating that drug use for fun potentially becomes much more commonplace and normalised under legalisation.

        1.2.1.1.4. Pro:
        Many young people start using drugs as a means of breaking the law. They will no longer have this motivation when drugs are legal.

          1.2.1.1.4.1. Con:
          If drugs are legalised, young people who purposefully seek to break the law will simply take up other, potentially more dangerous illegal activities.

            1.2.1.1.4.1.1. Pro:
            Some children engage in other illegal activities [118] such as shoplifting, dangerous driving, or vandalism as a way of rebelling and testing limits. Without illegal drugs, instances of these other risky behaviors may increase.

              1.2.1.1.4.1.1.1. Con:
              While these activities are certainly dangerous, few among them carry health risks as grave as those associated with excessive drug use.

                1.2.1.1.4.1.1.1.1. Con:
                Such activities may have risks which are as dangerous as drug-taking: young people may be arrested, spend time with criminals or engage in physical violence.

          1.2.1.1.4.2. Con:
          Given that drug use will still be illegal for underage people, it will still be attractive to young people who seek to break the law.

          1.2.1.1.4.3. Pro:
          Many young people purposefully break the law as a way to rebel and take risks [119].

      1.2.1.2. Pro:
      Legalising drugs may lead to many feeling as though drugs do not represent a significant danger and are therefore safe to consume.

        1.2.1.2.1. Con:
        With educational campaigns, informative product packaging, and other awareness-raising policies, governments can make the negative effects of drugs clear and discourage excessive use even if they are legalised.

          1.2.1.2.1.1. Con:
          People often do not trust official advice regarding drugs due to persistent and historical government misinformation.

            1.2.1.2.1.1.1. Pro:
            The US government has previously supported [120] the claim that marijuana use causes individuals to rape, murder, or commit suicide.

            1.2.1.2.1.1.2. Con:
            Trust in official advice may increase if the public were to see governments reverse their outdated approaches to drugs.

            1.2.1.2.1.1.3. Con:
            Government signals are effective [121] at communicating information, especially to those who otherwise may not care or think too much about issues like drug legalisation.

          1.2.1.2.1.2. Pro:
          Information about the content of legalised drugs would be more accurate and easier to find, helping people make more informed and safer choices.

            1.2.1.2.1.2.1. Pro:
            Increased education and negative advertisements have helped to decrease [122] the number of tobacco smokers.

            1.2.1.2.1.2.2. Pro:
            In the context of legalisation, education about drug use could become universally mandatory, just as patients being counselled on the effects of any new medications, treatments, or procedures is mandatory in healthcare settings [123].

          1.2.1.2.1.3. Pro:
          Many countries have mandated public health warning labels for all alcohol [124] and tobacco advertising and products.

            1.2.1.2.1.3.1. Pro:
            The World Health Organization Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (WHO FCTC [125]), a supranational agreement that is legally binding to 181 ratifying countries, lays out universal standards on stating the dangers of tobacco in advertising and products.

            1.2.1.2.1.3.2. Con:
            At present, the UK only has a system of self-regulation [126] with regards to health warning labels on alcohol-containing products.

        1.2.1.2.2. Con:
        Whether young people choose to use drugs is often influenced most by their family [127], peers [128], and the media [129] they consume.

          1.2.1.2.2.1. Con:
          If their government has banned drugs, this may be enough to make young people more skeptical of the safety of drugs even if their surrounding social sphere promotes drug use.

          1.2.1.2.2.2. Pro:
          Young people are more likely to consume drugs if their friends and families [130] do so.

        1.2.1.2.3. Con:
        The idea that illegal drugs are not necessarily more harmful than legal substances may in fact be a more accurate impression than the one given by current drugs classification systems.

          1.2.1.2.3.1. Pro:
          In a multi-criteria analysis of a range of recreational drugs, alcohol and tobacco were given higher scores for physical and psychological damage done to the user than many other currently illegal drugs (p. 1562 [131]).

            1.2.1.2.3.1.1. Pro:
            In an expert assessment [132] of the physical, psychological, and social harm caused by various recreational substances in the UK, it was found that alcohol and tobacco had harm ratings similar to drugs in the Class A and Class B categories of illicit substances respectively.

            1.2.1.2.3.1.2. Con:
            Crack cocaine, heroin and methamphetamine are all more dangerous [133] to the user than alcohol, and these substances as well as cocaine, amphetamine and GHB are all more harmful to users than tobacco.

        1.2.1.2.4. Con:
        Partially replacing [134] alcohol consumption with certain previously illegal drugs could be healthier and less dangerous for many people.

          1.2.1.2.4.1. Pro:
          There is good evidence [135] that the health risks of marijuana are less than those of alcohol.

          1.2.1.2.4.2. Con:
          Legal drugs may lead to more users mixing alcohol with other drugs, which would endanger their health [136] to a greater extent.

        1.2.1.2.5. Pro:
        When legal, drug use may be seen as a normal activity that most people take part in.

          1.2.1.2.5.1. Pro:
          Excessive consumption of alcohol, a legal substance, is now normalised [137] and even expected [138] in many cultures, even if [139] it was historically prohibited. If drugs are legalised, a similar cultural shift may occur.

          1.2.1.2.5.2. Pro:
          While many people currently consume drugs, their use is often not very visible [140] since they must hide it from others. The increased visibility of drug consumption with legalisation may normalise drug use.

          1.2.1.2.5.3. Con:
          Even when drugs are illegal, drug use is very widespread, especially within certain demographics [141]. The use of drugs is thus already normalised.

          1.2.1.2.5.4. Pro:
          If legalised, drug use may become more prominent in media and television, normalising it further. This may make users less reticent to experiment with them.

        1.2.1.2.6. Pro:
        A UK study [142] found that people tend to see legal substances and illegal drugs that carry less severe penalties as less harmful than illegal drugs with higher penalties. This suggests a correlation between perception of substance-related harms and their legal status.

      1.2.1.3. Pro:
      The legal repercussions of taking illegal drugs may deter many from using drugs.

        1.2.1.3.1. Con:
        Several studies have found no relation between police enforcement of drug bans and overall levels of drug consumption in the general population.

          1.2.1.3.1.1. Pro:
          While the US' drug law enforcement budget has risen by nearly 50% [143] since 2002, drug use since 2000 has been largely constant or decreasing [144] only slightly.

          1.2.1.3.1.2. Pro:
          The UK House of Commons Committee on Science and Technology found no evidence [145] to support the deterrent effect of making drugs more or less illegal through differing classifications.

        1.2.1.3.2. Pro:
        Singapore, along with other Southeast Asian countries, has successfully used strict drug prohibition laws to keep levels of drug use extremely low [146].

          1.2.1.3.2.1. Con:
          Despite such strict laws, the drug trade remains rampant [147] in many parts of Southeast Asia. Indeed, drug smuggling has gotten worse [148] in Singapore.

          1.2.1.3.2.2. Pro:
          Those found using drugs in Singapore can face prison time. Those found selling drugs can even face the death penalty [149]. These punishments act as immense deterrents, preventing people from committing such crimes.

        1.2.1.3.3. Con:
        Among intravenous drug users (IDUs), studies have found that law enforcement has no effect [150] on drug use.

        1.2.1.3.4. Con:
        The enforcement of drug bans rarely focuses on individuals using drugs recreationally. They tend to focus more on drug dealing and producing.

          1.2.1.3.4.1. Pro:
          In the UK [151], police forces have stopped closely enforcing drug laws against casual marijuana users.

          1.2.1.3.4.2. Con:
          There is often an incentive for police departments to focus on street-level possession and users, meaning that many who use drugs recreationally will be targetted.

            1.2.1.3.4.2.1. Pro:
            Such arrests are easy to make and allow police forces to increase arrest numbers, which can often be politically beneficial [152] for police leaders and politicians.

            1.2.1.3.4.2.2. Pro:
            Cracking down on large-scale, organised drug gangs can often be more expensive and more difficult [153] for police, leading them to focus more on the smaller-scale, more easily targetable cases of drug use or dealership.

            1.2.1.3.4.2.3. Con:
            There exists great backlash [154] against the overpolicing of drug crimes, leading many police departments to shift their focus away from arresting users.

          1.2.1.3.4.3. Pro:
          Drug crime is mostly fueled by drug gangs. This makes gangs the primary focus [155] of anti-drug policing.

        1.2.1.3.5. Pro:
        Punishments for drug possession can often include fines and imprisonment [156].

          1.2.1.3.5.1. Con:
          First-time offences for possession are often quite lenient: in the UK, those discovered may be offered 'community resolutions [157]'.

            1.2.1.3.5.1.1. Con:
            Elsewhere, drug possession offences often remain on one's record [158], meaning there can be long-term costs of one-time drug use, including difficulties finding jobs.

            1.2.1.3.5.1.2. Pro:
            If those convicted successfully complete their community resolutions, they can even have their drug arrest expunged from their record.

          1.2.1.3.5.2. Pro:
          In the UK, drug possession can warrant punishments of up to seven years [159] in prison.

          1.2.1.3.5.3. Pro:
          In US states with three strikes rules [160], illegal drug possession convictions can eventually lead to individuals facing life in prison.

    1.2.2. Pro:
    Drug legalisation is likely to reduce the price of drugs.

      1.2.2.1. Pro:
      A 2014 study [161] indicated that substances such as cocaine would be as much as ten times cheaper if legalised, which drug policy experts predict would at least triple the rates of hard substance misuse.

      1.2.2.2. Con:
      In the event of drug legalisation, taxes [162] on legalised substances are often proposed. These would likely deter excessive or widespread drug use in the population.

        1.2.2.2.1. Con:
        High prices of legalised drugs may push consumers back into the black market, where they can find cheaper drugs.

          1.2.2.2.1.1. Con:
          As long as taxes do not make the net prices of legal drugs surpass those of the black market, legal ones will not necessarily be more expensive.

          1.2.2.2.1.2. Pro:
          In some US states where marijuana is legal, there are still large marijuana black markets [163] due to regulations that increase the cost of legal marijuana.

        1.2.2.2.2. Pro:
        High prices [164] may reduce the volume consumed by regular users, as they cannot afford to buy large quantities.

        1.2.2.2.3. Pro:
        High prices [165] may discourage new users from trying drugs in the first place.

        1.2.2.2.4. Pro:
        Sin taxes on currently legal substances such as alcohol and tobacco have been found to reduce [166] their consumption.

      1.2.2.3. Pro:
      Since the legalisation of marijuana in some US states, its wholesale price has declined [167] every year.

      1.2.2.4. Pro:
      Breaking the law imposes specific costs on sellers which do not apply to legal businesses.

        1.2.2.4.1. Pro:
        The threat of being caught by law enforcement may discourage black market businesses from investing in productivity-increasing, immovable capital like expensive technology or machinery used in the production of drugs.

        1.2.2.4.2. Con:
        Black market products are often cheaper because sellers do not have to abide by legal regulations and restrictions.

          1.2.2.4.2.1. Con:
          Even if criminals were able to offer drugs at lower prices, people will likely still prefer to buy legal drugs.

            1.2.2.4.2.1.1. Con:
            Cheaper black market alcohol [168] is highly popular and accounts for a substantial amount of total alcohol trade in many lower-income countries in Central and Latin America and Africa, despite alcohol being a legally purchasable substance in such countries.

            1.2.2.4.2.1.2. Pro:
            People will likely not want to risk criminal punishment for buying illegal drugs when legal alternatives exist.

            1.2.2.4.2.1.3. Pro:
            Buying drugs illegally is generally more inconvenient and logistically harder than buying them legally would be.

              1.2.2.4.2.1.3.1. Pro:
              If convenience and chain stores that sell drugs become widespread, drugs can be ordered or bought at any time; in contrast, illegal dealers - who must necessarily remain hard to find - may be less reliable or readily available.

              1.2.2.4.2.1.3.2. Con:
              Illegal drug dealers often operate as part of large geographical networks, meaning even customers living in very remote areas [169] or heavily monitored academic institutions [170] can get drugs easily delivered to, and dealt from, their doorstep.

              1.2.2.4.2.1.3.3. Con:
              Most people begin trying drugs due to friends [171]. This means that they already have connections allowing them to easily obtain drugs.

          1.2.2.4.2.2. Pro:
          Currently, illegal producers keep costs down by using cheap substitute ingredients [172] to produce their drugs since they are not subject to any centralized form of quality control.

            1.2.2.4.2.2.1. Con:
            If illegal drug producers use ingredients that are too cheap or dangerous, consumers will choose to buy from better dealers, incentivizing them to keep their quality relatively high.

          1.2.2.4.2.3. Pro:
          Illegal businesses do not need to provide minimum wages or decent conditions for workers like legal businesses do. That means the cost of labor is lower for illegal drug businesses.

            1.2.2.4.2.3.1. Con:
            Legal businesses often underpay and exploit [173] their workers, especially in low-income countries where most current drug production happens.

            1.2.2.4.2.3.2. Pro:
            Worker exploitation in the drug trade is widespread [174].

          1.2.2.4.2.4. Con:
          Large corporations often have some say [175] in determining what regulations are created. It is likely that a balance will be reached to ensure the cost of regulations are not excessive.

        1.2.2.4.3. Pro:
        Transporting drugs is more expensive for illegal dealers, as they incur additional costs attempting to evade law enforcement [176].

          1.2.2.4.3.1. Pro:
          Legal producers do not have to worry about having their supply taken by the police, and thus do not have to invest in measures, like increased security [177], to preempt such losses.

          1.2.2.4.3.2. Con:
          Drug smugglers often use the same transportation methods as normal producers, particularly using cargo ships [178] where crews are unaware of the presence of drugs.

      1.2.2.5. Con:
      Drug legalisation will likely produce highly inefficient corporate oligopolies, which will lead to higher prices.

        1.2.2.5.1. Pro:
        Since Afghanistan accounts for more than two-thirds [179] of opium production, and cocaine production is concentrated in Latin America, large-scale access to these areas would be crucial to securing long-term profits as a legal recreational drugs provider. Only the largest international firms would be likely able to access such regions in this way.

        1.2.2.5.2. Con:
        Governments can take measures to avoid oligopolistic practices. For example, many legalisation proposals [180] see nationalised drug stores as an attractive solution.

        1.2.2.5.3. Con:
        Some research indicates that the markets of legalised substances such as alcohol are varied and not wholly oligopolistic [181].

        1.2.2.5.4. Pro:
        This has been the case in the beer industry, wherein the largest firms enjoy economies of scale [182] in production, leaving smaller businesses increasingly unable to compete.

      1.2.2.6. Con:
      Illegal drug prices are steadily falling [183] on their own, meaning it is unlikely that legalisation will make drugs significantly cheaper than illegal alternatives.

        1.2.2.6.1. Con:
        Even if illegal drug prices are falling, when drugs are illegal, sellers are able to artificially keep prices high.

          1.2.2.6.1.1. Pro:
          Dealers can engage in price discrimination [184], where they charge clients whom they know to be wealthier a larger amount of money for the same amount of illegal drugs.

          1.2.2.6.1.2. Con:
          The artificial increasing of prices by certain sellers simply encourages other dealers to offer lower prices so as to increase their market share.

            1.2.2.6.1.2.1. Con:
            When drugs are illegal, it is harder for consumers to compare the prices of different sellers, creating less incentive for sellers to outcompete each other and lower their prices.

              1.2.2.6.1.2.1.1. Pro:
              When drugs are illegal, dealers are unable to publicly market any changes in their prices.

              1.2.2.6.1.2.1.2. Pro:
              There exists no easy or centralized place where users can compare the price and quality of different dealers' products when drugs are illegal.

              1.2.2.6.1.2.1.3. Con:
              Online resources [185] have made it much easier for drug dealers to signal changes in price.

            1.2.2.6.1.2.2. Pro:
            This would create a 'race to the bottom [186]' where companies repeatedly lower their product prices to keep them cheaper than those of rivals, so as to increase demand for their products.

          1.2.2.6.1.3. Pro:
          Local dealers sometimes collectively engage in 'price-gouging [187]'.

        1.2.2.6.2. Pro:
        Over time, drug dealers have gotten better [188] at manufacturing and transporting drugs, decreasing their overall costs and allowing them to reduce prices.

    1.2.3. Con:
    In many places where drugs have been legalised, their consumption has not increased.

      1.2.3.1. Con:
      Following legalisation, Canada experienced shortages of cannabis [189], suggesting that legalisation had caused new individuals to begin using the drug.

        1.2.3.1.1. Con:
        The shortages in Canada were the result of slow processing and packaging [190], rather than a dramatic increase in demand.

        1.2.3.1.2. Pro:
        After legalisation, many stores were hit with unexpected demand [191] which quickly depleted their marijuana inventory.

      1.2.3.2. Pro:
      Marijuana use in the Netherlands has not increased [192] following decriminalisation, and cannabis consumption is lower than that in countries with stricter rules, like the UK.

      1.2.3.3. Con:
      In a 2018 UK poll, 26% of 18–24 year olds who had never previously tried cannabis stated that they would definitely or probably try it if it were legalised (p. 3 [193]).

      1.2.3.4. Pro:
      In some cases, drug legalisation has actually lead to decreases in drug use.

        1.2.3.4.1. Pro:
        Following decriminalisation, after a short period of increased consumption, drug use in Portugal fell below pre-decriminalisation levels [194].

        1.2.3.4.2. Pro:
        In the US state of Colorado, there has been a drop [195] in teen drug use following the legalisation of marijuana.

          1.2.3.4.2.1. Con:
          In Colorado, youth marijuana use has been steadily increasing [196] since 2017.

          1.2.3.4.2.2. Con:
          In the US, reductions in marijuana use among teens were seen across the country [197] in 2016, both in states which had legalised marijuana and in those who had not.

    1.2.4. Pro:
    If legal, drugs will likely be easier for individuals to access, which may lead to more people trying them.

      1.2.4.1. Con:
      There is no correlation between the availability of drugs and their frequency of use.

        1.2.4.1.1. Pro:
        Substance abuse increased during the COVID-19 [198] pandemic despite it being harder to access drugs during periods of lockdown. This indicates that psychological stress, rather than ease of access, is a primary determinant of frequent drug (mis)use.

        1.2.4.1.2. Con:
        The current American opioid crisis, in which hundreds of thousands of people have become addicted [199] to and overdosed [200] on opioids, has been caused by easy access to certain opioids due to their legality.

          1.2.4.1.2.1. Con:
          Many blame the current opioid crisis in the US on the irresponsible and criminal behavior of large pharmaceutical companies [201].

            1.2.4.1.2.1.1. Pro:
            Throughout the opioid crisis, pharmaceutical companies have heavily marketed their opioids as being medical necessities [202] to treat pain, and have paid doctors [203] to over-prescribe them.

          1.2.4.1.2.2. Con:
          Many people rely on, and get addicted to, opioid medication primarily because they cannot afford [204] other forms of legal pain management, and not just because opioids are legal.

          1.2.4.1.2.3. Pro:
          Since opioids like fentanyl can be obtained legally [205] with a prescription in the US, it has become much easier for individuals to access them, and consequently develop an addiction or overdose.

            1.2.4.1.2.3.1. Con:
            Fentanyl-caused overdoses are usually the result of particularly potent, illegally-produced [206] analogues [207] of fentanyl mixed with other substances. Full legalisation of all forms of fentanyl would regulate and restrict the production of these harmful analogues.

            1.2.4.1.2.3.2. Pro:
            Many of those who are addicted to opioids in the US initially began using them medicinally to deal with legitimate chronic pain [208] and health issues. That these individuals could access opioids easily and legally would have played a significant role in their choice to medicate with them.

            1.2.4.1.2.3.3. Con:
            Many people have supported their addiction by buying illegal drugs [209] once their prescriptions have ended, indicating that accessibility is not the most significant barrier to addiction.

              1.2.4.1.2.3.3.1. Con:
              Accessibility may play a significant role in preventing new, first-time addictions: 80% [210] of current US heroin users were misusing legal, prescription opioids before transitioning to heroin.

                1.2.4.1.2.3.3.1.1. Con:
                Recent research concludes that the first opioid used by one-third [211] of all patients commencing treatment for opioid use disorder is heroin.

          1.2.4.1.2.4. Pro:
          Other countries, where access to opioids is more controlled [212], have not experienced such crises.

            1.2.4.1.2.4.1. Pro:
            Countries in Europe have far fewer cases of opioid-related addiction because opioids are far harder [213] to access, indicating a relationship between accessibility and addiction.

      1.2.4.2. Pro:
      Legalising drugs will likely make it a lot easier for young people to get access to them, which may increase drug use among this demographic.

        1.2.4.2.1. Con:
        Surveys of young people indicate that it is already very easy for them to access illegal drugs, suggesting that legal status does not significantly affect access for this demographic.

          1.2.4.2.1.1. Pro:
          A 2018 UK poll [214] found that teenagers find it easier to acquire marijuana - an illegal substance - than alcohol.

          1.2.4.2.1.2. Pro:
          According to a 2020 poll [215], approximately 20% of people aged 16-19 in the UK had smoked cannabis in the 12 months prior and 59% of them considered it easy to buy illegal drugs. This indicates that young people have little difficulty accessing drugs regardless of their legal status.

        1.2.4.2.2. Pro:
        Once legalised, the only barrier to prevent minors from obtaining and using drugs would be age-related restrictions on their purchase. Current evidence suggests that this is an ineffective obstacle to underage access.

          1.2.4.2.2.1. Con:
          There still appears to be a significant difference in alcohol consumption between adult and underage drinkers: in the US, the adult drinking rate is 59% and 47% [216] in men and women respectively, compared to an underage rate of 29% [217].

          1.2.4.2.2.2. Pro:
          Underage people will easily be able to use fake IDs to buy legalised drugs, as they often do to buy currently legal substances that have age-related restrictions.

            1.2.4.2.2.2.1. Con:
            This will likely become less common with the invention and use of computerized ID systems [218].

            1.2.4.2.2.2.2. Pro:
            A study at a US university found that almost two-thirds [219] of the underage students asked had used a fake ID at least once to obtain alcohol.

            1.2.4.2.2.2.3. Con:
            Liquor store owners and door staff at pubs, bars, and nightclubs report that they are highly adept [220] at identifying fraudulent identification.

          1.2.4.2.2.3. Con:
          It is easier for governments to prevent the sale of legal substances to underage people than it is to prevent the sale of illegal substances.

            1.2.4.2.2.3.1. Pro:
            The penalties for selling legal products with age restrictions to underage people are often significant [221], so legal sellers will likely be deterred from doing it.

            1.2.4.2.2.3.2. Con:
            It is relatively easy for police to currently find illegal drug dealers that form part of a drug network, as these can be highly conspicuous [222]. Legal, independent businesses selling these to young people could be harder to spot.

          1.2.4.2.2.4. Pro:
          A large proportion of underage people who smoke or drink report that they are given tobacco and alcohol by of-age [223] friends or family members [224], or pay them to buy these substances on their behalf.

          1.2.4.2.2.5. Pro:
          Underage drinking and smoking is highly common, while illegal drug use among minors is less common.

            1.2.4.2.2.5.1. Con:
            -> See 1.2.4.2.2.2.1.

            1.2.4.2.2.5.2. Pro:
            Statistics indicate that underage drinking is relatively commonplace in the US and UK, while illegal drug-taking among this demographic is not.

              1.2.4.2.2.5.2.1. Pro:
              A study [225] found that underage drinking accounted for 17.5% of total US expenditure on alcohol consumption in 2001, with 47% of all 12- to 20-year-olds admitting to being current drinkers.

              1.2.4.2.2.5.2.2. Pro:
              A 2014 UK report [226] found that 38% of 11- to 15-year-old pupils surveyed had tried alcohol, but only 15% had taken drugs.

            1.2.4.2.2.5.3. Pro:
            Underage smoking is incredibly common in the US [227] and UK [228], despite only adults being legally permitted to purchase tobacco in both countries.

        1.2.4.2.3. Con:
        When recreational marijuana was legalised in Colorado, the usage rate [229] among teens and children stayed the same.

        1.2.4.2.4. Pro:
        It has been shown [230] that when school-aged children perceive their access to illicit substances as being easy, they are more likely to use such substances.

      1.2.4.3. Con:
      It is already easy for many people to access drugs.

        1.2.4.3.1. Pro:
        People [231] can buy drugs [232] on the street easily from street-level dealers or gangs.

        1.2.4.3.2. Pro:
        Using the dark web [233] is an easy way for people to buy illegal drugs.

        1.2.4.3.3. Con:
        -> See 1.2.2.4.2.1.3.

    1.2.5. Con:
    Increased drug use does not necessarily correlate to problematic drug use.

      1.2.5.1. Pro:
      It is social stigma [234] that perpetuates problematic drug use. If that stigma were diminished, increased drug use would not lead to increased problematic drug use.

        1.2.5.1.1. Con:
        Legalising drugs will not necessarily destigmatise them.

          1.2.5.1.1.1. Pro:
          Drug users have often been portrayed as 'dirty', morally corrupt and unstable in media for a long time. That image will likely remain after legalisation.

            1.2.5.1.1.1.1. Pro:
            Large-scale anti-drug advertising has often leaned on the idea that drug use leads to extreme volatility and violence [235].

            1.2.5.1.1.1.2. Con:
            There is likely to be a more visible countermessage to this image as businesses seek to market their drugs as safe and not harmful. Over time, this portrayal will thus fade away.

            1.2.5.1.1.1.3. Pro:
            Common terms like 'junkie' and 'crackhead' dehumanize and otherize drug users, while ubiquitous references to former users as 'clean' imply that those who still have substance use disorders are 'dirty' (p. 2 [236]).

            1.2.5.1.1.1.4. Con:
            If drug use becomes more normalised due to legalisation, it is likely that prejudice will slowly fade away.

              1.2.5.1.1.1.4.1. Con:
              Stigma against drug use is often caused by the impulsive and dangerous actions of some drug users. This will likely persist regardless of legalisation.

                1.2.5.1.1.1.4.1.1. Pro:
                Even if people see only a few people being violent or troublesome while taking drugs, confirmation bias [237] means the prejudice will likely remain. The chances of this happening are higher if drug use becomes more visible due to legalisation.

                1.2.5.1.1.1.4.1.2. Con:
                Anti-social behavior has many causes (pp. 23-24 [238]), not all of which are villified and stigmatised in general. If normalised, drug use may be treated similarly.

              1.2.5.1.1.1.4.2. Pro:
              When they are no longer confessing to committing crimes, more public figures will likely be transparent about their own drug use, providing a counter-image to harmful stereotypes.

              1.2.5.1.1.1.4.3. Pro:
              Drugs are used in a variety of ways by many different people. Seeing that diversity may dispel many of the discriminatory views that people hold.

                1.2.5.1.1.1.4.3.1. Pro:
                -> See 1.2.1.2.5.2.

                1.2.5.1.1.1.4.3.2. Pro:
                It is likely that, with more visibility, skeptical people will be confronted with more cases of non-harmful drug use which, over time, may change their minds.

              1.2.5.1.1.1.4.4. Con:
              Often, stigma against drugs associated with poor or minority communities is more entrenched because it intersects with other discriminatory stigmas. This multilayered stigma is less likely to go away with drug legalisation.

                1.2.5.1.1.1.4.4.1. Pro:
                Drug epidemics among white, suburban individuals in the United States are often treated with more sympathy and less aggression [239] than equivalent ones in African-American communities.

                1.2.5.1.1.1.4.4.2. Pro:

            1.5.8.1.1.3.1. Pro:
            -> See 1.5.1.1.

            1.5.8.1.1.3.2. Pro:
            In countries [600] where a significant part of the population relies on public health services, the treatment of health issues caused by drug abuse could create an unfair economic burden on taxpayers.

              1.5.8.1.1.3.2.1. Con:
              Healthcare-related economic burdens are not so much caused by individual choices, but by underfunding [601] and dysfunction within public healthcare systems.

              1.5.8.1.1.3.2.2. Pro:
              If the overall costs of administering healthcare go up, it may lead to non-drug users making higher contribution payments or losing some guaranteed insurance benefits.

            1.5.8.1.1.3.3. Con:
            Smoking [602] tobacco around others means that non-consenting people suffer harm, yet tobacco is legal.

              1.5.8.1.1.3.3.1. Con:
              In the UK, there are bans [603] on smoking in and around enclosed public spaces so as to minimise the harmful effects of smoking on non-smokers.

              1.5.8.1.1.3.3.2. Pro:
              Secondhand smoke can be particularly harmful for children [604], who may develop asthma or infections as a result.

              1.5.8.1.1.3.3.3. Pro:
              People can develop lung problems and even cancer [605] from secondhand smoke.

              1.5.8.1.1.3.3.4. Con:
              Studies indicate that second-hand exposure to freebase cocaine (crack) smoke, by contrast, is unlikely [606] to have harmful effects.

              1.5.8.1.1.3.3.5. Con:
              There is significant support to ban smoking [607].

            1.5.8.1.1.3.4. Con:
            Plenty of human actions will have potentially negative impacts on others. It does not follow that states ought to ban those actions.

              1.5.8.1.1.3.4.1. Con:
              According to the Harm Principle [608], the actions of individuals can be curtailed when they infringe upon the rights of others. Insofar as drug use has the potential to harm others, it must be banned.

              1.5.8.1.1.3.4.2. Pro:
              Driving can have harmful [609] and potentially fatal consequences for many non-drivers, yet driving is still legal.

              1.5.8.1.1.3.4.3. Pro:
              Certain forms of low-wage work may damage the health of workers and make it harder [610] for them to take care of their families. Yet, these are accepted despite the harms they incur to others.

          1.5.8.1.1.4. Con:
          -> See 1.3.1.2.3.4.1.3.

        1.5.8.1.2. Pro:
        According to Human Rights Watch [611], subjecting people to disproportionate criminal sanctions for the personal use of drugs "infringes on their autonomy".

          1.5.8.1.2.1. Con:
          Under legalisation, employers worried about lowered productivity may excessively monitor employees to ensure they are not using drugs, which would infringe on the personal freedom of workers.

            1.5.8.1.2.1.1. Pro:
            If we legalise all drugs, people would likely face a greater number of mandatory drug tests [612] from their employers.

              1.5.8.1.2.1.1.1. Con:
              Most employers do not regularly [613] check their employees for alcohol consumption.

              1.5.8.1.2.1.1.2. Pro:
              Likewise, bosses may force employees to disclose drug use.

            1.5.8.1.2.1.2. Con:
            Bosses will likely not waste time and money on routinely monitoring the drug use of all their employees. It is easier and cheaper to reserve tests and surveillance exclusively for cases where they have good reason to suspect an employee is intoxicated while at work.

            1.5.8.1.2.1.3. Con:
            If drug use is legalised, employees will be able to take legal action if their rights are being infringed upon by drug monitoring.

        1.5.8.1.3. Con:
        -> See 1.4.1.

        1.5.8.1.4. Con:
        Since the choice of whether or not to consume drugs is a relatively inconsequential one, it matters very little to citizens' overall personal autonomy if they are denied this choice.

          1.5.8.1.4.1. Pro:
          People can already change their mental states with alcohol or even without substances [614], so the option of legally consuming a greater array of mind-altering drugs would not represent a significant expansion on the current legal activities available.

          1.5.8.1.4.2. Con:
          Free choice is not important just because it allows important decisions to be made autonomously. It is important precisely because only individuals are able to decide what choices are important to them.

            1.5.8.1.4.2.1. Con:
            Free choice can never be absolute [615] as one's freedom must always be tempered by the needs of others and the community. As such, relatively unimportant choices are the preferable freedoms to limit.

      1.5.8.2. Pro:
      Governments frequently put restrictions on actions that may cause citizens to harm themselves or others.

        1.5.8.2.1. Pro:
        -> See 1.5.7.3.

        1.5.8.2.2. Pro:
        Most states ban speech that can cause immediate harms or incite violence [616] so as to protect other citizens.

        1.5.8.2.3. Con:
        Most governments actually do allow their citizens to do a wide variety of potentially dangerous activities, from extreme sports [617] to drinking alcohol and driving cars, which can lead to death or injury.

        1.5.8.2.4. Con:
        The fact that governments do frequently restrict the actions of their citizens does not make this justified.

        1.5.8.2.5. Pro:
        Many countries ban [618] or heavily restrict [619] personal ownership of guns and other weapons.

      1.5.8.3. Pro:
      States are meant to be moral arbiters (p. 710 [620]) who determine whether certain behaviors are acceptable or not. Given the dangers of drugs, states must declare them unacceptable.

        1.5.8.3.1. Con:
        Democratic [621] states are meant to aggregate and represent the views of their citizens. Thus, if the public favours legalisation of drugs, the laws their state enshrines should reflect that preference.

          1.5.8.3.1.1. Con:
          Support for legalising stronger drugs than marijuana is actually quite low, even in the US [622].

            1.5.8.3.1.1.1. Con:
            In places like Oregon [623], where general drug decriminalisation has been put to a vote, it has often won, indicating that people are happy to decriminalise more drugs than just marijuana.

            1.5.8.3.1.1.2. Pro:
            In many parts of Asia [624], where there exist strict anti-drug laws, there is little support for decriminalisation or legalisation of any sort.

          1.5.8.3.1.2. Pro:
          In many countries, large numbers of voters support the legalisation of marijuana, particularly in the US [625] and UK [626].

          1.5.8.3.1.3. Con:
          Even if many people support drug use and legalisation, insofar as it is so potentially dangerous, states are justified in usurping that democratic will to protect the interests of their citizens.

        1.5.8.3.2. Pro:
        According to utilitarian accounts [627], the primary purpose of a state is to prevent harm coming to its citizens. As such, it is a state's purpose to determine what actions cause harm and stop them.

        1.5.8.3.3. Con:
        State power is always potentially corrosive to the rights of individuals. Therefore, governments should, in most cases, allow citizens to determine their own best interests for themselves (2.1 [628]).

          1.5.8.3.3.1. Con:
          This rule would allow an unreasonably small number of cases where states are allowed to intervene in the affairs of citizens. Most people accept that states can adopt moral stances and prevent certain dangerous actions.

            1.5.8.3.3.1.1. Pro:
            State intervention is often justified in cases where individuals do not use reason effectively [629]. Insofar as individuals consume drugs despite the harms, drug policy can be considered such an area.

            1.5.8.3.3.1.2. Con:
            While this does suggest that states would be justified in adopting a moralising, anti-drug stance and producing corresponding propaganda, it does not follow that they should ban drugs altogether.

      1.5.8.4. Con:
      Criminalisation has not protected citizens from drugs.

        1.5.8.4.1. Con:
        While the War on Drugs has failed, there exist other proposals for 'smart' [630] drug prohibition which may work better. As such, the historical record is insufficient to declare that criminalisation has failed.

        1.5.8.4.2. Pro:
        According to the Global Commission on Drug Policy [631], the War on Drugs has been a failure.

        1.5.8.4.3. Pro:
        Drug use has increased [632] as drug enforcement has, indicating that states have failed to protect citizens from drugs.
