Discussion Name:
Do we need religion for morality?

1. Thesis:
We need religion for morality.

  1.1. Con:
  Morality can be based on rationality and reason without recourse to foundational concepts like gods or scriptures.

    1.1.1. Pro:
    Coherentism [1] is a philosophical approach that establishes an ever more information-dense collection of interlocking, self-supporting ideas without the need for foundational assumptions. It says something should be provisionally regarded as true if it is consistent with everything else that is known. A coherentist morality could be built upon the totality of human experience and knowledge without the need for religion or gods

      1.1.1.1. Pro:
      Rights-based law develops a coherentist body of self-supporting doctrine by re-iteratively evaluating specific examples (slavery is wrong) in the context of shared general principles (people should be treated fairly and as equals). The consensus of belief that emerges (a reflective equilibrium [2]) is more information-dense than the starting position and can provide a more enlightened context for evaluating further specific examples (homosexuals should have the same right to marry as heterosexuals).

      1.1.1.2. Pro:
      Popper [3] developed coherentism into a theory of science by saying hypotheses should be discarded if refuted [4] by experimental evidence but added to the collected body of scientific knowledge if they survive experimental testing. Experiments should be designed to test precise and (ideally) unexpected predictions from a new hypothesis. Ideas that cannot possibly be refuted [5] are dismissed as non-scientific.

        1.1.1.2.1. Con:
        Ideas of inherent or intrinsic value cannot be experimentally determined. Because they "cannot possibly be refuted," they should be "dismissed as non-scientific." This means that statements like, "humans should live sustainably," must be dismissed because the values of humans and sustainable living cannot be determined.

      1.1.1.3. Pro:
      Combining ‘reflective equilibrium [6]’ with ‘conjecture and refutation [7]’ creates a scientific world view and a moral/ legal system without the need for foundational, metaphysical structures like religion. Ideas are accepted if they add to a self-supporting coherent and functional world view (e.g. people should be treated fairly as equals, slavery is wrong and gay marriage is right). Ideas (e.g. Nazism) are rejected if they are incompatible and/or lead to catastrophic societal breakdown.

        1.1.1.3.1. Con:
        The claim assumes that these tools will remove most moral conflict without the need for appeal to the metaphysical. However, it is possible for two conflicting coherent and functional world views to exist using this method. Therefore, it may still be necessary to appeal to metaphysical concepts to determine a true rightness between two coherent but competing world views. An example is the conflict of WW2 where both sides felt their side was morally righteous and reasonably sound.

        1.1.1.3.2. Con:
        Reflective equilibrium cannot exist without metaphysical concepts. It has been argued [8] that reflective equilibrium cannot be both an objectivist modal that appeals to our broad "sense of justice," while simultaneously rejecting foundationalism.

          1.1.1.3.2.1. Con:
          Chimpanzees understand metaphysical concepts (e.g. fairness) [9] without the need for religion. Likewise, reflective equilibrium can refer to metaphysical concepts (e.g. "our broad sense of justice"), without an underpinning need for religion.

            1.1.1.3.2.1.1. Con:
            Chimpanzees may have a sense of fairness, but this does not refute a religious origin. It can be argued that a broad sense of justice isn't exclusive to humanity, but that humanity has a greater understanding of it.

          1.1.1.3.2.2. Con:
          Sem de Maagt [10] concedes that "Obviously, reflective equilibrium can account for this minimal type of objectivity. In reflective equilibrium, a moral belief is not simply justified when an individual actually holds a specific moral belief but only when this belief coheres, in the appropriate way, with all possibly relevant considerations to the issue at hand.". That is sufficient to demonstrate that reflective equilibrium can produce some measure of moral objectivity.

          1.1.1.3.2.3. Con:
          Reflective equilibrium can be simultaneously objective and non-foundational because it creates a network structure of provisional ‘truths’ rather than the single pillar of absolute truth produced by foundationalism. With a pillar, if the base fails then the whole structure falls, whereas with a network each link can be amended whilst leaving the overall structure intact

        1.1.1.3.3. Con:
        The shared sense of justice in the reflective equilibrium modal is more compatible with a religious framework that provides a universal moral standard for the world.

        1.1.1.3.4. Pro:
        Reflective equilibrium predicts that it is possible for two or more conflicting coherent and functional world views to arise and co-exist within a single society. This is consistent with the observed existence of groups like Amish and Burning Man festival attendees within the USA, where both groups consider themselves ‘moral’ from within their own incompatible terms of reference

        1.1.1.3.5. Pro:
        The idea that morality must arise from a single, absolute, religiously-derived moral framework is refuted by the existence of a plurality of subgroups within a society (e.g. Amish and Burning Man festival attendees) each of whom consider themselves moral from within their own incompatible terms of reference.
