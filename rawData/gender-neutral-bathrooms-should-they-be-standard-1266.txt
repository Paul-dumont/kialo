Discussion Name:
Gender Neutral Bathrooms: Should They be Standard?

1. Thesis:
All public bathrooms should be gender neutral.

  1.1. Pro:
  The design of gender neutral bathrooms best meets the practical needs of all users.

    1.1.1. Pro:
    Gender neutral bathrooms could make facilities more accessible to caregivers.

      1.1.1.1. Con:
      Not all people with physical and mental disabilities have caregivers. For such individuals, gender neutral bathrooms with individual stalls that need to be locked for privacy and safety may pose safety risks.

        1.1.1.1.1. Con:
        Regardless of the existence of gender neutral bathrooms for able-bodied people, accessible bathrooms [1] will persist, especially because they are increasingly mandated in many countries.

        1.1.1.1.2. Pro:
        For some individuals with disabilities, caregivers may not be available (e.g., deceased parent caregivers [2]) or affordable [3], especially given the wide disability pay gap [4]. The unique needs of these individuals should not be overlooked.

        1.1.1.1.3. Con:
        Gender neutral bathrooms benefit older adults [5] without a carer who need assistance. As helpers could be of any gender, gender neutral bathrooms would make it easier for those who need assistance to ask others for help.

      1.1.1.2. Con:
      Accessible toilets are typically gender neutral and, as such, pose no issues for carers or those with disabilities. Therefore, there is no need for an overhaul of all bathrooms.

        1.1.1.2.1. Con:
        Around the world, there is a shortage of accessible toilets [6] for people with disabilities. Where this is true, people with disabilities may need caregiver support to use bathrooms designed for able-bodied people.

        1.1.1.2.2. Pro:
        The symbol used to indicate that a bathroom is accessible is gender neutral, thereby rendering any bathroom with this sticker gender neutral by default [7].

        1.1.1.2.3. Pro:
        People with disabilities constitute a minority [8]. Therefore, it is more cost effective to clearly indicate that accessible toilets are gender neutral, as they are fewer in number than bathrooms intended for able-bodied people.

          1.1.1.2.3.1. Con:
          The prevalence of disabilities [9] increases with age, due to conditions such as arthritis. Given that many societies are ageing at an unprecedented rate, the need for accessible toilets will increase.

          1.1.1.2.3.2. Pro:
          Guidelines either do not specify the number of bathrooms [10] that should be disability friendly or require only one bathroom [11] per gender to be accessible. Given the extra cost involved in making toilets accessible, establishments would likely prefer to have as few accessible toilets as possible.

      1.1.1.3. Pro:
      Care assistants may find gender segregated bathrooms [12] judgemental or unwelcoming if they are caring for someone of another gender.

        1.1.1.3.1. Pro:
        Caregivers of individuals with invisible disabilities [13] could benefit from gender neutral bathrooms as they reduce the risk of confrontation if their gender is different from that of the person they are caring for.

        1.1.1.3.2. Pro:
        As formal and informal caregivers are overwhelmingly women [14], gender segregated bathrooms pose a problem that disproportionately impacts women who care for men.

        1.1.1.3.3. Con:
        Older adults often wear diapers [15] when they anticipate being in places where gender neutral bathrooms are not an option. This would eliminate any social risks associated with using gendered bathrooms.

        1.1.1.3.4. Con:
        Anecdotal reports suggest that announcing "Male caretaker coming in" or "Female caretaker coming in" before entering gender segregated bathrooms is an effective way to avert negative reactions [16] from others using the facility.

      1.1.1.4. Con:
      Accessible bathrooms can be designed so as to render caregiver assistance unnecessary, negating the need for gender neutral bathrooms.

        1.1.1.4.1. Con:
        Poorly designed accessible bathrooms may still require caregiver assistance for entry and exit, in which cases gender neutral bathrooms would be preferable.

        1.1.1.4.2. Pro:
        Examples of bathrooms that render caregiver assistance unnecessary include disability-friendly [17] and senior-friendly bathrooms [18].

      1.1.1.5. Pro:
      Gender neutral bathrooms would give everyone access to baby changing tables which are currently more commonly found in women's public bathrooms.

        1.1.1.5.1. Con:
        Like any other object in a public bathroom, baby changing tables are dirty [19] and can cause serious illnesses. To circumvent this problem and gendered access, baby changing stations should be built as separate gender neutral facilities.

        1.1.1.5.2. Pro:
        Sometimes fathers are accosted by female restaurant staff and patrons [20] because they have used the women's bathrooms to diaper their baby. This is only necessary as they lack their own baby-changing facilities.

        1.1.1.5.3. Con:
        Asymmetric access to childcare facilities can be resolved by adding changing tables to men's facilities [21], instead of making all bathrooms gender neutral.

          1.1.1.5.3.1. Pro:
          Installing changing tables in only women's bathrooms promotes gender inequality, as it implicitly allows men to avoid participating in this caregiving act, while designating it entirely to women.

          1.1.1.5.3.2. Con:
          Installing changing tables in men's rooms is not resource efficient; therefore, it's preferable to make women's rooms (with changing tables) and men's rooms (without changing tables) gender neutral, so parents of both sexes can use them.

        1.1.1.5.4. Pro:
        Changing tables are primarily present in women's bathrooms as a result of stereotypical assumptions about gender roles that may not reflect reality [22].

          1.1.1.5.4.1. Pro:
          Regardless of frequency of use, guardians should have equal access to necessary facilitates to care for their children's most basic needs.

          1.1.1.5.4.2. Con:
          Women are more likely to stay at home [23] or work fewer hours [24] to care for their family. Thus, it makes sense for their bathrooms to have changing tables, as they are more likely to leave the house accompanied by their children than men.

      1.1.1.6. Pro:
      Parents often have to negotiate bathroom use for their different-gender children to ensure their safety or supervision.

        1.1.1.6.1. Con:
        Exposure to adults of the opposite sex is likely to be a more frequent issue in gender neutral bathrooms, as people of all genders share the same space.

        1.1.1.6.2. Pro:
        Where parents are not allowed to accompany children into bathrooms intended for the other gender, they must take other precautions, such as waiting outside and asking their child to only use stalls within earshot [25].

        1.1.1.6.3. Pro:
        Children have been murdered [26] in bathrooms, while their caregivers waited outside. Gender neutral bathrooms could allay parental anxieties [27] triggered by these events.

        1.1.1.6.4. Pro:
        Children [28] may feel uncomfortable or insecure about accessing bathrooms intended for individuals of another gender.

          1.1.1.6.4.1. Pro:
          Once children reach a certain age, they often experience a sense of discomfort [29] when in a bathroom not intended for their sex.

          1.1.1.6.4.2. Con:
          Children's discomfort with those of other genders is not natural but rather an issue of education and socialization. It is likely to diminish with time and use of gender neutral bathrooms.

            1.1.1.6.4.2.1. Pro:
            Children start with a neutral response to those of the opposite sex and are gradually socialized [30] to alter their response. As such, a change in social norms would likely embrace this initial neutrality.

            1.1.1.6.4.2.2. Con:
            Whether discomfort with people of other genders is a natural response or emerges due to socialization and learning has not been determined.

            1.1.1.6.4.2.3. Pro:
            In Scandinavian countries, adult family members being nude in the presence of children is perfectly normal [31].

              1.1.1.6.4.2.3.1. Con:
              That children in Scandinavia are comfortable in the presence of nude adults does not necessarily imply that children are naturally comfortable in such contexts; this could also be the product of socialization.

              1.1.1.6.4.2.3.2. Con:
              Scandinavian parents may be comfortable with their children being nude within the home [32], but this does not necessarily imply that they are comfortable with them being nude in the presence of strangers [33].

              1.1.1.6.4.2.3.3. Pro:
              Scandinavian cultural norms surrounding nudity are conducive to positive child development [34], as they implicitly teach children to be comfortable in their own skin rather than regard them as shameful.

            1.1.1.6.4.2.4. Con:
            Children do not fear strangers indiscriminately; instead, they learn to fear strangers, particularly men, who pose threats to their safety. Thus, because stranger danger [35] is real, fearing strangers serves an adaptive purpose.

        1.1.1.6.5. Con:
        Taking young children into a bathroom not intended for their sex is not typically problematic, meaning that gender neutral bathrooms would make little difference.

          1.1.1.6.5.1. Con:
          Some children develop more slowly [36] and, thus, require additional parental supervision. This may lead to significantly older children being in bathrooms intended for the opposite sex, which may cause discomfort to other patrons.

          1.1.1.6.5.2. Pro:
          Use of bathrooms intended for the other sex is considered problematic primarily because of safety concerns for women [37]. As young children are typically physically small and helpless, they pose no threat.

          1.1.1.6.5.3. Con:
          Opposite-sex parents cannot freely assist each other to change a diaper or toilet train a toddler together. Therefore the burden is given to one caregiver or the parents must break the gender bathroom rule.

          1.1.1.6.5.4. Pro:
          Some businesses require only boys older than 6 [38] to use the men's room. This proves that use of women's bathrooms by very young boys is not considered problematic.

    1.1.2. Con:
    Many urinary and digestive conditions are caused or worsened [39] by anxiety. Gender neutral bathrooms may deprive people of their need for privacy to deal with such sensitive conditions.

      1.1.2.1. Pro:
      Shy bladder syndrome [40] is one of the most common social phobias. Affected individuals may feel more anxious and vulnerable [41] in gender neutral bathrooms, making it more difficult for them to urinate.

        1.1.2.1.1. Pro:
        Gender neutral bathrooms accommodate a greater number of patrons, making it harder than normal for people with shy bladder syndrome to urinate in public bathrooms.

        1.1.2.1.2. Con:
        Shy bladder syndrome is partially attributable to a lack of privacy [42]; thus, urinal use may explain why this condition is more common among men. Creating stalls that provide privacy and separation from others would alleviate such problems.

          1.1.2.1.2.1. Con:
          Men with shy bladder syndrome could urinate using the stalls in men's bathrooms rather than urinals, as this provides greater privacy and may reduce any anxiety they may otherwise experience. A gender neutral bathroom is not needed to facilitate this.

          1.1.2.1.2.2. Pro:
          Urinals have a net negative impact on the psychological well-being [43] of men. Therefore, men may be better served by a facility that favors the use of stalls.

            1.1.2.1.2.2.1. Pro:
            Urinal use is governed by several social norms relating to the use of adjacent urinals [44] and making small talk [45] while urinating. These norms are anxiety-inducing, and breaching them can lead to conflict [46].

            1.1.2.1.2.2.2. Con:
            If urinals have a negative impact on men, then the appropriate solution would be to remove them from all public bathrooms, regardless of whether they are gender neutral or segregated, rather than to make all bathrooms gender neutral.

        1.1.2.1.3. Pro:
        People may feel particularly nervous interacting [47] with those of another gender, exacerbating their shy bladder syndrome when using gender neutral bathrooms.

      1.1.2.2. Con:
      Nervousness and embarrassment are most often caused by the presence of others [48], irrespective of gender. Thus, segregated bathrooms do not eliminate this risk for people with medical conditions.

        1.1.2.2.1. Con:
        Individuals reportedly experience higher levels of nervousness and anxiety when talking to members of the opposite gender [49].

        1.1.2.2.2. Pro:
        Those with medical conditions that affect their bowel or bladder commonly tailor their timetables [50] and travel to minimise their need for public toilet use. This suggests that gender segregated public toilets are insufficient to meet their needs at present.

      1.1.2.3. Pro:
      Digestive disorders can be worsened by stressful situations and nervousness [51]. Gender neutral bathrooms may have a disproportionate effect on individuals with these conditions.

        1.1.2.3.1. Pro:
        Individuals with IBD and IBS [52] often need to use the toilet urgently. As women are expected to be discreet [53] about bodily functions, notably in front of men, rushing to the bathrooms may lead to nervousness about appearance.

        1.1.2.3.2. Con:
        Gender neutral bathrooms may combat gender-based nervousness and anxiety, by normalising the mixing of men and women in public bathrooms. Thus, nervousness is unlikely to be long lasting.

        1.1.2.3.3. Pro:
        Women's bathrooms are notorious for their camaraderie [54], which may mitigate nervousness amongst women with IBS, by making them feel safe and supported. Gender neutral bathrooms are likely to stall these interactions.

        1.1.2.3.4. Con:
        Gender neutral bathrooms are likely to be highly functional and anonymous spaces, due to the increased footfall. This dynamic may enable greater anonymity and, thus, reduce nervousness about people noticing time taken in the bathroom.

      1.1.2.4. Con:
      Gender neutral bathrooms will likely be designed with greater privacy [55], due to the mixing of multiple genders. As such, they may better meet the privacy needs of those with medical conditions.

        1.1.2.4.1. Pro:
        Many people try to avoid being heard [56] while using the toilet, for example, by turning on the hand drier or running the tap. As gender neutral bathrooms [57] commonly have floor-to-ceiling walls, these sound concerns are likely to be reduced.

        1.1.2.4.2. Pro:
        Those who struggle with IBS or urinary incontinence often need to change their underwear [58] in a stall. Floor-to-ceiling stalls better enable this, as other patrons can easily see under the door.

        1.1.2.4.3. Con:
        As many facilities will likely be minimally renovated to become gender neutral, increased privacy is not a guarantee.

    1.1.3. Pro:
    It may be more efficient to have one large bathroom for all genders than two smaller ones for separate genders.

      1.1.3.1. Pro:
      Having one gender neutral bathroom [59] in place of two gender segregated bathrooms could be more time-efficient for users.

        1.1.3.1.1. Con:
        There is no guarantee that a new gender neutral bathroom would be as large as two combined gender segregated bathrooms. These smaller bathrooms could be inefficient.

          1.1.3.1.1.1. Con:
          Instead of transforming gender segregated bathrooms into a single large gender neutral facility, it may be more feasible to make existing bathrooms open to all genders.

            1.1.3.1.1.1.1. Pro:
            In monuments, venues, and sites of historical importance, it may be neither architecturally feasible [60] nor permissible [61] to reconstruct, rebuild, and redesign bathrooms. Making existing bathrooms open to all genders may be preferable.

              1.1.3.1.1.1.1.1. Con:
              Bathrooms in monuments and heritage buildings may not be functional or hardy enough to be open for public use; instead, facilities may be located in an adjacent museum or visitor centre which can be created to meet modern standards (p. 123 [62]).

              1.1.3.1.1.1.1.2. Con:
              Renovating heritage buildings and monuments based on modern considerations could distort their history [63]. Specifically, redesigning gender segregated bathrooms into gender neutral ones could erase the history of gender segregation.

                1.1.3.1.1.1.1.2.1. Con:
                The US has a history of racially segregated bathrooms, which is reflected in their historical artifacts [64]. As this history is remembered without maintaining such a practice, the same could be done for gender segregation.

                1.1.3.1.1.1.1.2.2. Pro:
                Heritage sites and monuments are conceptualized as "living witnesses of their age-old traditions" (p. 1 [65]). To this end, they ought to be restored and preserved [66], rather than modernized.

              1.1.3.1.1.1.1.3. Pro:
              Renovating existing gender segregated bathrooms to suit all genders would require minimal changes [67] and preserve the existing structure of historically important buildings.

              1.1.3.1.1.1.1.4. Pro:
              Bathrooms in historical sites are commonly updated to meet current health codes [68]. Within the same process, making them gender neutral would be simple and cost effective.

            1.1.3.1.1.1.2. Con:
            Unless upgraded to meet the needs of all genders, bathrooms are likely to be unwelcoming to those of other genders and remain gender segregated by default.

          1.1.3.1.1.2. Pro:
          Gender segregated bathrooms may not always be located next to each other in public buildings, so forming one gender neutral bathroom from two gendered ones would not always be a case of knocking through a dividing wall and doubling floorspace.

          1.1.3.1.1.3. Con:
          Urinals take up less space than stalls, meaning that men's bathrooms are a more efficient [69] use of space than gender neutral bathrooms - especially single-occupancy ones - would be.

        1.1.3.1.2. Pro:
        Gender neutral bathrooms would be easier and, therefore, faster to locate as individuals would not have to find the appropriate gender segregated bathroom.

        1.1.3.1.3. Con:
        If existing gender segregated bathrooms are converted into two smaller gender neutral facilities, patrons will continue to be split between the rooms, leading to no increase in efficiency.

          1.1.3.1.3.1. Con:
          Given that men's rooms commonly have a high turnover of patrons [70] and lower use times, efficiency will surely increase with the introduction of gender neutral bathrooms.

          1.1.3.1.3.2. Pro:
          Having two gender neutral bathrooms instead of one could be confusing to patrons, who might wonder which would be the "correct bathroom" for them to use. This can reduce time-efficiency and increase frustration.

        1.1.3.1.4. Pro:
        In buildings with gender segregated bathrooms, one can have a queue while the other is empty. By making all bathrooms gender neutral, this issue can be avoided, increasing the availability to more people.

          1.1.3.1.4.1. Con:
          Few venues are likely to restrict entrance to a specific gender, even for a limited duration of time. Therefore, the cost involved in making all bathrooms gender neutral may not be justified.

            1.1.3.1.4.1.1. Con:
            Regardless of the rarity of gender-exclusive venues and services, the inconvenience caused by long waiting times for gender segregated bathrooms in these spaces warrants bathrooms that are accessible to people of all genders.

            1.1.3.1.4.1.2. Pro:
            Gender segregation [71] in venues is increasingly being perceived as a form of gender discrimination [72]. To avoid lawsuits, legal costs, and negative publicity, fewer venues are likely to employ gender segregated practices.

          1.1.3.1.4.2. Pro:
          The lines for women's bathrooms are almost always longer [73] than the lines for men's. Gender neutral bathrooms could significantly reduce this inequity [74] by giving everyone equal access to available facilities.

            1.1.3.1.4.2.1. Con:
            The problem of women's bathrooms being generally busier could be avoided by increasing the number of stalls in women's bathrooms, rather than making all bathrooms gender neutral.

            1.1.3.1.4.2.2. Con:
            Men's bathroom queues are often shorter because using urinals is more time-efficient; forcing men to use stalls could slow them down.

              1.1.3.1.4.2.2.1. Pro:
              Urinals do not have stall doors, and many do not require flushing. This - alongside accessible clothing - can greatly reduce wait times for men who need to urinate.

              1.1.3.1.4.2.2.2. Con:
              It is unfair to penalize women with longer bathroom wait times because urinals were not designed with them in mind. Therefore, people of all genders should wait in the same queue to use a bathroom.

                1.1.3.1.4.2.2.2.1. Pro:
                The fact that urinals are commonly installed to facilitate men's easy use of public bathrooms reflects a continued deprioritization of women's needs (p. 472 [75]).

                1.1.3.1.4.2.2.2.2. Con:
                In bathrooms with urinals, a man standing behind women at the end of a long queue would not know when a urinal becomes available; this could increase wait times.

            1.1.3.1.4.2.3. Pro:
            Women generally need to urinate more often because they have smaller bladders [76]. Having more stalls available could greatly reduce wait times.

              1.1.3.1.4.2.3.1. Pro:
              There are significant sex differences [77] in urination patterns: men consume more fluid but urinate greater volumes and less frequently than women.

              1.1.3.1.4.2.3.2. Con:
              Longer wait times in women's bathrooms are partially attributable to non-biological factors [78] such as layered clothing and care giving responsibilities. Fixing these gender inequities could reduce wait times without necessitating widespread introduction of gender neutral bathrooms.

            1.1.3.1.4.2.4. Pro:
            AFAB [79] anatomy makes some people, often women, more prone to conditions that cause frequent urination, such as IBS, UTIs, and pregnancy [80]. Increasing access to bathrooms can make life easier for these groups.

            1.1.3.1.4.2.5. Pro:
            Changing, cleaning, and disposing of menstrual products is time-consuming [81]. Given the high number of people [82] of menstruating age, period care significantly increases the amount of time spent in bathrooms.

            1.1.3.1.4.2.6. Con:
            AFAB-friendly [83] urinals [84] - designed to reduce waiting times in women's bathrooms - are six times more time-efficient than traditional toilets. These negate the need to build gender neutral bathrooms to combat long queues in women's bathrooms.

          1.1.3.1.4.3. Pro:
          Certain venues may exclusively serve men and women on different days or at different times [85] (e.g., swimming pools). At such times, one bathroom would be empty, while the demand for the other bathroom would be increased; gender neutral bathrooms counteract this.

      1.1.3.2. Pro:
      Having one gender neutral bathroom instead of two gender segregated bathrooms could be more cost-effective.

        1.1.3.2.1. Con:
        Converting gender segregated bathrooms into a single large gender neutral bathroom could create unnecessary expenses, including the cost of removing urinals or building stalls.

          1.1.3.2.1.1. Con:
          Existing buildings could be given time to comply with gender neutral bathroom regulations, allowing them to be phased in with inevitable reconstructions to spread the financial burden out.

            1.1.3.2.1.1.1. Pro:
            By combining bathroom renovations with other building adaptations, businesses can reach critical economies of scale [86] that reduce their expenditure. This makes gender neutral bathrooms more affordable.

            1.1.3.2.1.1.2. Con:
            Construction work within a store may require business owners to close down until the area is safe and suitable for customers. The high cost of redesigning bathrooms may be compounded by this loss of revenue, which would increase with the duration of construction.

          1.1.3.2.1.2. Pro:
          In order to increase the accessibility of facilities, gender neutral bathrooms may require the installation of more toilet bowls. These are less water-efficient than urinals [87] so may increase costs.

            1.1.3.2.1.2.1. Pro:
            Waterless urinals [88] are becoming increasingly common. If all flushed urinals were replaced with waterless alternatives, the water saved would be equal to that used by about 2 million people annually [89].

            1.1.3.2.1.2.2. Con:
            Countries like Switzerland [90] and Germany [91] have urinals in gender neutral bathrooms.

              1.1.3.2.1.2.2.1. Pro:
              Drafts [92] for gender neutral bathrooms include a section of urinals behind one door and individual stalls for seated toilets.

              1.1.3.2.1.2.2.2. Con:
              Only AMAB [93] people can use urinals, but anyone can use a stall, regardless of gender. Therefore, installing urinals in gender neutral bathrooms effectively increases options for men, to the detriment of those who can use only toilet bowls in stalls.

          1.1.3.2.1.3. Pro:
          The cost of making a facility truly accessible to all genders can reach 50,000 USD per bathroom [94]. This is prohibitively expensive for small businesses and could be a significant financial burden for buildings with multiple bathrooms [95].

            1.1.3.2.1.3.1. Pro:
            Companies would have little incentive to make bathrooms accessible to all genders if the cost of conversion is not offset by increased patronage by those who feel strongly about gender neutral bathrooms.

            1.1.3.2.1.3.2. Con:
            Only existing buildings would require renovation into gender neutral facilities. New or renovated buildings could comply with gender neutral requirements from the beginning of construction, which would be more cost effective.

          1.1.3.2.1.4. Con:
          It takes little to no funding [96] to make gender segregated bathrooms gender neutral.

            1.1.3.2.1.4.1. Pro:
            Existing bathrooms require regular renovation [97]; thus, making them gender neutral would not pose a financial strain more burdensome than those posed by keeping bathrooms in line with current ordinance.

            1.1.3.2.1.4.2. Con:
            Grant High School was able to make bathrooms gender neutral for limited cost as they did little more than change the locks. This may not be suitable in other cases, as seen in situations where companies have had to spend millions of dollars [98] to comply with legislation.

        1.1.3.2.2. Con:
        A gender neutral bathroom serving twice as many people would have to be twice as large and have twice as many resources and facilities to stop queues from becoming unmanageable. This is costly to provide.

        1.1.3.2.3. Pro:
        Having only one bathroom saves space and money because fixed-cost [99] facilities such as sinks can be shared.

      1.1.3.3. Con:
      Due to their large size, gender neutral bathrooms are less efficient for cleaning purposes.

        1.1.3.3.1. Con:
        Regardless of their size, bathrooms can be easily and efficiently cleaned as long as they have individual stalls; this will ensure that patrons still have access to stalls other than the one being cleaned.

          1.1.3.3.1.1. Con:
          Cleaning floors and emptying bins is a near impossibility while patrons are present as they tend to be bulk jobs that cannot be done in parts.

          1.1.3.3.1.2. Pro:
          Individual stalls can continue to be used while others are cleaned. While this may temporarily slow down access, it will have less of an impact that closing the entire bathroom.

        1.1.3.3.2. Pro:
        Gender neutral bathrooms will likely be larger and take longer to clean, inconveniencing people for longer periods of time.

          1.1.3.3.2.1. Pro:
          Bathrooms are commonly closed while being cleaned. In the case of gender neutral bathrooms, all potential patrons would be disadvantaged by this procedure.

          1.1.3.3.2.2. Con:
          To avoid inconveniencing patrons, cleaning could be scheduled for times when foot traffic is low [100]. For example, in commercial establishments, bathrooms could be cleaned at night, once customers have left the premises.

        1.1.3.3.3. Con:
        Public bathrooms should ideally be cleaned once a day [101]. Smaller floorspaces and fewer facilities (e.g., washbasins) in gender neutral bathrooms would reduce the labor and cleaning resources required for this purpose.

          1.1.3.3.3.1. Pro:
          Gender segregated bathrooms may require more cleaning staff, as staff should not be permitted to access bathrooms that do not align with their gender. This would not be the case with gender neutral bathrooms where one person can clean all bathrooms.

          1.1.3.3.3.2. Con:
          The optimal frequency of bathroom cleaning is determined by footfall [102]. Gender neutral bathrooms have higher footfall, as they can be used by everyone; thus, they require cleaning on a more frequent basis, increasing costs.

          1.1.3.3.3.3. Pro:
          One large gender neutral bathrooms could have a smaller floor plan than two separate gender segregated bathrooms, as inefficiencies in fixed [103] floorspace in separated facilities are reduced.

      1.1.3.4. Con:
      If gender segregated bathrooms are merged to create one gender neutral facility, the number of users will increase. This may lead to more queueing bottlenecks (i.e., entry and exit) and, as such, be less efficient.

        1.1.3.4.1. Con:
        Bottlenecks in bathroom queues can be avoided or reduced by increasing the number of entry and exit doors or installing a large main door.

        1.1.3.4.2. Pro:
        Bathroom doors are generally designed to be smaller [104] because, unlike other doors, large furniture and equipment is unlikely to be carried through them. Therefore, finding larger doors to accommodate increased traffic may be challenging.

    1.1.4. Con:
    Gender segregated bathrooms currently meet the unique needs of biological males and females; it is pointless to alter this system.

      1.1.4.1. Pro:
      Men's bathrooms have urinals for efficiency, while women's bathrooms have sanitary bins for the disposal of menstrual products.

        1.1.4.1.1. Con:
        Gender neutral facilities can easily include typically gender segregated resources, like urinals and sanitary bins, without compromising gender neutrality.

        1.1.4.1.2. Pro:
        Almost half of girls between 14 and 21 are embarrassed about their periods [105] and would feel uncomfortable discussing them with their father or male friends. Using bathrooms in the company of unfamiliar men may worsen this feeling.

          1.1.4.1.2.1. Pro:
          Period shame and embarrassment can make it difficult for women to use period products [106] made available in public bathrooms. The possible presence of men could exacerbate this.

          1.1.4.1.2.2. Pro:
          Women and girls self-disclose [107] that discreetly using period products in public bathrooms is often difficult. This may be particularly anxiety-inducing for those with strong period shame in gender neutral bathrooms.

          1.1.4.1.2.3. Con:
          Menstruation is a reality for many people. Therefore, efforts should be made to reduce period stigma [108] and shame [109], including by making sanitary bins ubiquitous through the standardisation of gender neutral bathrooms.

            1.1.4.1.2.3.1. Con:
            Men already share home bathrooms with family members who may menstruate. Given that people use home bathrooms more than public bathrooms, period stigma is unlikely to be reduced by gender neutral public bathrooms.

              1.1.4.1.2.3.1.1. Con:
              Usage of bathrooms in a domestic setting is rarely, if ever, simultaneous between individuals, thus avoiding the need to confront normative discretion around periods - such as hiding menstrual products and waste. As such, gender neutral bathrooms are likely to have a greater impact on period stigma than domestic bathrooms.

              1.1.4.1.2.3.1.2. Con:

        1.4.2.1.2. Con:
        The solution suggested by Jewish law is to combine privacy and modesty concerns and change bathrooms into single-occupancy gender neutral facilities [383].

        1.4.2.1.3. Pro:

      1.4.2.2. Con:

        1.4.2.2.1. Pro:

        1.4.2.2.2. Con:

        1.4.2.3.6. Pro:
        A significant percentage of the US population believes that the Bible should influence legislation [393], even if it runs counter to the popular vote. This implies that public and legislative opinions are unlikely to favor standardizing gender neutral bathrooms.

      1.4.2.4. Pro:
      Many religions consider menstruating women to be "impure [394]," and everything she touches consequently becomes impure. Thus, places of worship may prefer gendered bathrooms to reduce the spread of impurity.

          1.4.2.4.4.2. Pro:

      1.4.2.6. Con:
      Religious experts believe that while religious freedom [408] should be respected, it should not be used as a grounds to discriminate against others.

        1.4.2.6.1. Pro:

          1.4.2.6.1.1. Con:
          Religious people are not a homogenous group. The fact that some religious people will not experience a deprivation of basic rights due to gender neutral bathrooms does not negate that many religious people will.

    1.4.3. Pro:
    Gender segregated bathrooms run counter to the Equal Protection Clause [410] in the 14th Amendment to the US Constitution.

      1.4.3.1. Pro:
      Title VII of the Civil Rights Act [411] and Title IX of the Education Amendments Act [412] protect individuals from gender discrimination. Forcing gender-queer individuals to use bathrooms based on their sex is discrimination, as they could be exposed to physical and psychological harm.

        1.4.3.1.1. Con:
        Given that gender neutral bathrooms may be a space for women to be exposed to sexism and gender based violence, it is necessary to balance their right to be protected from gender discrimination, with that of transgender people.

        1.4.3.1.2. Pro:
        Gender segregated bathrooms are founded on inherently discriminatory beliefs, rooted in benevolent sexism and heteronormativity, about the need to protect women (p. 369 [413]).

      1.4.3.2. Con:
      Gender segregation is different from gender discrimination. The US Constitution [414] protects citizens from the latter, not the former.

        1.4.3.2.1. Pro:
        Gender segregation is practiced in different institutions (e.g., schools [415]) and is necessary to prevent gender discrimination. For example, in sports, gender segregation ensures that female athletes are not held to male standards (p. 55 [416]).

        1.4.3.2.2. Con:
        Segregation of public spaces on the basis of any other demographic characteristics would be in violation of the US Constitution [417]. Yet, in the case of gender, it is not regarded as such.

        1.4.3.2.3. Pro:
        Society is constituted by distinct groups of people. Instead of attempting to erase these differences to reduce bias and prejudice, efforts should be directed toward embracing and celebrating this diversity.

      1.4.3.3. Pro:
      In Brown v. Board of Education [418], which pertained to the racial segregation of schools, the US Supreme Court ruled that institutions that are separate but otherwise equal are unconstitutional, as they breach the Equal Protection Clause.

        1.4.3.3.1. Con:
        Brown v. Board of Education was unsuccessful [419] in ensuring equal opportunity to school children, with Black children still underperforming relative to white children. This suggests that segregation may enhance equality in some realms.

        1.4.3.3.2. Con:
        Brown v. Board of Education was ineffective as the neighbourhoods in which schools were located remained segregated [420] by nature. The same may be true with gender neutral bathrooms - with some remaining more open and welcoming to certain gender identities.

        1.4.3.3.3. Pro:
        The racial differences [421] which underpinned segregation, and were prohibited in Brown v. Board of Education, were irrelevant to education in the same manner [422] perceived gender differences are to bathrooms.

    1.4.4. Pro:
    Gender neutral bathrooms allow society to create more inclusive spaces.

      1.4.4.1. Con:

        1.4.4.1.2. Con:

        1.4.4.1.3. Con:
        A third gender neutral bathroom would compound the problematic segregation of men and women by further othering those who do not fit within the gender binary.

        1.4.4.1.4. Pro:
        Having both gender segregated and gender neutral bathrooms would allow transgender individuals to use the bathroom of their choice while also offering a third option to gender-diverse individuals.

          1.4.4.1.4.1. Con:
          Transgender individuals may continue to face discrimination with the availability of a third gender-diverse bathroom. As some people do not recognise transgender people as being within the gender binary, they may insist they use the third option.

          1.4.4.1.4.2. Pro:
          Many transgender individuals do not undergo gender affirming surgery [424], and those who do may be at different stages [425] in the transitioning process; a broader range of bathrooms may better accommodate this diversity.

        1.4.4.1.5. Con:
        A lack of space could make it impossible to add a third bathroom.

      1.4.4.2. Con:

        1.4.4.2.1. Pro:
        Family and accessible bathrooms may be less controversial [427] than gender neutral bathrooms, as they do not relate to issues surrounding gender identity. As such, they may be easier to implement due to lesser resistance.

        1.4.4.2.2. Con:

      1.4.4.3. Pro:
      Gender neutral bathrooms could minimize the problems caused by gender markers currently used on bathroom signs.

        1.4.4.3.1. Con:
