Discussion Name:
Should American Football Be Banned?

1. Thesis:
American football should be banned.

  1.1. Con:
  Football is very popular and a critical part of many communities.

    1.1.1. Con:
    The institution of American football alienates many communities.

      1.1.1.1. Pro:
      The institution of American football promotes problematic gender roles, alienating people who do not conform to them.

        1.1.1.1.1. Pro:
        American football promotes a form of masculinity [1] that is harmful.

          1.1.1.1.1.1. Con:
          Jim Harbaugh [2] and Rush Limbaugh [3] argue that American football is the last hope for keeping American men tough.

            1.1.1.1.1.1.1. Con:
            A player formerly coached by Jim Harbauge [4] claims that Harbauge was unreasonable and often pushed players too hard.

          1.1.1.1.1.2. Pro:
          The toxic masculinity [5] promoted by American football often leads players to take unnecessary risks.

          1.1.1.1.1.3. Pro:
          The NFL has a long history of homophobia [6].

      1.1.1.2. Pro:
      The institution of American football alienates people from many cultures through cultural appropriation [7].

        1.1.1.2.1. Pro:
        Several NFL team mascots, chants, and celebrations appropriate Native American [8] culture.

          1.1.1.2.1.1. Pro:
          The Kansas City Chiefs [9] begin their games with chants, dances, and face paint appropriated from Native American culture.

        1.1.1.2.2. Pro:
        Many of the names and mascots of college football organizations appropriate [10] indigenous cultures.

        1.1.1.2.3. Pro:
        Youth football [11] organizations regularly base their names and mascots on indigenous cultures.

        1.1.1.2.4. Con:
        Cultural appropriation and alienation are not unique [12] to American football.

        1.1.1.2.5. Con:
        Cultural appropriation [13] should not be seen as problematic. Popular activities have always involved borrowing and adapting elements of other cultures.

          1.1.1.2.5.1. Con:
          Cultural appropriation [14] is problematic when a dominant culture appropriates elements of a vulnerable minority culture.

            1.1.1.2.5.1.1. Pro:
            Cultural appropriation [15] in American football typically involves the dominant American culture appropriating elements of vulnerable indigenous cultures.

      1.1.1.3. Con:
      The NCAA has an Office of Inclusion [16], which promotes diversity and inclusion in college athletics programs.

        1.1.1.3.1. Con:
        The NCAA diversity and inclusion initiatives were started relatively recently [17], which suggests that the changes are still ongoing.

      1.1.1.4. Con:
      The NFL promotes diversity and inclusion by partnering with the You Can Play [18] organization, which promotes LGBTQ+ inclusion and representation in sports.

        1.1.1.4.1. Con:
        Many of the inclusion efforts of the NFL were enacted as recently as May 2020 [19], which suggests that more change is necessary.

    1.1.2. Pro:
    College football programs contribute significantly to the identity and brand of universities in the United States.

      1.1.2.1. Pro:
      A lot of the typical American college experience [20] is built around the college football team.

        1.1.2.1.1. Con:
        Some large universities [21] do not have college football teams, yet still provide an American college experience.

        1.1.2.1.2. Pro:
        Many schools have community based events like tailgates [22] and parties before or after football games, which are essential for the enjoyment of students and their college experience.

          1.1.2.1.2.1. Con:
          Tailgating is associated [23] with excessive drinking and other dangerous activities.

        1.1.2.1.3. Pro:
        Interuniversity football rivalries [24] form the core identity of many colleges.

      1.1.2.2. Pro:
      A connection to the college football team can build strong alumni networks [25] that continue to support the college after graduation.

      1.1.2.3. Pro:
      Many colleges center their homecoming celebration [26] around a football game.

      1.1.2.4. Con:
      While it is true that football programs are core to the identity of many universities [27], this connection is not necessary and can be severed.

        1.1.2.4.1. Con:
        Severing the connection between a football program and the college experience can fail. For example, University of Alabama at Birmingham reinstated [28] football shortly after dropping it.

          1.1.2.4.1.1. Con:
          Importantly, the football program at Alabama Birmingham was dropped for political and financial reasons, and not for moral ones.

        1.1.2.4.2. Con:
        Decoupling football programs from college identity would fundamentally change the college experience, making it less fulfilling for many students.

          1.1.2.4.2.1. Con:
          Saying that decoupling football programs from college identity would fundamentally change the college experience overstates the impact on the college experience.

            1.1.2.4.2.1.1. Pro:
            College football attendance is declining [29].

        1.1.2.4.3. Pro:
        In 1901, the MIT student body voted 119-117 to discontinue [30] the intercollegiate football squad.

          1.1.2.4.3.1. Con:
          In 1978, a group of students self-organized to re-establish [31] the MIT team.

      1.1.2.5. Con:
      University mission statements that define the core identity of the university do not mention football. This is true even for universities that are famous for their football programs.

        1.1.2.5.1. Pro:
        The University of Alabama [32]'s mission statement puts emphasis "...on quality programs in the areas of teaching, research and service" without mentioning football.

        1.1.2.5.2. Pro:

        1.1.2.5.3. Pro:
        The Ohio State University's core values [34] do not include football or competitive sports.

        1.1.2.5.4. Con:
        Mission statements at universities often present and articulate a world view that is narrow, shallow, irrelevant and devoid of meaning [35].

      1.1.2.6. Pro:
      College football is the main source of entertainment [36] in many small towns.

    1.1.3. Pro:
    Professional football organizations contribute to the identity of the cities and states that they affiliated with.

      1.1.3.1. Pro:
      Professional football organizations shape [37], and are shaped by [38], the culture of the city they are based in.

      1.1.3.2. Con:
      Celebrations after the Philadelphia Eagles' 2018 Super Bowl [39] victory disintegrated into rioting and looting, ultimately costing the city thousands of dollars in damages.

    1.1.4. Con:
    Communities are capable of changing, and can support other sports or cultural activities.

      1.1.4.1. Pro:
      There are long traditions supporting baseball [40] and basketball [41] in many communities, and this could be shared with new communities.

    1.1.5. Pro:
    High school football programs are important for small towns and communities.

      1.1.5.1. Pro:
      Student athletes playing for a high school team represent the communities [42] they live in. This inspires a sense of honor and responsibility to perform well and come together as a team.

        1.1.5.1.1. Pro:
        Camaraderie and togetherness are the most prevalent in competitions against rival schools. Rivalries [43] are often built upon long traditions that give players a sense of being a part of something greater than themselves.

      1.1.5.2. Con:
      High school football programs are associated with harmful things like hazing and aggression.

        1.1.5.2.1. Pro:
        A national hazing [44] scandal originated from New Jersey in 2014, "where six players accused of sexually brutalizing former teammates were sentenced to 50 hours of community service."

        1.1.5.2.2. Pro:
        In 2015, a group of parents of players in Southern California forcibly entered a high school locker room in order to attack students in retaliation [45] for an incident in a football game.

        1.1.5.2.3. Con:
        High school football is more beneficial than harmful.

          1.1.5.2.3.1. Pro:
          High school football improves the educational opportunities of student athletes.

            1.1.5.2.3.1.1. Con:
            High school varsity and junior varsity student athletes tend to get lower English and history grades [46] during active sports seasons.

            1.1.5.2.3.1.2. Pro:
            Some student athletes who might otherwise have dropped out [47] of high school stay in school instead and graduate because of football.

            1.1.5.2.3.1.3. Pro:
            High school football enables athletes to compete for scholarships [48] that could give them a great college education.

          1.1.5.2.3.2. Con:
          High school football programs are uniquely ill-equipped [49] to manage the health risks associated with playing the game.

            1.1.5.2.3.2.1. Pro:
            High school athletic programs often lack certified athletic trainers capable of advising students when to take breaks or continue playing.

              1.1.5.2.3.2.1.1. Pro:
              There was no certified athletic trainer present at Coahoma County High School to prevent Dennis Mitchell [50] from re-entering the game after taking a hard hit and vomiting. He later collapsed on the field and died as a result.

            1.1.5.2.3.2.2. Con:
            Rather than banning high school football programs, they should be reformed and given additional funding to deal with any health problems that arise.

          1.1.5.2.3.3. Con:
          It is difficult [51] to weigh the harm of football for a young athlete against the benefits, given that the potential for long-term harm varies.

          1.1.5.2.3.4. Pro:
          High school football gives young kids lessons [52] in teamwork, hard work, and sportsmanship that they otherwise would not learn in classrooms.

            1.1.5.2.3.4.1. Con:
            Evidence suggests that character building [53] and good judgment follow from participation in music, theater, and community service programs, but not from participation in sports.

            1.1.5.2.3.4.2. Pro:
            High school football teams help discover and hone athletic talents in students who would otherwise go untrained.

          1.1.5.2.3.5. Pro:
          High school football is good for students who are not directly involved with their school's team.

            1.1.5.2.3.5.1. Pro:
            Students go to games together, cheer their team on together and follow their teams to away games. This strengthens relationships and builds a unique sense of camaraderie.

            1.1.5.2.3.5.2. Con:
            Passion for high school football forms a culture that is inherently exclusionary to the students who do not want to follow the sport.

              1.1.5.2.3.5.2.1. Con:
              The exclusionary culture of football is not specific to football. It can surround any competitive high school sport or extra curricular activity.

  1.2. Pro:
  American football is too dangerous to permit league football.

    1.2.1. Pro:
    Traumatic brain and spinal injuries are more common in football than in any other sport.

      1.2.1.1. Con:
      A 1994 study found that spinal cord injuries are most common [54] in swimming and gymnastics, rather than American football.

        1.2.1.1.1. Pro:
        Nearly [55] 51% of sports-related spinal cord injuries that were recorded occurred while swimming.

        1.2.1.1.2. Con:
        The 1994 [56] study is based on a very small and non-representative sample population (N = 31), and thus the results should not be generalized.

      1.2.1.2. Pro:
      Football players are at risk of catastrophic [57] spinal cord injuries, which are significant life-changing events and can sometimes result in death.

        1.2.1.2.1. Pro:
        Darryl Stingley [58]'s career as a receiver for the New England Patriots was cut short because of a head-on collision with Oakland Raider safety Jack Tatum, rendering him paralyzed from the neck down.

        1.2.1.2.2. Con:
        Catastrophic injuries are increasingly rare [59] in American football, with deaths caused directly by football significantly declining over the decades.

      1.2.1.3. Pro:
      Football is associated with chronic traumatic encephalopathy [60] (CTE), which is a neurodegenerative disease found in people with multiple head injuries that typically entails behavioral, emotional, and cognitive difficulties.

        1.2.1.3.1. Con:
        The long term effects of multiple head injuries, including whether it leads to CTE, have still not been explored [61] sufficiently to establish that there is a causal link.

        1.2.1.3.2. Pro:
        One large study [62] found that 110 out of 111 (99%) deceased NFL players had chronic traumatic encephalopathy.

          1.2.1.3.2.1. Con:
          The sample that showed widespread chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) suffered from selection bias [63]. The players who donated their brains to the study were the ones experiencing cognitive issues.

        1.2.1.3.3. Pro:
        The rate of depression [64] for former football players who suffered from concussions is much higher than for other former athletes.

      1.2.1.4. Pro:
      In the United States, severe head injuries in sports are most commonly associated [65] with American football, and a reported 90% of cases are acute subdural hematomas (ASDH).

        1.2.1.4.1. Con:
        In 2009, the number of estimated head injuries for cycling was nearly double [66] the head injuries associated with football.

        1.2.1.4.2. Pro:
        The number of cases of ASDH is increasing [67] annually.

        1.2.1.4.3. Pro:
        In the United States, almost all patients with ASDH due to sport-related head injuries are senior high school students or younger [68].

      1.2.1.5. Con:
      Severe head injuries are prevalent in many other [69] contact sports such as rugby, boxing, judo, ice hockey, skiing, and snowboarding.

    1.2.2. Pro:
    American football is a contact sport that results in acute injuries that harm players over their lifetime.

      1.2.2.1. Pro:
      Minor injuries and concussions are common in American Football.

        1.2.2.1.1. Pro:
        Concussions are common [70] in professional American Football.

        1.2.2.1.2. Con:
        The 2012 [71] and 2016 [72] International Conferences on Concussion in Sports found that the data connecting repeated concussions and long-term health issues is inconclusive.

          1.2.2.1.2.1. Con:
          Even if the literature on long-term consequences is inconclusive, this does not mean that there is no evidence of long-term consequences.

      1.2.2.2. Pro:
      The grueling nature of the sport includes constant high intensity practice which can become dangerous [73] for football players.

        1.2.2.2.1. Con:
        Football players are capable of great feats of strength [74] and athleticism as a result of the intensity of football practice and competition.

        1.2.2.2.2. Con:
        Football leagues could reduce [75] the number of heat-related issues by limiting the number of games and practices during hot and humid times of the year.

        1.2.2.2.3. Con:
        Football programs could reduce the exposure of players to dangerous temperatures by investing in enclosed [76] stadiums and indoor practice facilities.

        1.2.2.2.4. Pro:
        Football players often have to use dangerous amounts of painkillers [77] simply to survive the season.

        1.2.2.2.5. Pro:
        On average, former NFL players (even relatively young former players) have disproportionately [78] high levels of whole-person impairment (WPI) due to muscular-skeletal damage.

          1.2.2.2.5.1. Pro:
          Professional players lose, on average, three years [79] of life expectancy for every year they play professional football.

        1.2.2.2.6. Pro:
        Playing football prior to age 12 is associated [80] with long-term health consequences.

      1.2.2.3. Con:
      Studies that compared recreational football players to those with alternative pastimes during their childhood found that there was no difference [81] in numbers of adults from each group who went on to develop neurodegenerative illnesses.

        1.2.2.3.1. Con:
        The rate of dementia at 50 years of age is 5 times [82] higher among men who played American football than men who did not.

    1.2.3. Con:
    People should be able to voluntarily participate in dangerous activities so long as everyone's participation is voluntary.

      1.2.3.1. Pro:
      The ability to engage in dangerous acts is fundamental to our freedom [83] and autonomy.

        1.2.3.1.1. Con:
        People should only be free to take on reasonable risks, but the risk involved in football is unreasonable.

          1.2.3.1.1.1. Pro:
          The NFL and other American football associations have not been honest about the risk of playing. People cannot meaningfully agree to risks they do not know.

            1.2.3.1.1.1.1. Con:

            1.2.3.1.1.1.2. Pro:
            The NFL tried to cover up [84] the long-term brain damage caused by American football multiple times.

              1.2.3.1.1.1.2.1. Con:

                1.2.3.1.1.1.2.1.1. Con:
                While Dr. Pellman was identified as a controversial figure [86] in 1994, he continued to work in the NFL for 30 years and retired in 2016.

              1.2.3.1.1.1.2.2. Pro:
              The documentary 'League of Denial' details [87] two decades of the NFL's efforts to suppress research on health impacts of football, and discredit research [88] that made it into the public sphere.

                1.2.3.1.1.1.2.2.1. Con:
                The documentary has had little impact [89] on the popularity of professional football and TV ratings [90] of the NFL. This suggests that the findings are not compelling.

                  1.2.3.1.1.1.2.2.1.1. Con:

                1.2.3.1.1.1.2.2.3. Pro:
                The campaign [93] to suppress the health impacts of football slowed down, and halted, much research being completed, and made available to the public. In the decades of this campaign many more athletes incurred damaging, life-threatening, and even fatal, injuries.

              It is possible that long-term brain damage information was simply mismanaged by the NFL due to incompetent leadership.

                Paul Tagliabue [94], the commissioner of the NFL from 1989 to 2006, used his position to publicly downplay the risks of brain injury in the NFL.

                1.2.3.1.1.1.2.3.2. Con:
                The incompetence of other organizations does not absolve the NFL of culpability for its actions.

                1.2.3.1.1.1.2.3.3. Pro:
                For example, the NFLPA [95] (NFL Players Association) failed to represent the interests of the players by working to minimize long-term brain damage.

              1.2.3.1.1.1.2.4. Pro:
              A congressional report issued in 2016 indicated that the NFL used their funding of concussion research to try to influence [96] the results of that research.

            1.2.3.1.1.1.3. Con:
            While people should know risks before they consent, one can set the bar too high. There will always be unknowns and it is fairly obvious that football involves the potential for very serious physical injuries.

            1.2.3.1.1.1.4. Pro:
            Former college athletes report [97] that they didn't feel able to discuss the extent of their injuries and didn't feel that their coaches were sufficiently honest with them.

            1.2.3.1.1.1.5. Pro:
            A 2018 lawsuit against [98] the NCAA alleged that it knew the risks associated with repeated head trauma and did not adequately disclose these risks.

          1.2.3.1.1.2. Con:
          What counts as a reasonable or unreasonable risk should be determined by the person taking the risk. Thus, the distinction between reasonable and unreasonable risk is irrelevant in deciding when to limit freedom.

        1.2.3.1.2. Pro:
        Americans identify individual freedom [99] as an essential virtue for living as an American.

        1.2.3.1.3. Con:
        People should not have the ability to exercise their autonomy to undertake actions that could be detrimental to their self interest.

          1.2.3.1.3.1. Pro:
          People are not allowed to not wear seat-belts while driving because it is against their own interest to do so.

          1.2.3.1.3.2. Con:
          Paternalism [100] (the view that society can force or prohibit certain actions for the good of the individual) is generally held to be of limited value in democratic societies, only to be allowed when all the relevant information cannot be communicated to the individual.

      1.2.3.2. Pro:
      Adults have the authority and capacity to consensually engage in many risky activities. Football is no different.

        1.2.3.2.1. Con:
        -> See 1.2.3.1.1.1.

        1.2.3.2.2. Pro:
        Adults are allowed to participate in a host of dangerous activities for pleasure, such as mixed martial arts (MMA), wrestling, boxing, sword fighting [101], skydiving, cabling and rappelling, caving, cliff jumping and base jumping.

          1.2.3.2.2.1. Pro:
          There are reports of paralysis [102] and other severe injuries [103] arising from the play of rugby, but there's no call to ban rugby as a sport.

          1.2.3.2.2.2. Pro:
          While American football is a sport that carries risks to participants, other sports [104] that are widely enjoyed are more dangerous than American football.

            1.2.3.2.2.2.1. Con:
            Even if other sports are more dangerous than American football, it doesn't follow that American football is safe and should not be banned. It may be the case that all sports as dangerous as American football should be banned.

            1.2.3.2.2.2.2. Pro:
            Skiing, equestrian [105] sports, and cycling have been deemed [106] more dangerous than American football.

          1.2.3.2.2.3. Con:
          The extent to which American football permeates the day to day life of the average American makes it unique in terms of the scope and degree of harm it can cause.

            1.2.3.2.2.3.1. Con:
            An activity's degree of popularity has no effect on its level of safety. If such hazardous activities are legal for the general public, football should be as well.

      1.2.3.3. Con:
      Children are too young to meaningfully understand and consent to the potential long-term damage of football.

        1.2.3.3.1. Con:

          1.2.3.3.1.1. Con:
          Many low-income families are coerced to have their children play football because they view it as a means of creating economic opportunities [107] for their children.

        1.2.3.3.2. Pro:
        Paternalism [108] is justifiable when dealing with non-adults. Thus, it is acceptable for society to restrict even voluntary actions such as choosing to participate in youth football.

      1.2.3.4. Con:
      Risky behavior is more likely [109] when peer pressure is involved, particularly among teens and young adults. This makes it hard to say that participation is truly voluntary in an incredibly popular activity like playing football.

      1.2.3.5. Con:

            1.2.3.5.1.3.1. Pro:
            It is a common practice [114] for college teams to drop injured players from the team and then refuse to cover their healthcare.

        1.2.3.5.2. Pro:
        A person's liberty to engage in risky behaviors is limited by the risk it puts on other people.

          1.2.3.5.2.1. Pro:

          1.2.3.5.2.2. Pro:

          1.2.3.5.3.1. Pro:

  1.3. Con:
  Reforming the way football is played would be preferable to a ban.

    1.3.1. Con:
    While reforming American football might be an alternative to banning it outright, actually doing so is prohibitively difficult.

      1.3.1.1. Pro:
      The organizations that govern American football have shown themselves to be resistant to change in the past. This suggests that reform in the future will also be incredibly difficult.

        1.3.1.1.1. Pro:
        Close to a century of attempts to reform college football have failed [124].

        1.3.1.1.2. Con:
        The NFL [125] has continued to change rules and implement better sideline procedures nearly every year since 1985 to make the game safer.

          1.3.1.1.2.1. Con:
          While most Americans support making football safer, NFL fans largely reject [126] the proposed changes to the game. This likely limits the NFL's willingness to make meaningful changes.

          1.3.1.1.2.2. Pro:
          Football has become safer because of the innovations in protective gear in the 1960's and 1990's.

            1.3.1.1.2.2.1. Pro:
            Shoulder pads [127] began to be manufactured from plastic materials in the 1960s and 1970s, which improved their effectiveness.

              1.3.1.1.2.2.1.1. Pro:
              The use of plastic in the 1960's led to the development [128] of the sleek and efficient shoulder pads in the 1990's.

            1.3.1.1.2.2.2. Con:
            While protective gear has improved in the last fifty years, players have also gotten [129] bigger and faster. This greatly increases the danger from head-on collisions.

          1.3.1.1.2.3. Pro:
          The NFL has asserted [130] that rule changes have yielded measurable results in safety.

            1.3.1.1.2.3.1. Pro:
            Removal of the three-point stance [131], in which a lineman crouches with his hand on the ground, allowing him to launch himself at the opposing lineman, should greatly decrease the impacts over an athlete’s career.

              1.3.1.1.2.3.1.1. Pro:
              Linemen absorb many hundreds of repetitive subconcussive [132] impacts each season, which is largely why many of the former NFL players diagnosed with CTE have been offensive and defensive linemen.

            1.3.1.1.2.3.2. Pro:
            In 2018, the NFL prohibited the lowering helmets to initiate contact with their opponents (The Helmet Rule), and initial data [133] suggests that there was a significant reduction (20%) in concussions caused by an opponent lowering their helmets.

              1.3.1.1.2.3.2.1. Pro:
              The Helmet Rule directly outlaws helmet-to-helmet [134] contact, which accounts for 40 - 50% of all concussions in the NFL.

              1.3.1.1.2.3.2.2. Con:

          The perverse incentive that coaches have to cover up crimes is further exacerbated [250] by their lack of job security.

      1.5.6.3. Pro:
      -> See 1.5.1.7.

      1.5.6.4. Con:
      Football is not the only sport or high prestige activity that people are willing to cover up horrible crimes for. Thus, there is no reason to think that banning American football would change this.

      1.5.6.5. Pro:
      There have been multiple examples [251] of high schools and universities covering up sexual assaults for star players.

        1.5.6.5.1. Pro:
        Many universities including Florida State University [252], Baylor University [253], New Mexico University [254], and University of Notre Dame [255] have been accused of covering up sexual assaults committed by their athletes.

        1.5.6.5.2. Con:
        Schools and universities have a long history of underreporting and downplaying [256] sexual assault allegations in general. This problem is not unique to athletics.

          1.5.6.5.2.1. Pro:
          School leadership [257] often fails to establish a campus culture in advance that encourages students to properly report sexual assault and inappropriate behavior.

          1.5.6.5.2.2. Con:
          -> See 1.5.6.2.1.

        1.5.6.5.3. Pro:
        Penn State covered up decades of child sex abuse [258] to protect their football team.

          1.5.6.5.3.1. Con:
          When the abuse at Penn State finally was uncovered, the NCAA levied the most severe penalties [259] in the history of the league against them.

        1.5.6.5.4. Con:
        The cover-up of sexual assault, while horrific, is not integral to the game. Better rules and transparency can address this problem without banning football.

          1.5.6.5.4.1. Pro:
          Sexual assault [260] could be added to the list of offenses that make college athletes ineligible to play in the NCAA.

            1.5.6.5.4.1.1. Con:
            The systems in place to transfer and protect star college athletes is deeply ingrained [261] in American football culture.

            The NCAA has recently added rules [262] requiring that programs vet athletes for sexual assault. This could pave the way for an outright ban.

              1.5.6.5.4.1.2.1. Con:
              Sexual violence advocates criticize [263] these rules for being insufficient because they stop short of banning students involved in sexual assault from playing at all.

  1.6. Con:
  American football is too economically important [264] to ban.

    1.6.1. Pro:
    College football programs generate revenue [265] for universities.

      1.6.1.1. Pro:
      College athletic apparel [266] sales help fund college athletics programs and support the university's brand.

      1.6.1.2. Pro:
      Some college football programs generate millions of dollars in TV contracts [267] and broadcasting rights.

      1.6.1.3. Con:
      Most [268] university athletics programs at large schools lose money.

        1.6.1.3.1. Con:
        College football accounts for a significant amount [269] of money that the school would not have received otherwise.

        1.6.1.3.2. Pro:
        All but one [270] public Division I college in Ohio loses money on its football program leaving students to subsidize the cost at between $700 and $1500 per student per year.

    1.6.2. Pro:
    With over 25 billion dollars in revenue [271], the NFL is the largest and economically most important sport in the US.

      1.6.2.1. Con:
      The NFL's revenue depends on billions of dollars [272] of subsidies and tax breaks states and cities give to the league, NFL teams, and their sponsors.

        1.6.2.1.1. Pro:
        -> See 1.4.3.3.3.3.2.1.

      1.6.2.2. Pro:
      In the long term, people who attend games or work for the team generate new spending in the community, expanding local employment.

        1.6.2.2.1. Pro:
        The average number of people employed [273] directly or indirectly by an NFL team is probably around 3,700. Assuming an economic multiplier [274] of 3.78 for jobs in the arts, entertainment and recreation sector, it is reasonable to assume that in the long term, the average NFL franchise supports more than ten thousand jobs.

        1.6.2.2.2. Pro:
        Case studies [275] that examine the economic impact of several recently built stadiums have found increases in both commercial activity and real estate value.

          1.6.2.2.2.1. Con:
          A study [276] found that a new sports facility can have an extremely small or even negative effect on overall economic activity and employment.

        1.6.2.2.3. Con:
        Data [277] shows that jobs and sales tax revenue actually decrease around NFL stadiums.

          1.6.2.2.3.1. Con:

          -> See 1.6.2.2.2.

          -> See 1.6.2.3.1.

    1.6.3. Pro:

    1.6.5. Con:

      1.6.5.1. Pro:
      According to reports in 2020 [290], the sports economy is shifting away from traditional sporting events and toward women's sports, e-sports, and online streaming events.

        1.6.5.1.1. Pro:
        E-sports [291] are a viable alternative to in-person sports like American football because they are not physically dangerous to play and they can be viewed online, which is compatible with social distancing precautions.

        1.6.5.1.2. Con:
        The differences [292] between e-sports and in-person sports make them difficult to compare, and thus differences in viewership difficult to evaluate.

    1.6.6. Con:
    It is reasonable to assume that a good deal of football spending would move to other areas of the sports economy, and not simply disappear.

      1.6.6.1. Pro:
      The size of the sports entertainment market is demand driven. If spending on football were to decrease, there would be a higher demand for substitute goods [293].

      1.6.6.2. Con:
      The elimination [294] of college football in 2020 could exceed $4 billion and lead to the elimination of non-revenue sports programs.

        1.6.6.2.1. Con:
        Colleges could save a substantial [295] amount of money by firing or drastically reducing the salary of their football coaching staff.

          1.6.6.2.1.1. Pro:
          Colleges and universities face deficits of millions of dollars because of complications related to Covid-19 [296]. This is causing many athletics programs to rethink their financial priorities.

    1.6.7. Con:
    Historically, the size of the sports economy has been eclipsed [297] by the size of the (until recently largely illegal) sports betting economy. Sports betting is very likely to have an overall negative impact [298] on the economy.

      1.6.7.1. Con:

      1.6.7.2. Pro:
      Betting on sports can contribute to gambling addiction [300], which has negative health impacts [301].

    1.6.8. Con:

      1.6.8.1. Pro:

      1.6.8.2. Pro:
      American students spend far more time [305] doing sports than students in other countries. This time usage comes at the expense of time for more academically rigorous activities.

      1.6.8.3. Con:
      Comparing spending on football to spending on mathematics is misleading because educational spending is not a zero sum game. That is, it is possible to spend on both football and mathematics, or on neither.
