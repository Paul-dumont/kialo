Discussion Name:
Is the Scientific Process Objective?

1. Thesis:
The scientific process is objective.

  1.1. Pro:
  The scientific method promotes objectivity in scientific research.

    1.1.1. Pro:
    The scientific method attempts to remove all traces of subjectivity.

      1.1.1.1. Con:
      Developing hypotheses is an essential element of science, and hypotheses are inherently subjective (p. 95 [1]).

        1.1.1.1.1. Con:
        The requirement of testability [2] in scientific hypotheses mitigates the subjective element of experiments.

          1.1.1.1.1.1. Pro:
          The fact that others can empirically test the validity of a hypothesis allows for the objective analysis of even subjective propositions.

        1.1.1.1.2. Con:
        The scientific method establishes the extent to which a subjective hypothesis conforms with objective facts.

        1.1.1.1.3. Pro:
        Hypotheses are essentially well-informed guesses based on a researcher's subjective understanding of a phenomenon [3] (p. 1 [4]).

          1.1.1.1.3.1. Con:
          While a hypothesis is an educated guess and science is based on hypotheses, one of the conditions of the hypothesis is that it has to be able to be tested conclusively to be able to determine whether that guess was right or wrong. The best explanation aren't always simple, and can (in some cases) require heavy detail to describe.

          1.1.1.1.3.2. Pro:
          Hypotheses can never be proven to be objectively true [5], as there could always be new evidence or facts that disprove them.

      1.1.1.2. Pro:
      Because the results of scientific inquiry can be applied independent of subjective matters, science is objectively useful (p. 2 [6]).

        1.1.1.2.1. Pro:
        Regardless of the intentions, motivations, and beliefs of those making scientific discoveries, humanity at large, including those with no understanding of the principles at work, has greatly benefited from the fruits of their scientific inquiry [7].

        1.1.1.2.2. Con:
        Just because science is "objectively useful" does not imply that science is truly "objective."

    1.1.2. Pro:
    The scientific method can correct for bias.

      1.1.2.1. Pro:
      Science encourages the use of blinded testing [8] and control groups [9] to reduce the influence of bias.

        1.1.2.1.1. Pro:
        Double-blind testing helps prevent observer and confirmation bias [10], and mitigates the placebo effect.

        1.1.2.1.2. Con:
        Blinded experiments [11] are only possible in certain situations and can pose ethical problems.

      1.1.2.2. Pro:
      When a hypothesis is tested, the design [12] of an experiment, the collection [13] and statistical analysis [14] of its results, and the review of other scientists are all mechanisms by which bias is identified and resolved. Thus, if the scientific method is applied properly, all bias will eventually be eliminated.

        1.1.2.2.1. Pro:
        The use of statistical analysis and tools is integral to any scientific experiment. Their use reflects the importance placed on objectivity in science.

        1.1.2.2.2. Con:
        Many scientific studies are statistically or methodologically weak (p. 4451 [15]).

      1.1.2.3. Con:
      There is no single "scientific method," but rather a collection of related practices [16], which are often applied differently in distinct fields.

        1.1.2.3.1. Pro:
        The scientific method is composed of a variety of research and rational methods [17].

        1.1.2.3.2. Con:
        Objectivity is an essential element [18] of any scientific methodology.

    1.1.3. Con:
    Continued application of the scientific method has consistently disproven innumerable previous scientific paradigms, so there is no reason to believe that scientific inquiry leads to objective truth.

      1.1.3.1. Con:
      Scientific paradigms have often been found to be incomplete or part of a bigger picture, but they have rarely been found to be completely wrong.

        1.1.3.1.1. Pro:
        As an example, Einstein's theory of gravity did not entirely invalidate Newton's description; it elucidated the range of validity for Newton's theory.

        1.1.3.1.2. Con:
        A great number of respected scientific theories have been shown to be completely wrong [19].

        1.1.3.1.3. Pro:
        Science has [20] changed [21] since [22] when this was more often the case. In contemporary science, paradigms are quite likely to either be not completely wrong or to be relatively known to have potential problems along with potential alternative theories.

      1.1.3.2. Pro:
      The fact that science is always evolving implies that current theories are likely biased or incorrect in ways we do not yet know and will eventually be disproven.

        1.1.3.2.1. Con:
        Even if this were true, it does not argue against the objectivity of science; a theory that is updated to include a wider range of results is inherently more objective.

        1.1.3.2.2. Pro:
        If science were truly objective, then its results and theories would not be subject to further revision.

      1.1.3.3. Con:
      Science tends to approach objective truth over time [23] as rudimentary models become more developed and models which do not agree with observations are thrown out.

        1.1.3.3.1. Pro:
        Theories evolve as research clarifies misconceptions.

        1.1.3.3.2. Con:
        It is entirely possible for different, mutually exclusive theories [24] to conform with observations.

    1.1.4. Con:
    Science is based on assumptions [25] that cannot be objectively confirmed.

      1.1.4.1. Con:
      The basic assumptions of science can and have been tested [26] and are no longer considered controversial issues [27].

        1.1.4.1.1. Con:
        The scientific commitment to methodological naturalism [28] is an unsupported subjective position that prevents it from investigating supernatural phenomena.

        1.1.4.1.2. Pro:
        There is strong evidence to suggest that the physical laws uncovered by science operate the same way throughout the universe [29].

      1.1.4.2. Pro:
      Science assumes that all of nature has an order or pattern that can be observed and attempts to prove the existence of this order (p. 13 [30]).

        1.1.4.2.1. Pro:
        Science assumes that the laws of nature can be consistently applied through space and time. This assumption has recently come under fire. [31]

        1.1.4.2.2. Con:
        A great deal of human experience, observation, and experimentation support belief in the order of nature [32], while there is little to no evidence to the contrary.

      1.1.4.3. Pro:
      Scientists assume that the physical universe operates independent of our observations [33], although this cannot be proven.

        1.1.4.3.1. Pro:
        In certain experiments and conditions, different observers will observe different empirical evidence [34], inherently resulting in disparate conclusions.

        1.1.4.3.2. Con:
        Many observed processes and phenomena have been demonstrated to produce overwhelmingly similar empirical evidence for different observers and at different times (p. 140 [35]).

          1.1.4.3.2.1. Pro:
          The ability of different observers to reach similar conclusions [36] about an object or phenomenon implies that both are interacting with an objective reality.

      1.1.4.4. Pro:
      Science assumes that “the best explanations are always simple or posit the fewest necessary conditions” as embodied in the principle known as Occam’s razor [37].

        1.1.4.4.1. Con:
        Occam's razor is used in science as a general rule of thumb for removing unnecessary assumptions [38] rather than as an irrefutable law. The best scientific explanations are those that conform best to the evidence rather than the simplest ones.

        1.1.4.4.2. Pro:
        While simple explanations may be more useful, they are no more likely to be true [39] than complex ones.

      1.1.4.5. Con:
      Science does not operate by proving things to be true, it proceeds by developing reasonable theories that conform with evidence, seeking to disprove them [40], and revising them according to results.

        1.1.4.5.1. Pro:
        Positive evidence is often subject to confounding causes and variables (p. 227 [41]); scientific progress is often driven more by negative evidence [42] that challenges or disproves a theory (p. 350 [43]).

        1.1.4.5.2. Pro:
        Science is not a closed system, and therefore cannot support the construction of proofs [44].

        1.1.4.5.3. Con:
        Results that falsify a scientific theory do not inherently imply that it is incorrect, but rather that demonstrate a lack of agreement between multiple scientific theories [45].

  1.2. Con:
  Because science arises from humans, who inherently hold subjective perspectives, it cannot be entirely objective.

    1.2.1. Pro:
    The scope of scientific observation is limited to the current extent of human experience [46], therefore even empirical data is subjective.

      1.2.1.1. Pro:
      The generation of hypotheses and the subjects and directions of scientific inquiry are limited by the extent of human awareness.

        1.2.1.1.1. Pro:
        It is possible that humans remain entirely unaware of different processes and forms of energy to which our senses and experiences are not attuned.

        1.2.1.1.2. Con:
        Scientific inquiry has led to expanded human awareness, including many phenomena that cannot be directly perceived by human senses. For example, scientists have posited the existence of dark energy [47] to explain certain phenomena despite the lack of observable evidence for its existence.

      1.2.1.2. Pro:
      Humans are inherently limited by biology and cognitive capacity [48] in their ability to observe, acquire, and retain knowledge.

        1.2.1.2.1. Pro:
        Human intelligence is limited by time, computational ability, and communication (p. 2 [49]).

        1.2.1.2.2. Con:
        The breadth and depth of human knowledge has continued to increase, and we don't really know [50] the extent to which it can continue to increase.

      1.2.1.3. Con:
      Scientific exploration has manifestly expanded the range of human awareness and experience [51].

        1.2.1.3.1. Pro:
        Scientific research extends human knowledge in an orderly fashion that subjects new results to objective scrutiny [52].

        1.2.1.3.2. Con:
        Regardless of the extent to which human awareness and experience expands, it will always have an outer limit [53], which represents the subjective perspective of humanity itself.

    1.2.2. Pro:
    When processing data, human perceptions and bias are infused into science (p. 3 [54]).

      1.2.2.1. Con:
      Science can often identify and estimate human biases and, in turn, correct for them. Many statistical tools have been developed for this very purpose and are widely used in scientific data analysis.

        1.2.2.1.1. Pro:
        There are a number of methods available for reducing statistical bias, including random or stratified sampling [55] and weighted logistical regression [56] (WLR).

        1.2.2.1.2. Con:
        Scientific data analysis itself can introduce new forms of bias (p. 14 [57]).

          1.2.2.1.2.1. Pro:
          The increase in research flexibility and the complexity of study designs combined with sample size issues [58] and search for increasingly subtle effects may lead to an increase in the likelihood that statistically significant findings are spurious [59].

      1.2.2.2. Pro:
      Facts must be sensed and interpreted to be documented by science. Human senses and perception can be flawed, resulting in misunderstandings of reality.

        1.2.2.2.1. Pro:
        Human senses are fallible [60] and are not calibrated in a way that supports objective measurement (p. 149 [61]).

        1.2.2.2.2. Con:
        Science often relies on instruments [62] to improve the accuracy, scope, and reliability of human senses.

        1.2.2.2.3. Pro:
        The raw data collected by the senses, such as light waves, chemicals, and sound waves, require processing by the brain in order to become a describable experience of an object, and even more processing to be conceptually articulated into a scientific theory.

    1.2.3. Pro:
    Science should not be entirely objective, as the purpose of science is to further humanity's interests.

      1.2.3.1. Pro:
      The questions posed by science are limited to those in which humans have a subjective interest [63].

        1.2.3.1.1. Con:
        Even if humans are motivated by self-interest to study some phenomena over others, the empirical evidence gathered is unaffected by such concerns.

        1.2.3.1.2. Pro:
        Science has historically overlooked a great deal of knowledge that was deemed less essential to human interests.

      1.2.3.2. Pro:
      The imposition of human value judgments [64], such as determining which lines of inquiry will be most beneficial, inherently requires a subjective perspective.

        1.2.3.2.1. Con:
        Scientific knowledge is concerned with determining what is rather than making value judgments [65].

        1.2.3.2.2. Pro:
        Scientific inquiry cannot feasibly avoid value judgments [66] entirely.

      1.2.3.3. Con:
      Human curiosity and, by extension, scientific inquiry extend far beyond the immediate concerns of humanity to include basic questions about reality, existence, and the nature of the universe.

        1.2.3.3.1. Con:
        Even seemingly useless scientific discoveries [67] often have a profound impact on human society and technology.

        1.2.3.3.2. Pro:
        The great amount of human effort and resources that have been devoted to the scientific exploration of space have offered humanity little in the way of practical benefits [68].

      1.2.3.4. Con:
      Scientific knowledge does not inherently benefit humanity.

        1.2.3.4.1. Pro:
        Scientific knowledge can equally be used to the benefit or detriment of humanity; for example, the scientific understanding of nuclear fission [69] led to the creation of both nuclear power and atomic bombs.

        1.2.3.4.2. Con:
        The iterative process of science inherently builds up knowledge [70] that can be used to solve human problems.

    1.2.4. Con:
    Although humans inherently hold a perspective, science is concerned with properties that remain constant [71] regardless of perspective.

      1.2.4.1. Pro:
      The evidence gathered via empirical observation exists independently of the knowledge, experience, or existence of the observer.

        1.2.4.1.1. Pro:
        -> See 1.1.4.3.2.1.

        1.2.4.1.2. Con:
        It is inherently impossible to definitively prove this to be the case, as the only way to verify empirical evidence is via observation.

      1.2.4.2. Pro:
      Scientific observations made by independent observers remain statistically consistent [72].

        1.2.4.2.1. Con:
        A great many scientific studies have been demonstrated to suffer from significant inconsistencies in data analysis (p. 1 [73]).

        1.2.4.2.2. Pro:
        Scientists revise their models and methods [74] to identify and mitigate sources of statistical inconsistency.

      1.2.4.3. Con:
      Some properties and phenomena only exist under, or are fundamentally altered by, observation [75].

        1.2.4.3.1. Pro:
        Electrons behave as waves when unobserved but behave as particles when under observation [76].

          1.2.4.3.1.1. Con:
          The observer effect on electrons results from the use of electronic measurement devices and does not rely on conscious, subjective observation [77].

        1.2.4.3.2. Con:
        The observer effect found in quantum systems relies upon agents who are situated differently in reference to the observed phenomena. If such agents were similarly situated, their observations would be found to align (pp. 13–14 [78]).

    1.2.5. Con:
    Scientists are trained to make unbiased observations.

      1.2.5.1. Con:
      Numerous individual factors can result in subconscious subjectivity. A person cannot remove all bias no matter how hard they try.

        1.2.5.1.1. Con:
        Even if individual scientists are biased, scientific communities and processes [79] act as a check on individual bias.

        1.2.5.1.2. Pro:
        Every researcher inherently holds a position relative to the subject [80] based on their personal experiences and relevant information they have previously encountered.

      1.2.5.2. Pro:
      Scientific training involves an in-depth understanding of the different categories and methods of observation [81].

        1.2.5.2.1. Pro:
        Science distinguishes between natural, staged, qualitative, and quantitative [82] observations and acknowledges the issues inherent to each.

        1.2.5.2.2. Con:
        Objective observations can still be interpreted in a biased way (p. 14 [83]).

      1.2.5.3. Pro:
      Proper study design can eliminate many possible sources of observational bias (p. 664 [84]).

        1.2.5.3.1. Con:
        Objective study designs are not inherently more likely to generate scientific truth [85].

        1.2.5.3.2. Pro:
        Processes such as randomization [86] can greatly reduce the opportunity for bias in research.

      1.2.5.4. Con:
      Training may be insufficient in overcoming the natural tendency of an observer to impose subjective beliefs.

        1.2.5.4.1. Pro:
        Implicit bias training has largely been found to be ineffective (p. 3 [87]).

        1.2.5.4.2. Con:
        Even if training cannot produce entirely objective observers, it can still promote increasing levels of objectivity [88].

  1.3. Con:
  Science is subject to institutional and social forces that compromise its objectivity (p. 1 [89]).

    1.3.1. Pro:
    Science is subject to political motivations and influence.

      1.3.1.1. Pro:
      Scientific research on sensitive political topics, such as vaccines [90] or climate change, is heavily politicized, and results are often skewed for political purposes.

        1.3.1.1.1. Con:
        While news and public discourse surrounding climate change are driven by politics, climate scientists [91] themselves remain focused on objective inquiry and analysis.

        1.3.1.1.2. Pro:
        Even when scientific research remains objective, it is often used the broader context of society to support subjective political positions [92].

          1.3.1.1.2.1. Con:
          When subjective political positions find support in science, the more they do so the less subjective they become. The use of science in policy [93], law [94]-making and politics is beneficial to society and objectivity.

            1.3.1.1.2.1.1. Pro:
            The scientific knowledge about the harms of lead as well as the science about alternatives were used in policy when lead-use was [95] largely phased out [96] against [97] the [98] opposition of industry and short-term economic interests.

      1.3.1.2. Con:
      Even if scientific research is supported or opposed by politicians to suit their purposes, this does not affect the objectivity of the science itself.

        1.3.1.2.1. Con:
        Decisions about scientific research funding are made by politicians, who are likely to hold entrenched views on certain topics and support the work of scientists who hold similar perspectives.

        1.3.1.2.2. Con:
        A fear of losing funding can compromise the integrity of scientific researchers.

        1.3.1.2.3. Pro:
        The methodology and results of politically funded research can be independently evaluated or reproduced.

    1.3.2. Pro:
    When scientific research is supported by commercial entities [99], researchers are often pressured to distort results.

      1.3.2.1. Pro:
      Pharmaceutical giant Merck manipulated the clinical studies of Vioxx by using flawed methodologies to conceal the increased cardiovascular risk [100] associated with the drug while seeking FDA approval.

        1.3.2.1.1. Con:
        The massive fines and settlements [101] in the Vioxx case illustrate the economic risks of manipulating the scientific process for commercial gain.

        1.3.2.1.2. Pro:
        The flawed results of the study led to a great amount of harm in patients [102], which would have been avoided by an objective analysis.

      1.3.2.2. Pro:
      Commercial entities are often eager to present their research as objective [103], even in cases where they have a clear conflict of interest (p. 45 [104]).

        1.3.2.2.1. Pro:
        Industry-sponsored research consistently produces results that are skewed to support the sponsor's financial interest (p. 1213 [105]).

        1.3.2.2.2. Con:
        Industry sponsorship does not inherently compromise the objectivity of research; each study must be evaluated on its own merits [106].

      1.3.2.3. Con:
      A great deal of science is funded by government grants and institutions [107] rather than commercial interests.

        1.3.2.3.1. Pro:
        Many countries have implemented revised regulations for publicly funded research, which promote more objective outcomes (p. 52 [108]).

        1.3.2.3.2. Con:
        Commercial support of scientific research surpassed federal spending in 2013 [109] and has continued to rise.

    1.3.3. Con:
    The scientific community itself acts as an institutional check on the objectivity of results.

      1.3.3.1. Pro:
      There is great intellectual diversity in the scientific community, so not all members hold the same perspectives and biases (p. 1 [110]). Productive scientific arguments occur when different biases clash, so it is not inherently problematic that scientists have biases

        1.3.3.1.1. Con:
        Less than 30% [111] of the world’s researchers are women, which creates gender bias in scientific research.

        1.3.3.1.2. Pro:
        Even homogenous groups are able to detect and mitigate for potential bias. Given that recognizing bias is an essential part of analyzing data, it is likely that researchers are more able than other demographics to self-identify inherent bias when it exists.

        1.3.3.1.3. Con:
        Because some sciences are disproportionately composed of people from similar ethnocultural backgrounds, cultural bias often goes internally unchecked.

          1.3.3.1.3.1. Pro:
          Race science [112] and other scientific theories have been used to perpetuate [113] white supremacy and legitimize social biases.

            1.3.3.1.3.1.1. Con:
            Scientific racism [114] is one of many topics uniformly acknowledged as pseudoscience (p. 57 [115]); science itself can't be blamed for pseudoscientific theories.

            1.3.3.1.3.1.2. Pro:
            Many mainstream scientists today continue to make use of race as a category in their studies (pp. 719–720 [116]). Thus, race science continues to feature prominently in works that are considered scientifically valid despite having no grounding in fact.

        1.3.3.1.4. Con:
        Almost all scientists are highly educated, so they tend to share the cultural biases of the highly-educated.

      1.3.3.2. Pro:
      Processes such as presentations and discussions at scientific conferences [117] attempt to pool and apply a maximum number of perspectives to eliminate bias before research is accepted.

        1.3.3.2.1. Pro:
        Presentations at scientific conferences involve interactive communication [118] between presenters and attendees, allowing results to be interrogated and raising questions for future study. This promotes a broader investigation of findings that facilitates objectivity (p. 432 [119]).

        1.3.3.2.2. Con:
        Scientific presentations and discussions can place a great deal of pressure on the presenter to conform [120] with prevailing opinion.

      1.3.3.3. Con:
      Scientific communities often espouse particular frameworks and values that can predispose researchers towards conformity with accepted theories and ideologies (p. 10 [121]).

        1.3.3.3.1. Pro:
        Scientists may choose to act or present information in a way that contradicts their own beliefs in order to conform with current opinion (p. 19 [122]).

        1.3.3.3.2. Con:
        The reputation of many notable scientists rests on their discovery of novel concepts opposed to current consensus; as such, there is an equal pressure to avoid conformity [123].

      1.3.3.4. Con:
      There are numerous examples where the scientific consensus has later been discovered to be objectively incorrect [124].

        1.3.3.4.1. Con:
        Such situations act as proof that, over time, science self-corrects [125] to become more objective.

        1.3.3.4.2. Pro:
        Scientific fallacies often go unchallenged, and at times may constitute the bulk of the available knowledge in a particular field (p. 645 [126]).

      1.3.3.5. Pro:
      The established norms of the scientific community [127] promote objectivity.

        1.3.3.5.1. Con:
        The behavior of scientists is not bound by such norms, which are often used predominantly as a method for presenting results to outsiders (p. 4 [128]).

        1.3.3.5.2. Pro:
        The scientific community functions as a self-governing society [129] organized around values and ethics that support objectivity.

    1.3.4. Con:
    Some institutional forces are specifically established to protect scientific objectivity.

      1.3.4.1. Pro:
      Research institutions require the disclosure of any conflicts of interests to help ensure scientific objectivity (p. 1 [130]).

        1.3.4.1.1. Con:
        There have been numerous instances where scientific research supported by institutions has suffered from serious undisclosed conflicts of interest (p. 35 [131]).

        1.3.4.1.2. Con:
        Research institutions themselves often have undisclosed conflicts of interest that can compromise their objectivity (pp. 201-202 [132]).

        1.3.4.1.3. Pro:
        Researchers are not only required to reveal actual conflicts of interest; they must disclose any relationship or relevant information that could be perceived as undermining their objectivity (p. 197 [133]).

      1.3.4.2. Pro:
      Government agencies, such as the National Science Foundation (NSF) must adhere to strict guidelines regarding the quality of information they provide, including its objectivity (pp. 1, 9 [134]).

        1.3.4.2.1. Con:
        Research has revealed significant disparities in the awarding of NSF grants [135] to researchers of different ethnicities, indicating a lack of objectivity in their research funding decisions.

        1.3.4.2.2. Pro:
        The NSF employs both internal and external review of proposals [136] to ensure the objectivity of funding decisions and of the research itself.

      1.3.4.3. Con:
      Institutions inherently create opportunities for systemic biases, which can often be unconscious and difficult to uncover (p. 436 [137]).

        1.3.4.3.1. Pro:
        Institutional practices, funding methods, and decision-making processes are often structured in a way that supports the interests of privileged social groups [138].

        1.3.4.3.2. Con:
        Institutions have the resources to investigate and address systemic bias [139] in a comprehensive way, which is often beyond the means of individual scientists.

    1.3.5. Pro:
    Scientists are subjected to certain career pressures, such as the need to "publish or perish," that can affect their results (p. 87 [140]).

      1.3.5.1. Con:
      Scientific journals generally make extensive use of peer review to ameliorate the effects of researcher bias (p. 228 [141]).

        1.3.5.1.1. Con:
        Peer review is subject to blind spots.

          1.3.5.1.1.1. Con:
          As long as the reviewers are diverse enough to represent different perspectives and interrogate the research, then implicit bias and blind spots are reduced.

          1.3.5.1.1.2. Pro:
          Peer review is fundamentally conservative. Since the peers performing a review are already successful within the current paradigm, they act as gatekeepers to prevent the acceptance of new ideas outside of that paradigm.

            1.3.5.1.1.2.1. Con:
            This conservative function of peer review promotes objectivity by examining new results in the context of existing knowledge.

        1.3.5.1.2. Pro:
        Blinded peer review, in which reviewers are given no information about the author, results in a more objective treatment [142] of the material.

      1.3.5.2. Pro:
      Many academic careers [143] require candidates to have an extensive history of research publications.

        1.3.5.2.1. Pro:
        The evaluation of academic publication history by administrators untrained in the field has resulted in a glut of low-quality research [144].

        1.3.5.2.2. Con:
        The faster availability and shorter formats enabled by online publication will continue to increase the objectivity [145] of modern research.

    1.3.6. Pro:
    Many individual studies get retracted [146].

      1.3.6.1. Pro:
      In 2023, a record of 10,000 retractions [147] was reached, often studies by Hindawi journals overseen by guest editors that were detected to likely be exploited to rapidly publish low-quality or sham papers.

        1.3.6.1.1. Con:
        That so many have been detected just shows how effective and robust the issue detection mechanisms in science are. Rather than staying in the scientific literature as is, they are clearly removed from it or marked as retracted.

          1.3.6.1.1.1. Con:
          Studies have suggested as few as 4% of citations to retracted papers [148] clearly recognize the retraction.

  1.4. Pro:
  Science is based on facts, which are inherently objective (p. 1 [149]).

    1.4.1. Pro:
    Facts are objective [150] and are not subject to opinion or bias [151].

      1.4.1.1. Con:
      There is no such thing as an observation without theory [152].

        1.4.1.1.1. Con:
        Observations rely on the existence of both facts and theory, but facts do not depend on either theories or observations for their existence.

        1.4.1.1.2. Pro:
        Facts themselves are objective, but which facts one chooses to observe and report are subjectively determined.

      1.4.1.2. Con:
      Science also deals with subjective facts [153].

        1.4.1.2.1. Pro:
        Many forms of community-based and participatory research are inherently subjective in nature (p. 9 [154]).

        1.4.1.2.2. Con:
        If a so-called "fact" relies on human experience, then it should not be considered as such in the scientific sense.

      1.4.1.3. Pro:
      Scientific facts are based on defined, measurable parameters [155].

        1.4.1.3.1. Con:
        Scientific measurement relies on mathematics, which carries inherent ethnocultural biases [156].

        1.4.1.3.2. Pro:
        Measurable parameters rely on mathematics [157], which is independent of our subjective thoughts and perspectives.

    1.4.2. Pro:
    Science utilizes empirical evidence [158], which can be observed by anyone [159] who carries out the experiment.

      1.4.2.1. Pro:
      The requirement that science be reproducible [160] means that any person with the requisite skills and materials can carry out the experiment and verify results.

        1.4.2.1.1. Con:
        Over 70% of surveyed scientific researchers have failed to achieve the same results [161] when reproducing the experiments of others, and more than 50% have failed to reproduce their own experiments.

          1.4.2.1.1.1. Pro:
          Results were even more bleak in certain fields; for example, only 40% of psychological study results and 10% of cancer biology study results [162] could be reproduced.

        1.4.2.1.2. Con:
        In many fields, non-reproducible studies are routinely performed; researchers estimate that up to 66% of social science research [163] cannot be replicated.

        1.4.2.1.3. Pro:
        Many of the most influential scientific experiments can easily be reproduced [164] by anyone using easily available materials.

          1.4.2.1.3.1. Con:
          Many modern experiments require materials and instruments [165] that are inaccessible to those who are outside the scientific community or lack institutional funding (p. 16 [166]).

          1.4.2.1.3.2. Pro:
          Amateur scientists [167] have continued to make numerous influential discoveries.

          1.4.2.1.3.3. Pro:
          In the long run and in principle, this means reproducibility is sufficient to represent a pillar [168] of the objectivity [169] of the scientific process [170].

            1.4.2.1.3.3.1. Pro:
            If experiments are then not reproduced in practice, it may be due to issues regarding societal implementation of this methodology.

          1.4.2.1.3.4. Con:
          Many highly cited studies as well as large shares of e.g. [171] psychological [172] research [173] and scientific [174] studies on cancer [175] are not reproducible in practice [176], which some have described as a replication crisis.

      1.4.2.2. Con:
      The scientific community has little interest in repeating experiments in support of accepted theories and is instead primarily focused on new research and discoveries.

        1.4.2.2.1. Pro:
        Although replication studies are essential to good science, they are rarely pursued or incentivized as they lack the prestige of original studies [177].

          1.4.2.2.1.1. Pro:
          Often pieces of new research are not re-produced by other scientists because it is too expensive. As a result, many studies are technically unconfirmed.

        1.4.2.2.2. Con:
        The focus on new research may be to the benefit of scientific objectivity [178], as new research is more likely than replication studies to yield results that challenge existing theories and models.

    1.4.3. Con:
    Science is not based entirely on facts; it includes theories and models that propose explanations consistent with, yet extending beyond, the observed facts.

      1.4.3.1. Pro:
      Science generates frameworks of theories and predictive models for future experiments, proposing causal explanations that are unsupported by, and extend beyond, the facts.

        1.4.3.1.1. Con:
        Scientific theories and models are understood to be plausible explanations that conform with the available evidence and do not purport to be factual.

        1.4.3.1.2. Pro:
        Educators and politicians have often presented disputed scientific theories, such as human-induced climate change, as facts rather than theories (p. 2 [179]).

          1.4.3.1.2.1. Pro:
          Political organizations, such as the UN, have failed to distinguish between fact and scientific theory [180] with regard to climate change.

          1.4.3.1.2.2. Con:
          The theory that climate change is a result of human activity is well-supported by the facts and is not disputed within the scientific community [181].

      1.4.3.2. Pro:
      Science often relies on inferred models and visualizations [182] that do not accurately represent the phenomena in question.

        1.4.3.2.1. Pro:
        Models inherently lack the detail and accuracy [183] of the phenomena they represent.

          1.4.3.2.1.1. Con:
          A model that accurately represented every characteristic of a phenomenon would be unnecessary, as it would be indistinguishable from the phenomenon itself.

        1.4.3.2.2. Con:
        Models can focus on specific aspects of a phenomenon to intentionally highlight and explore the relationship between particular characteristics [184].

      1.4.3.3. Con:
      Even when science proposes ideas and explanations that extend beyond the facts, such theories only remain valid insofar as they continue to conform to future experimentation and evidence (p. 2 [185]).

        1.4.3.3.1. Pro:
        Over time, scientific theories and models that fail to conform to empirical facts are modified accordingly or discarded (p. 5 [186]).

        1.4.3.3.2. Con:
        A scientific explanation being consistent with the available facts does not make it correct; many long-held scientific theories have later been found incorrect despite conforming with the evidence [187].

    1.4.4. Con:
    Science is based upon our subjective assessment of certain claims as factual rather than on facts themselves.

      1.4.4.1. Pro:
      Information presented as fact is often subjective due to imperfect measurement apparatuses or a lack of objectivity on the part of the experimenters.

        1.4.4.1.1. Con:
        Scientists strive to maximize the precision and accuracy [188] of their measurements wherever possible.

        1.4.4.1.2. Pro:
        Systematic and random errors [189] in measurement are unavoidable.

          1.4.4.1.2.1. Con:
          Additive correction, proportional correction, and averaging [190] can be used to improve the accuracy of scientific measurements.

      1.4.4.2. Con:
      Facts are distinct from claims, such as inferences and judgments [191], insofar as they exist independently of the observer and their interpretations.

        1.4.4.2.1. Pro:
        The mass and chemical composition of a rock remains demonstrably the same regardless of observation, whereas an inference about the formation of a rock relies on the prior experience and knowledge of the observer.

        1.4.4.2.2. Con:
        A truly objective observation would necessarily include a description of the observer as an element of the phenomenon being investigated. For example, it is more objective to say "a human observer recorded the mass of the rock as 50 kg using a triple-beam balance" than to say "the mass of the rock is 50 kg."

      1.4.4.3. Pro:
      While facts themselves are not subject to personal opinion, the development of scientific theories [192] involves a subjective interpretation of facts.

        1.4.4.3.1. Pro:
        The interpretation of facts is subject to personal, subjective factors, such as gender or cultural bias.

        1.4.4.3.2. Con:
        Scientific theories are not arbitrary interpretations, as they must remain consistent with objective facts, such as observable measurements [193].
