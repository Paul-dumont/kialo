Discussion Name:
Should There be a Universal Basic Income (UBI)?

1. Thesis:
Wealthy countries should provide citizens with a universal basic income (UBI).

  1.1. Pro:
  A UBI would reduce crime rates.

    1.1.1. Con:
    A UBI would attract more illegal immigration, which typically leads to increased crime rates.

      1.1.1.1. Pro:
      In many countries, being born in that country grants the child automatic citizenship. If that child then receives the UBI, it provides an incentive to immigrate illegally.

        1.1.1.1.1. Con:
        UBI payments for children are typically set at approximately half of that of an adult. Even if a child receives a UBI payment, that is unlikely to be enough money to support the parents.

        1.1.1.1.2. Con:
        Historically, Europe has granted citizenship [1] by right of blood (i.e. inherited by parents) and America by birth, thus this is only applicable to North and South American countries as most countries allow citizenship by birth with restrictions, and several have increasingly changed policies due to immigration.

        1.1.1.1.3. Con:
        This seems to be more an issue with how citizenship works.

      1.1.1.2. Con:
      Eventually, UBIs may be in place in every country, so there would not be such movement across borders due to economic migration.

    1.1.2. Con:
    Studies suggest that implementing a UBI would have little impact [2] on crime rates.

      1.1.2.1. Con:
      Implementing a UBI would likely have a profound effect on poverty rates. Lowering poverty rates and more evenly distributing wealth has been shown to lower crime rates (p. 14 [3]).

      1.1.2.2. Pro:
      Studies have shown that withdrawing the eligibility of prisoners for UBI does not have a deterrent [4] effect.

      1.1.2.3. Con:
      In Namibia, crime decreased by 40% [5] in areas where a UBI was introduced.

    1.1.3. Pro:
    Government cash transfers into households could significantly increase education for children coming from poor backgrounds, thereby decreasing the probability of minor criminal offences (p. 112 [6]).

    1.1.4. Pro:
    A UBI would address the economic causes of crime.

      1.1.4.1. Con:
      Most major financial crimes are motivated by greed [7], to acquire wealth above a subsistence level. A UBI is unlikely to diminish this greed.

        1.1.4.1.1. Pro:
        Four of the five most frequently committed crimes in the US are property crimes [8], despite the existence of existing social safety nets.

          1.1.4.1.1.1. Con:
          This could equally be evidence that existing social safety nets are significantly insufficient.

          1.1.4.1.1.2. Con:
          The US social safety net is one of the least generous in the world [9].

        1.1.4.1.2. Pro:
        Organized crime groups attempt to get rich from crime; not merely subsist. Internationally their acts are worth USD$870 billion a year [10].

          1.1.4.1.2.1. Con:
          Low-level gang members don't make a lot of money [11], but hope they will eventually. This may be enough of an incentive to keep them from low paying jobs, but likely not enough of an incentive for them to continue in the gang culture if a UBI provides an alternative.

        1.1.4.1.3. Con:
        Even if true, a minimal reduction in economic crime does not mean the UBI is not effective in reducing other types of crime, namely the ones committed to acquire basic sustenance.

        1.1.4.1.4. Pro:
        White-collar crime [12] is a significant crime that is motivated by greed rather than need.

      1.1.4.2. Con:
      While UBI can address economic problems, many crimes do not occur due to these issues. For instance, many crimes stem from issues with drugs [13], mental illness [14], relationships [15], or even social status.

        1.1.4.2.1. Con:
        There is a positive relationship [16] between poverty and substance abuse.

          1.1.4.2.1.1. Pro:
          Poverty incentivizes the sale of drugs because it represents a high-risk/high-reward opportunity for people in low-income environments [17].

          1.1.4.2.1.2. Con:
          Correlation doesn't imply causation. The direction [18] of any proposed correlation between poverty and substance abuse is unclear.

        1.1.4.2.2. Con:
        This does not deny that a UBI would reduce the total rate of crimes.

        1.1.4.2.3. Pro:
        UBI is most beneficial to individuals below the poverty line. Some of these individuals are below the poverty line because of addictions. The UBI would enable addictive behavior.

          1.1.4.2.3.1. Con:
          This suggests that not giving them this benefit would stop them from being addicted. There is no reason to believe this.

          1.1.4.2.3.2. Con:
          Staying off of addictive substances is a real struggle, and one needs the means to be able to do it.

          1.1.4.2.3.3. Con:
          Some people in poverty may have addictions, but not all. There are many other causes of, and contributors to, poverty.

          1.1.4.2.3.4. Con:
          It may be worth inadvertently enabling some addictive behaviour in order to deliver the other benefits of the UBI, such as assisting those in poverty for other reasons.

          1.1.4.2.3.5. Pro:
          UBI would provide addictive, impulsive personalities with an endless source of income - enslaving them to impulsivity and rendering change impossible for many.

        1.1.4.2.4. Con:
        A UBI could also give people with drug addictions or mental illness the ability to pay for assistance.

          1.1.4.2.4.1. Con:
          UBI could also give people with drug addictions the means to purchase more drugs.

        1.1.4.2.5. Pro:
        Crime, drugs, broken families and other socially destructive outcomes are more likely [19] in places with high unemployment, due to the leaving a social support network that work provides.

          1.1.4.2.5.1. Con:
          UBI would give more spare time to citizens to organize in social clubs and create social networks outside of traditional, professional, or labor settings.

      1.1.4.3. Pro:
      A UBI could reduce the economic stresses that contribute to crime.

        1.1.4.3.1. Pro:
        Crime stems from a lack of economic opportunities. A UBI provides a means to ameliorate this by enabling people to continue to have an income whilst pursuing an education, or start a business; or at least live on a stable income.

          1.1.4.3.1.1. Pro:
          With their basic needs covered by a UBI, citizens can better afford to spend time [20] studying, overall levels of education grow, and the workforce becomes more skilled.

            1.1.4.3.1.1.1. Con:
            A UBI would not address educational inequality [21], which is one of the main drivers [22] of inequality overall.

          1.1.4.3.1.2. Pro:
          There is a higher correlation between unemployment and crime than between income level and crime. If the economic impoverishment thesis for crime holds, unemployed people receiving the UBI would still be equally likely to commit crime (p. 15 [23]).

          1.1.4.3.1.3. Con:
          Growing education levels do not necessarily increase social mobility, especially for people belonging to traditionally oppressed [24] groups. For example, many university graduates with bachelor degrees are unable to find work in their field, and end up stuck in minimum-wage or part time jobs [25].

            1.1.4.3.1.3.1. Con:
            This is more of a problem with the economy as a whole. It is still definitely true [26] that people with college degrees have an easier time finding high-paying jobs than those without.

        1.1.4.3.2. Pro:
        Financial stress [27] can cause crime when there is a lack of qualifications and social support for at-risk individuals. A UBI provides extra financial support to lower crime, by fostering stability and security towards a decent life instead of through crime.

        1.1.4.3.3. Con:
        Implementing a UBI would require massive increases in taxation on the middle class, resulting in greater stress for a large swath of the population.

          1.1.4.3.3.1. Con:
          There are alternative sources of financing, such as public dividends, that may be used to sustain UBI without creating extra pressure on people.

          1.1.4.3.3.2. Con:
          The tax effects on people who are already well-off and stable would not be as significant as the benefits to people whose quality of life and prospects would be significantly improved by a UBI.

        1.1.4.3.4. Pro:
        A UBI provides people with financial safety, reducing the stress associated with economic and social decisions. That means less stress cycling through from person to person [28] during interactions [29], which also improves social behavior as there will be fewer stressed people to interact with.

      1.1.4.4. Pro:
      A UBI reduces financial inequality by lifting the incomes of the poorest up by a higher percentage than for the wealthy. As crime is connected to financial inequality [30], the UBIs capability to reduce financial inequality will lead to lower crime levels.

        1.1.4.4.1. Con:
        Crime exists at all strata of income. [31]

          1.1.4.4.1.1. Con:
          -> See 1.1.4.2.2.

  1.2. Con:
  UBIs would have negative economic effects on the countries that implement them.

    1.2.1. Pro:
    A UBI would lead to greater inflation. [32]

      1.2.1.1. Pro:
      If the production of services and goods come to an halt, the available resources will become scarce, since nothing is being produced. Therefore it will probably lead to more inflation when more money is printed, causing a downward spiral of the economy.

      1.2.1.2. Pro:
      A UBI may need to be funded by the increased issue of fiat [33] currency. Doing so would increase inflation and thus render any benefits of a UBI moot, since goods would subsequently be priced above the budget a UBI would allow.

        1.2.1.2.1. Con:
        Given the profound levels of current waste and untapped potential, we are nowhere close to maxing out our real productive capacity. The best way to avoid critical inflation from fiat spending is thus to spend it on supporting real productive capacity, rather than upon speculation or rent-seeking (title-income, interest, etc.).

      1.2.1.3. Con:
      A UBI would require an unrealistically high level of inflation to occur for the benefits of a universal basic income to be strongly mitigated.

        1.2.1.3.1. Pro:
        The amount of inflation that UBI causes may not be much because it's aimed at getting people out of the poverty line [34]. Net recipients of UBI transfers are going to be spending mostly on basic needs and thus have minimal effect on the money supply.

        1.2.1.3.2. Con:
        Even if the level of inflation under a UBI was moderate, it might still be high enough to render the implementation of a UBI less efficient to a family than other forms of welfare.

        1.2.1.3.3. Pro:
        A UBI will only have an effect on inflation if it is set so high that demand outstrips the ability of the labor market and natural resources to meet that demand.

          1.2.1.3.3.1. Pro:
          In most cases, supply can rise to meet increasing demand, undercutting any inflationary pressure. The obvious exception is real estate, but a UBI will allow more people to migrate away from concentrated population centers because they are no longer dependent on the jobs there. This will effectively reduce demand for real estate, countering any inflationary pressure. Renewable energy will counter the inflation of fuel prices.

      1.2.1.4. Pro:
      A UBI will raise the general demand in a national economy as citizens would have more available income that could be used for consumption, investment and saving [35]. This process will lead to a higher price level and thus inflation [36].

        1.2.1.4.1. Con:
        There only is a very limited positive correlation [37] between changes in income and changes in consumption.

          1.2.1.4.1.1. Con:
          The researchers here are careful that the study would not infer macroeconomic trends (inflation being a macroeconomic trend). Those who already have a JPMorgan Chase account would not be those who go from being homeless to affording a mobile home, for example.

        1.2.1.4.2. Pro:
        Assuming the vast majority of citizens will not stop working, the UBI is an addition on top of their incomes. This leads to an increase in the amount of money in the national economy.

        1.2.1.4.3. Pro:
        The overall (even anticipated) increase in income from a UBI will increase demand (for better housing, products, foods, etc.) and, thus, costs. As such, any benefits of a UBI would level out within a few years (p.502 [38]).

          1.2.1.4.3.1. Con:
          The UBI rate can grow in response to changes in the cost of living. These problems have been faced, and dealt with with other welfare programs. [39]

          1.2.1.4.3.2. Con:
          To say people would consume more based on their income is, beyond plain obvious, not the same as saying such an event would cause inflation.

          1.2.1.4.3.3. Pro:
          A UBI will not only create more inflation, but it will create a positive feedback loop. A UBI will cause inflation, which will raise the cost of goods and services, which will lead to raising the UBI.

        1.2.1.4.4. Pro:
        It does appear that UBI would increase aggregate demand. This would then indirectly increase inflation as per demand-pull inflation [40].

          1.2.1.4.4.1. Con:
          This would only be the case in a scenario where offer was inelastic (either because goods are too expensive to produce, thus unenforceable, or already at peak production, because all the land and resources are already destined to other more important products, making opportunity cost too high), otherwise it would respond to the expansion in the consumer market by producing more, effectively offsetting inflation

        1.2.1.4.5. Con:
        This would only be the case in a scenario where supply was inelastic (either because goods are too expensive to produce, or already at peak production, because all the land and resources are already used for other more important products, making opportunity cost too high), otherwise producers would respond to the expansion in the consumer market by producing more, effectively offsetting inflation

          1.2.1.4.5.1. Con:
          Not all goods are elastic, e.g. land. Therefore, the inflation would only be offset in some sectors of the economy and not others.

        1.2.1.4.6. Con:
        The hand that provides the resources for UBI will be the same hand that taxes the economy to generate the revenue. UBI is redistributive in its principle, so what purchasing power you take from one you given to another. Ignoring the intricacies of exactly how consumer goods markets will adjust, the net impact on inflation is likely to be neutral. Furthermore, successful implementation of UBI will require coordination between fiscal and monetary policy authorities.

      1.2.1.5. Con:
      Inflation would only occur if a country's money supply was increased to pay for a UBI.

        1.2.1.5.1. Pro:
        Because a UBI [41] is a redistribution plan, not a growth-oriented one, the amount of money in existence will, in aggregate, be the same. Therefore, overall consumption will not increase and the net cost is zero.

          1.2.1.5.1.1. Con:
          Redistributing money [42] to the poor will likely increase goods sales as the poor spend proportionately more of their income on consumables than on savings.

          1.2.1.5.1.2. Pro:
          Implementing a UBI would allow governments to reduce spending in other areas (such as income tax credits [43]); thus reducing its cost.

          1.2.1.5.1.3. Con:
          The net cost would not be zero as there would be costs associated with managing the system itself.

        1.2.1.5.2. Pro:
        The Quantity Theory of Money [44] states that inflation will only happen if money supply increases. With UBI, aggregate money supply in the economy does not increase; it just redistributes money from one part of the economy (the rich, specific industries, specific economic activities, etc., depending on how the UBI was funded) to the public.

        1.2.1.5.3. Con:
        Besides the quantity of money, which affects general inflation, the redistribution of money creates localised inflation too.

          1.2.1.5.3.1. Pro:
          In the USA, as more money was made available for university costs through loans, the costs then soared far beyond that of general inflation. A UBI would create localised inflation rendering the income payments useless.

          1.2.1.5.3.2. Pro:
          An equal influx of money to all the population would create severe localised infaltion in consumer goods such as food, transport, and housing as the purchasing power of everyone is shifted upwards.

          1.2.1.5.3.3. Con:
          While a UBI would cause some localised inflation, it is impossible to make sweeping statements on whether that would offset the costs.

          1.2.1.5.3.4. Con:
          There's a critical difference between a UBI and money made available for higher education: the latter is specifically purposed (which logically increases the risk of inflation) and the former is not. It allows people to spend the money on a large variety of things, greatly lowering the risk/amount of inflation.

        1.2.1.5.4. Con:
        Inflation for individual products will increase faster for products generally bought by UBI recipients. Overall across all products inflation may remain the same.

      1.2.1.6. Con:
      Real cases suggest that a UBI do not cause greater inflation.

        1.2.1.6.1. Pro:
        In a study in Mexico [45], giving out cash actually had a smaller effect on the market than giving out necessary goods - in this case, food.

        1.2.1.6.2. Pro:
        Both Alaska and Kuwait [46] experienced decreases in inflation after versions of UBI were implemented.

    1.2.2. Con:
    A UBI would make people more likely to attempt to start their own companies or become self-employed.

      1.2.2.1. Con:
      A UBI may provide an income that is barely above the poverty line, which would be barely enough to live on let alone provide resources enough to start a business with.

        1.2.2.1.1. Con:
        Removing the risk of abject poverty as a result of failure could very well be a motivating factor for quite a few lower-income people deciding whether or not to risk entrepreneurship.

        1.2.2.1.2. Con:
        The largest barrier to starting a business is time. Most people cannot quit their job for long enough to get a business up and running, or the consequence of failure is to lose everything. You still require food, housing and transportation while starting a business. A UBI would allow most people to survive while also focusing on starting a business.

          1.2.2.1.2.1. Pro:
          Some potential entrepreneurs cannot afford to leave their current employment to work on projects they hope will pan out to successful businesses.  A UBI would allow such entrepreneurs to take extended time off of paid to work to invest in their startup.

          1.2.2.1.2.2. Con:
          Anyone whose bills still exceed the amount they get from the UBI (which would especially be the case for single-breadwinner family households) would not be able to start a business because of this. At best, it is helpful for a tiny slice of people at the margins.

      1.2.2.2. Pro:
      A UBI would enhance entrepreneurship, sustaining people while they develop projects that may take time to generate profits.

        1.2.2.2.1. Pro:
        61% [47] of employed Americans want to start their own business as they prefer to work for themselves than for someone else.

        1.2.2.2.2. Con:
        Start-up failure rate in the US after 4 years is between 42% and 63%. This shows that this is not a long-term sustainable pathway for most people. (StatisticBrain [48])

        1.2.2.2.3. Pro:
        UBI pilot studies in Zambia and Mexico showed increased entrepreneurial activity (p. 10) [49].

        1.2.2.2.4. Pro:
        UBI means that those looking to start new businesses or become self-employed can do so without the risk of losing the ability to pay for living costs. This allows for trial and error with relative safety for budding entrepreneurs.

        1.2.2.2.5. Con:
        UBI is designed for welfare, not venture funding. Those who start a business are involved in serving the public which is a large responsibility. Businesses that do not serve the public must be allowed to fail quickly.

          1.2.2.2.5.1. Con:
          Businesses that do not serve the public effectively would still fail or perform poorly with a UBI, as their more effective competitors will outperform them and substandard products and services will be recognized and rejected by consumers.

      1.2.2.3. Pro:
      A UBI provides an instant safety net and thus reduces the need for overly-expensive, or exploitative credit.

        1.2.2.3.1. Pro:
        With a UBI, banks will be more likely to grant lines of credit, as applicants have a UBI to fall back on as loan security.

          1.2.2.3.1.1. Con:
          Banks want a good asset-to-debt ratio when assessing loans, and will likely calculate UBI payments as a negative factor in the same way they already count Social Security Income, in the US, as a negative.

        1.2.2.3.2. Con:
        If the UBI is utilized to generate a line of credit then it is not being used to cover essentials such as food, clothing, and shelter.

      1.2.2.4. Con:
      Implementing a UBI could require increasing income or business taxes that make it harder to start businesses or maintain employees.

      1.2.2.5. Con:
      Extending government grants and support schemes would better support new businesses and cost less.

        1.2.2.5.1. Pro:
        For the same cost of a UBI, much more support to new businesses could be provided through targeted government grants and loans. The money saved from not paying 'wasted' UBI to the wealthy could go into initial grants for new small businesses, to get them off the ground.

        1.2.2.5.2. Pro:
        Government support would be time-bounded, which would give the necessary drive for businesses to work hard or fail. A UBI could indefinitely prop up businesses which may have no productive future.

        1.2.2.5.3. Pro:
        Support could be conditional on a good business plan. This helps make sure that people are working on a business with actual potential, which would reduce stress and the high chance of failure [50].

    1.2.3. Con:
    UBIs would increase the productivity of workers.

      1.2.3.1. Pro:
      Financial insecurity typically makes workers less efficient and motivated.

        1.2.3.1.1. Pro:
        Financial insecurity often leads people to take the first job available to them, not the job they would be best at.

        1.2.3.1.2. Pro:
        Many people work jobs they are not good at because they need them to earn money and survive. Consequently, they often do so tired, without motivation, and without care for the final outcome.

      1.2.3.2. Pro:
      Many willing and productive people are sitting at home, searching for a job. If UBI were implemented, people who don't like their jobs could leave, creating more job openings.

      1.2.3.3. Con:
      UBIs tend to incentivize more value-driven activities (like sports or the arts), creating inefficiencies in how people spend their time and money.

        1.2.3.3.1. Con:
        There is more to life than productivity. If there is no time to use and enjoy the utility we create, it is valueless.

          1.2.3.3.1.1. Pro:
          Factors other than mere productivity and GDP, including well-being [51], are considerations.

      1.2.3.4. Pro:
      Evidence [52] suggests that a UBI would benefit people's psychological well-being and productivity, which has been shown to increase workplace productivity [53].

        1.2.3.4.1. Pro:
        People who work due to passion and interest, as opposed to financial necessity, are more productive [54].

          1.2.3.4.1.1. Pro:
          One reason [55] for low productivity is that people are unable to enjoy the benefits [56] of the money they earn through their labor, since they have no time.

        1.2.3.4.2. Pro:
        Current welfare systems induce a psychology of fear of 'earning too much' [57], which can hinder the potential for self-reliance and economic growth. A UBI would remove this fear, as it is not dependent on one's earnings.

    1.2.4. Pro:
    Instituting a UBI would likely require higher taxes, which would hurt the economy.

      1.2.4.1. Pro:
      In the short run, tax increases have a negative impact on demand [58]. As households and companies have less available income and capital due to higher taxes, they will spend and invest less.

      1.2.4.2. Pro:
      In the long run high marginal tax rates [59] have a negative impact on the supply by discouraging work, saving, investment, and innovation.

      1.2.4.3. Pro:
      A UBI would enable people to work less or even leave the workforce permanently. This reduced incentive to work will reduce government tax revenue.

        1.2.4.3.1. Pro:
        It is in the best interest of society to heavily incentivize all people to participate in the workforce, as it is crucial for economic development. [60]

          1.2.4.3.1.1. Pro:
          A UBI, by eliminating the need to work, would strongly decrease the incentives that promote innovation [61].

        1.2.4.3.2. Con:
        A UBI will enable unhappy workers who detract from work productivity to leave their jobs, so happy and productive [62] workers can take their place.

          1.2.4.3.2.1. Con:
          This assumes happy workers are more productive, which is false [63].

        1.2.4.3.3. Con:
        Evidence suggests that social security systems like UBI do not [64] lead people to stop working.

          1.2.4.3.3.1. Pro:
          Germany has a very sophisticated social state [65], with a variety of public payments and benefits that enable citizens to live at least a humble life without work. Nevertheless, the unemployment rate [66] is very low.

          1.2.4.3.3.2. Pro:
          The average amount given for social security in the US is not enough [67] for many to live on without other streams of income.

          1.2.4.3.3.3. Pro:
          In 2011 Iran established a program [68] that guaranteed citizens cash payments equivalent to a third of the nation's median income. A report found no evidence for the hypothesis this unconditional income reduced the willingness to work.

          1.2.4.3.3.4. Pro:
          A UBI experiment in Finland showed that those who received UBI did not work less [69] than a control group of those who received traditional benefits.

        1.2.4.3.4. Con:
        People will not drop out of work due to cultural reasons.

          1.2.4.3.4.1. Pro:
          The Protestant work ethic [70] is a strong element of many western societies that causes moral and psychological [71] pressure to work, and is still a dominant spirit of the modern era. (Castells, Himanen, p. 19 [72]).

          1.2.4.3.4.2. Pro:
          In many cultures, especially in East Asia (e.g. South Korea [73]), there is huge societal pressure to work.

      1.2.4.4. Con:
      The IMF released a report claiming that higher taxes would not negatively affect growth [74].

      1.2.4.5. Con:
      It is estimated that having a UBI for 8 years will grow the USA's GDP by 2.5 trillion dollars. [75]

      1.2.4.6. Con:
      A UBI would improve people's heath, thus creating major savings in the healthcare sector, which is often publicly funded.

        1.2.4.6.1. Con:
        There are government public assistance programs [76] available that already provide benefits to improving health care access, education, proper nutrition, and housing.

        1.2.4.6.2. Pro:
        Basic income has been shown to have a positive affect [77] on birth outcomes in Alaska.

        1.2.4.6.3. Pro:
        A UBI reduces mental health complications (p. 4 [78]).

          1.2.4.6.3.1. Pro:
          Unresolved, compounded stress [79] can lead to mental disorders like depression and anxiety. With money being a major stressor [80] of 64% of Americans-particularly in the lower income bracket-a UBI can decrease this stress by increasing the amount of money contributed to a household.

          1.2.4.6.3.2. Pro:
          Many suicides are correlated with poverty [81], harsh living circumstances, work stress, and hatred of one's job. A UBI would diminish these causes.

        1.2.4.6.4. Pro:
        India trialed a UBI in 2010 [82]: The trial resulted in a sharp increase in food sufficiency and a drop in illness in villages that received the funds compared to those that did not.

          1.2.4.6.4.1. Pro:
          Chronic diseases [83] such as diabetes and heart disease often result in multiple hospitalizations. These conditions can be helped and prevented through proper nutrition [84]; a resource UBI can help provide.

    1.2.5. Con:
    A UBI gives everyone money to spend on goods and services, therefore increasing aggregate demand and economic growth.

      1.2.5.1. Con:
      Redistribution is zero-sum: everything given to unproductive people has to be taken from productive people, either directly through taxes, or indirectly through borrowing or inflation.

        1.2.5.1.1. Pro:
        Even if the transferred money drives up sales for productive people (who offer goods or services), because the money used to purchase the goods and services had been first taken from them, they essentially had to provide them for free.

      1.2.5.2. Pro:
      The multiplier effect [85] shows that the accumulation of capital harms the national economy. By redirecting money pooling at the top through a continual redistribution [86] from the top-middle down to the bottom-middle of the income spectrum, the entire economy would actually expand.

        1.2.5.2.1. Pro:
        Once people can easily afford basic goods like food, water or shelter, or health, this would release public money from health-related spending like disease prevention and boost the income of private companies with millions of new consumers. The company's new income would boost tax revenue and help compensate the initial investment in the UBI.

        1.2.5.2.2. Con:
        Increased spending on consumption has negative externalities.

      1.2.5.3. Pro:
      A UBI would likely have a growth effect on the market by putting money in the hands of the impoverished, who studies have proven spend a greater percentage [87] of their income than wealthier individuals.

        1.2.5.3.1. Pro:
        Those with lower incomes are less likely to save than their wealthier counterparts, meaning they will be adding money directly back into the economy (p. 1-2 [88]).

        1.2.5.3.2. Pro:

        1.6.5.4.2. Con:
        A UBI will make citizens very picky and demanding concerning their job choices. Some positions and jobs would be very hard or next to impossible to staff.

          1.6.5.4.2.1. Con:
          It is the companies' duty to create incentives in order to staff their positions.

        1.6.5.4.3. Pro:
        In the US, only 30% of workers feel engaged in their jobs; leading to productivity losses of around $500 billion a year [237].

          1.6.5.4.3.1. Pro:
          Productivity tends to increase [238] when people enjoy the work they do. Allowing them to do so will therefore benefit the economy.

        1.6.5.4.4. Con:
        UBIs threaten corporations that depend on salaried employees that earn all of their income from them.

          1.6.5.4.4.1. Pro:
          A UBI could lead to increased employee turnover if employees are able to quit due to small dissatisfactions. This hurts companies, and in the long run, the economy.

          1.6.5.4.4.2. Con:
          If a business can't survive paying a fair rate to its employees, it shouldn't exist in the first place.

        1.6.5.4.5. Pro:
        Taking renumeration to make ends meet out of the equation will also allow people to only pursue what is of interest to them. Counter-intuitively, the biggest impact will be in the job market, as workplace efficiency was studied and known [239] to be proportional to the psychological effects of workplace appreciation & gratitude.

        1.6.5.4.6. Con:
        This is not a feature unique to a UBI. It is something that targeted unemployment benefits manage to achieve quite well under the status quo.

          1.6.5.4.6.1. Pro:
          In some countries, governments guarantee [240] a percentage of their salary [241] to people who have been fired from their jobs. Often, that is a rate that would far exceed that of a UBI. This gives unemployed [242] people of all incomes enough of a buffer to handle jobless periods.

            1.6.5.4.6.1.1. Con:
            Most targeted unemployment benefits have time limitations. Across Europe, only Belgium guarantees unlimited unemployment benefits. In other countries, the benefits last from 3 to 24 months for a worker with 22 years of contributions (European Commission, pg. 5 [243]).

          1.6.5.4.6.2. Con:
          While unemployment benefits help people seeking full time employment, they generally provide a disincentive to finding part time work, as reporting this work means losing part or all of their benefits for that period.

      1.6.5.5. Pro:
      A UBI will help the labor market adapt to inevitable disruptions caused by advancements [244] in automation and artificial intelligence.

        1.6.5.5.1. Pro:
        Technological development and automation may leave half the population [245], or, in some cases, two thirds of the population [246], at risk of job loss. In such a scenario, even many productive and responsible people would benefit from having a UBI as a safety net.

        1.6.5.5.2. Con:
        It is too early to predict whether automation will actually take enough jobs that the economy couldn't support normal employment, with some arguing that the belief that this will lead to unemployment is actually an 'economic delusion' [247].

          1.6.5.5.2.1. Pro:
          In the past, new technologies [248] have always created more jobs than they have destroyed.

          1.6.5.5.2.2. Pro:
          In Germany, there is a huge number of robot-intensive industries and a high degree of automation. However, this does not negatively affect total employment [249].

            1.6.5.5.2.2.1. Con:
            Although total employment has not been intensively negatively affected, roughly 275,000 [250] full-time manufacturing jobs have been replaced by robots in the period 1994-2014.

          1.6.5.5.2.3. Con:
          Though we can't predict it with complete accuracy, there are observable patterns to follow. Not only can we vaguely see where technology is headed, we can observe the attitudes of various employers to new development to determine how employment is likely to rise or fall.

        1.6.5.5.3. Pro:
        Automation and the increased use of technology affects [251] the whole socio-economic spectrum from low to higher-income professions.

          1.6.5.5.3.1. Pro:
          Many jobs [252] can already be automated by adapting current technologies, with many more on the horizon [253].

            1.6.5.5.3.1.1. Pro:
            Automation will reduce [254] customer-facing job [255] opportunities.

            1.6.5.5.3.1.2. Pro:
            Automation and AI [256] are already replacing [257] creators in entertainment [258] industries.

            1.6.5.5.3.1.3. Pro:
            AI is beginning to replace specialists in the medical [259] and financial [260] sectors.

            1.6.5.5.3.1.4. Con:
            We are very far from a world without need for human labor [261]. There are many problems that can only be addressed by humans in healthcare, childcare, eldercare, cleaning the environment, etc.

          1.6.5.5.3.2. Con:
          Policy acceptance is more dependent on perceived vulnerability than actual vulnerability. Insofar as most higher-income professionals do not view themselves at risk, they are therefore still unlikely to support a policy that they see as irrelevant to their interests.

          1.6.5.5.3.3. Con:
          Executive-level jobs tend to rely on more transferable general management and administrative skills. It may be true that single-industry workers are most at risk of obsolescence if their factory closes down whereas professionals, managers, executives and technical occupations have comparatively less trouble transferring to a laterally-related field.

      1.6.5.6. Con:
      Companies could use a UBI as a justification to offer lower wages to their workers.

        1.6.5.6.1. Pro:
        Companies are constrained in the current environment by people needing enough wages to, at the least, survive. If a UBI provides this level of subsistence instead then employment wages act as a marginal top up and can be offered at lower rates.

        1.6.5.6.2. Con:
        If high enough, a UBI could allow the removal of minimum wages since they provide sufficient bargaining power to prevent exploitation.

          1.6.5.6.2.1. Pro:
          Removing the minimum wage would allow companies to hire lower-skilled, less experienced, or less productive workers at appropriate rates.

            1.6.5.6.2.1.1. Pro:
            Without a minimum wage, companies should be able to [262] hire more people.

            1.6.5.6.2.1.2. Con:
            This could bring down the wages of skilled workers as well.

          1.6.5.6.2.2. Pro:
          Without a minimum wage, prices would generally go down on products made in that country, which could help the economy.

        1.6.5.6.3. Con:
        Companies will still be restricted by their respective countries laws with respect to minimum wage.

        1.6.5.6.4. Con:
        Employers would be forced to increase wages for low-wage jobs, since many low-wage workers would drop out of the labor force if a UBI were enacted.

          1.6.5.6.4.1. Pro:
          The law of supply and demand [263] applies to labor just like it does most things that are bought and paid for. The more an employer is willing to pay for an hour of somebody's time, the more persons will be willing to accept the employment.
