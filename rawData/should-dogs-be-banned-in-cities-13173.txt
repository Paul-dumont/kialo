Discussion Name:
Should Dogs Be Banned In Cities?

1. Thesis:
Owning dogs should be banned in cities.

  1.1. Pro:
  Dogs pose significant threats to the physical health of city dwellers.

    1.1.1. Pro:
    Some people are allergic to dogs [1].

      1.1.1.1. Pro:
      Those who are allergic to dogs are actually allergic to their dander [2], which can circulate in the air or adhere to surfaces like furniture. This makes non-owners pay the price for others owning dogs.

        1.1.1.1.1. Con:
        Human beings are allergic to wide range of substances, ranging from peanuts [3] to pollen [4] to mold in old buildings [5]. Yet, this is not cited as a reason to ban them.

        1.1.1.1.2. Pro:
        Dog allergens [6] exist in all houses, regardless of whether the family owns a dog. This effectively ensures that those with dog allergies are left with no space where they can be free from allergens.

      1.1.1.2. Con:
      Dogs can help human beings develop their immunity [7] by exposing them to the bacteria they naturally carry.

      1.1.1.3. Con:
      Those allergic to dogs should limit their movement and activity to spaces where dogs are prohibited.

      1.1.1.4. Con:
      In the same way that other allergies are alleviated, those allergic to dogs can take standard allergy medication [8].

      1.1.1.5. Pro:
      Allergic reactions to dogs [9] are more severe than allergic reactions to other pets like cats.

    1.1.2. Pro:
    Dog feces contain disease-causing parasites and pathogens [10] like bacteria, which can cause serious illnesses in humans.

      1.1.2.1. Con:
      There are many source of bacteria in urban environments, including human feces [11]. Yet, this is not considered a reasonable justification to ban such sources of bacteria from cities.

        1.1.2.1.1. Pro:
        Instead of banning human beings from cities, efforts are being taken to build more toilets in places where open defecation [12] is common. Similarly, urban infrastructure should designed to accommodate the needs of dogs and dog owners.

        1.1.2.1.2. Con:
        Cities are equipped with adequate human waste management systems. This is not the case with animal waste [13]; unlike human beings, pets do not generate tax, so governments would have little incentive to invest in accommodating their needs.

      1.1.2.2. Pro:
      Dogs often relieve themselves in public spaces, which increases the risk of direct human exposure to their excreta, which is hazardous [14] to health.

        1.1.2.2.1. Con:
        Responsible dog owners clean up after their dogs [15]. For others, fines will ensure that they clean up after their dogs.

          1.1.2.2.1.1. Con:
          Enforcing a fine requires deploying police or other guards [16] at every street corner to ensure that rule breakers are caught and fined. This is an unnecessary expenses that can simply be avoided by banning dog ownership.

          1.1.2.2.1.2. Con:
          Fines are unlikely to have a significant impact on the amount of dog feces and urine in public spaces, given that a substantial proportion of dogs [17] in cities are strays.

          1.1.2.2.1.3. Pro:
          A vast majority [18] of dog owners clean up after their dogs. This behavior only needs to be reinforced with the threat of fines.

          1.1.2.2.1.4. Pro:
          Dog owners are most likely to clean up after their dogs if they are being watched [19]. Thus, this kind of behavior can be extinguished by limiting dog walking to public places with good footfall or installing CCTV cameras.

          1.1.2.2.1.5. Con:
          Cleaning up after a dog requires collecting the feces in a plastic cover. This essentially results in landfills of plastic-covered feces [20] that exacerbate the plastic and waste crisis.

          1.1.2.2.1.6. Pro:
          Some cities, such as New York City [21], impose a fine of $250 for not cleaning up after one's dog(s).

            1.1.2.2.1.6.1. Con:
            Public officials interested in keeping a city free of dog waste may unfairly impose fines. This is what happened in San Francisco [22], when the very person who was victimized by dog feces was sent a warning notice threatening a fine.

            1.1.2.2.1.6.2. Pro:
            Countries are instituting robust technology to ensure that fines are effectively enforced. Israel, for example, uses a DNA database [23] to test dog feces and track the offending owners.

            1.1.2.2.1.6.3. Con:
            Not all cities [24] have instituted or enforced fines. In such cities, irresponsible dog owners are likely to leave dog excrement on the streets.

        1.1.2.2.2. Pro:
        Dog excreta that remains uncleaned are fertile breeding grounds for parasites to multiply and infect [25] other living things, including human beings.

      1.1.2.3. Pro:
      Dogs are responsible for a significant proportion of bacteria from animal waste that contaminates waterways and pollutes air [26], thereby posing threats to public health.

        1.1.2.3.1. Pro:
        Dog waste can find its way into drinking water supply reservoirs [27], spreading diseases within the community, as many water treatment facilities are not equipped to remove harmful contaminants and parasites found in dog waste.

    1.1.3. Con:
    To function independently and maintain a good quality of life, individuals with disabilities need service or assistance dogs [28], which are allowed [29] in all spaces.

      1.1.3.1. Pro:
      Dogs are the most popular and capable [30] service dogs. Banning their ownership in cities would require those with disabilities to depend on less-capable animals like cats or impractical animals like horses and pigs.

      1.1.3.2. Con:
      Therapeutic benefits are often sufficient grounds to make legal exceptions (e.g., medical marijuana [31]). Therefore, it is possible to ban owning dogs as pets, while allowing those with health conditions to own dogs that serve a therapeutic purpose.

      1.1.3.3. Pro:
      Assistance dogs are often needed to save the lives of individuals with life-threatening conditions [32] like seizures or diabetes, that require healthcare professionals to be alerted when medical emergencies occur.

    1.1.4. Pro:
    Dogs transmit rabies [33] - a life-threatening illness which is considered one of the worst ways to die and for which there is no treatment or cure [34].

      1.1.4.1. Con:
      Banning dog ownership would have no impact on the prevalence of rabies transmitted by dogs.

        1.1.4.1.1. Pro:
        Most governments require dog owners to vaccinate their dogs against rabies [35].

        1.1.4.1.2. Pro:
        Rabies is commonly transmitted by unvaccinated stray or feral dogs [36]. As these dogs have no owners, banning dog ownership would have no impact on rabies transmission.

        1.1.4.1.3. Con:
        Following a ban on dog ownership, governments may vaccinate and sterilize [37] stray dogs to ensure that, over time, a city is entirely free of dogs, thereby reducing the prevalence of rabies.

      1.1.4.2. Pro:
      Rabies transmission and mortality are overwhelmingly attributable to dogs rather than to any other city-dwelling animals. Therefore, banning dog ownership would effectively reduce prevalence rates to less than 1% [38].

    1.1.5. Con:
    Dogs need to be taken outside for walks. As a result, dog owners spend more time walking, running, and jogging [39], thereby increasing their physical activity levels [40].

    1.1.6. Con:
    Many dog owners sleep alongside their dog - a practice that is associated with improved sleep [41].

      1.1.6.1. Pro:
      Making infants sleep alongside dogs [42] decreases their likelihood of developing allergies to dogs later in life.

      1.1.6.2. Con:
      Hyperactivity, restlessness, barking through the night are only a few examples of dog behaviors [43] that commonly disrupt sleep among dog owners.

  1.2. Pro:
  Cities are not well-suited to the needs of dogs.

    1.2.1. Pro:
    Cities are inherently limiting to dogs because they are designed keeping in mind the needs of human beings [44].

      1.2.1.1. Pro:
      Cities limit the amount of space available for dogs to roam or run around outdoors. This is essential for their physical and emotional well-being [45].

        1.2.1.1.1. Con:
        Dogs have been bred to live alongside humans [46] in many different environments, including spaces where freedom to roam is limited, such as cities.

        1.2.1.1.2. Con:
        Many dog breeds do not need a lot of physical activity. Indeed, some breeds like bulldogs can suffer from exhaustion [47], when they spend a lot of time outdoors in warm regions.

        1.2.1.1.3. Pro:
        Dogs have a basic need for physical activity [48], which is stifled by urban layouts. This is demonstrated by the excitement dogs typically exhibit when their owner asks them if they would like to go for a walk.

        1.2.1.1.4. Pro:
        In many cities, the law requires dogs to be leashed in public [49]. This is cruel and limits their freedom to roam, run around, and explore their surroundings.

        1.2.1.1.5. Pro:
        Dogs are often prohibited from entering public places like beaches [50] and parks [51], where they could run free for long distances. This is a product of human selfishness that limits natural animal activity for human gain.

      1.2.1.2. Con:
      Dogs gratify many basic human needs, such as companionship and touch [52]. Therefore, truly human-centered urban planning would make accommodations for dogs.

      1.2.1.3. Con:
      Cities are inherently limiting to a vast variety of species [53]. Therefore, if this were a valid reason to ban dog ownership, ownership of all affected animals would need to be prohibited.

        1.2.1.3.1. Pro:
        Urban planning often erases the inherent sustainability of the biosphere, from which cities are created. Nature-positive planning [54] can help urban planners design cities that accommodate both human and animal life.

        1.2.1.3.2. Con:
        Species that endanger (e.g., snakes [55]) or are endangered [56] by human beings are often removed from spaces where they would likely interact with people. The same principle can be extended to dogs.

      1.2.1.4. Pro:
      Cities are designed by human beings for human beings using tax generated [57] by human beings. Since dogs do not share this distinct status, there is no incentive to redesign cities to cater to the needs of dogs.

    1.2.2. Pro:
    Dogs living in cities tend to be more anxious and fearful [58] than those living in rural areas.

      1.2.2.1. Con:
      Mental health problems in dogs can be alleviated with the professional help of animal behaviorists [59]. Such facilities are more likely to be available in urban rather than rural environments.

      1.2.2.2. Pro:
      Increased proximity [60] to other dogs and human beings causes dogs living in cities to have staggeringly high levels of fearfulness than their counterparts living in less-urbanized environments.

      1.2.2.3. Con:
      A healthy level of fear and caution is adaptive in dogs. Leash-free roaming and a lack of fear of predators render dogs in the countryside more vulnerable [61] to lethal accidents, trauma, and injury.

    1.2.3. Con:
    Stray dogs [62] exist in many cities around the world. Banning dog ownership in cities would mean that fewer people are able to adopt these dogs to give them a safe and loving home, where their needs can be met.

    1.2.4. Pro:
    Urban living [63] fosters selfishness [64] in human beings - a quality that renders them incapable of being a good dog parent.

      1.2.4.1. Pro:
      City dwellers lead busy lifestyles [65] that do not allow them to take good care of their dogs, because of which they may be neglected, abandoned, or euthanized [66].

      1.2.4.2. Pro:
      Many people "gift" their family members puppies on special occasions like Christmas, only to return them to shelters [67] soon after the excitement of owning a puppy is dampened by the consequent cost and responsibility.

      1.2.4.3. Con:
      The negative impact of urban lifestyles on selfishness should render city dwellers unfit to be a parent to any animal or child. To restrict this to dog ownership is disingenuous.

      1.2.4.4. Con:
      Prior to adoption or purchase, potential dog owners should be required to undergo eligibility screening to ensure that only those well informed and equipped about the responsibilities of dog ownership are allowed to own a dog.

    1.2.5. Con:
    Due to centuries of domestication [68], modern pet dogs are highly reliant on human beings and cannot survive in the wild. As such, they are safer and better off living in cities.

    1.2.6. Con:
    Living in the countryside poses greater risks to dogs than urban living.

      1.2.6.1. Pro:
      Dogs living in the countryside often do not receive the medical care they need because owners often have to drive to cities [69] to receive veterinary assistance.

      1.2.6.2. Pro:
      Dogs are more exposed to harmful substances [70] like chemicals and parasites in the countryside; as owners in such places see their dogs less commonly than their urban counterparts, they may not be able to immediate action.

      1.2.6.3. Con:
      Banning dog ownership in the countryside may increase dog ownership in rural areas, necessitating an increase in dog-friendly policies and facilities, such as veterinary care.

  1.3. Con:
  Dogs enhance the mental well-being of city dwellers.

    1.3.1. Con:
    The existence of people who are able to live happily in cities without dogs is proof that dog ownership is not a necessary precondition for well-being.

    1.3.2. Pro:
    Dogs gratify the need for companionship [71] and reduce loneliness, which is a growing problem [72] in urban environments [73].

      1.3.2.1. Con:
      People should alleviate their loneliness by creating and maintaining relationships with other human beings, not by owning dogs.

        1.3.2.1.1. Con:
        Many people do not have [74] friends or family, and unique life circumstances (e.g., pandemic restrictions) may limit them from forming new relationships. In such cases, a dog can alleviate their loneliness.

        1.3.2.1.2. Pro:
        Unlike human beings, dogs cannot understand or produce speech. Given that loneliness is often defined by a feeling of having no one to talk to [75], it is essential for people to prioritize human relationships.

      1.3.2.2. Pro:
      Dogs help their owners forge new and positive connections [76] with strangers.

        1.3.2.2.1. Pro:
        Among other things, dogs are excellent ice breakers, and the social barriers [77] that reduce the attention people direct toward strangers cease to exist when the owner has a dog.

        1.3.2.2.2. Pro:
        In person and online, men and women who visibly have a dog are more likely to be successful [78] in their romantic pursuits (e.g., get phone number, swipes) than those without a dog.

        1.3.2.2.3. Con:
        Knowledge about the impact dogs have on strangers can be misused by miscreants. Specifically, they may own a dog simply to easily earn the trust of strangers, so that they can con them.

      1.3.2.3. Pro:
      Children who grow up with a pet animal like a family dog [79] have better social skills, are less lonely, and receive more social support, all of which contribute to health development.

      1.3.2.4. Con:
      The benefits of pet ownership are not exclusive to dogs. Owning other domesticated animals [80], such as cats, can also contribute to mental well-being.

    1.3.3. Con:
    Dogs may enhance mental well-being only in their owners; in others, they may elicit fear and distress, which could worsen their mental health.

      1.3.3.1. Pro:
      Fear of encountering dogs could cause cyanophobic individuals [81] to restrict their movement in public spaces, thereby reducing their physical activity levels [82], which can exacerbate mental health problems.

      1.3.3.2. Pro:
      In the presence of dogs, cyanophobic individuals may panic and make poor or risky decisions [83] that render them vulnerable to injury or harm.

      1.3.3.3. Con:
      Phobias do not warrant a ban on the causal agents. Analogically, banning dogs on an account of cynophobia [84] would be akin to banning open spaces for the benefit of agoraphobics [85].

        1.3.3.3.1. Pro:
        Phobias are irrational and disabling [86]. As such, cynophobia should be addressed through therapeutic interventions, the most effective and common of which is exposure therapy [87].

        1.3.3.3.2. Con:
        Phobias [88] are often the reason why certain groups of individuals [89], characterized by an "other" gender, religion, sexual orientation, or national origin, are banned from certain countries.

      1.3.3.4. Con:
      Direct and indirect exposure to crime takes a toll on mental health. Given that cities have higher crime rates [90] that rural areas, dogs are needed to reduce crime [91] and improve the mental health of urban residents.

      1.3.3.5. Con:
      Fear of dogs is not common. In the US, of the 9% of the population that has a phobic disorder, only a subset has a fear of animals, and only 1 in 3 of this subset [92] of individuals has a fear of dogs.

    1.3.4. Pro:
    Dogs have a therapeutic impact [93] on human beings that can be harnessed to alleviate distress and improve functioning.

      1.3.4.1. Con:
      -> See 1.1.3.2.

      1.3.4.2. Pro:
      Therapy dogs are commonly used in animal therapy [94] to help individuals with mental health problems ranging from autism to post-traumatic stress disorder to promote coping behaviors and recovery.

      1.3.4.3. Con:
      There are more than a dozen different animals, reptiles, and birds [95] that can be effectively used for therapeutic purposes, such as animal therapy.

      1.3.4.4. Pro:
      Emotional support dogs are prescribed by mental health professionals for individuals with disabling mental health problems [96]. Thus, banning dogs would be equivalent to banning psychotropic medication.

      1.3.4.5. Pro:
      Dogs elevate move wherever they go. For this reason, various institutions including schools [97] and offices [98] are increasing the presence of dogs in their workplace to improve overall psychological functioning.

      1.3.4.6. Con:
      Using dogs for human benefit is a form of animal exploitation. Banning dogs in cities would put an end to such inhumane practices.

        1.3.4.6.1. Con:
        Pre-post comparisons of physiological markers [99] in therapy dogs have found no significant increase in aversive emotional experiences in them, implying that such use is not a form of exploitation.

        1.3.4.6.2. Pro:
        In residential facilities, therapy dogs may be on duty throughout the day. Working around the clock with little to no time for rest can lead to fatigue and burnout in these dogs (p. 5 [100]).

        1.3.4.6.3. Pro:
        Problems inherent (e.g., allowing strangers to touch and pet the dog) and contextual (e.g., working in small enclosed spaces with limited access to necessities like water) to dog-assisted therapy render this practice unethical (p. 7 [101]).

  1.4. Con:
  Banning problematic dog behaviors is sufficient; there is no need to ban dog ownership in general.

    1.4.1. Pro:
    Certain dog breeds have a reputation for exhibiting problematic behaviors. These breeds can be selectively banned.

      1.4.1.1. Con:
      Breed-specific bans are discriminatory.

        1.4.1.1.1. Pro:
        Breed-specific bans are unfair because they punish an entire breed for the actions of a few unruly dogs with presumably irresponsible owners.

        1.4.1.1.2. Pro:
        Banning certain breeds of dogs can make it very difficult for owners of such dogs to find suitable accommodation [102]. This is a form of discrimination.

        1.4.1.1.3. Con:
        As the term "human rights [103]" suggests, the right to freedom from discrimination is specific to human beings and does not extend to the animal kingdom. This is why human beings can own dogs, but not other human beings.

        1.4.1.1.4. Con:
        Animal ownership is already discriminatory in that human beings are allowed to own only domesticated animals, not wild animals [104], which pose threats to other human beings. This same principle is applicable to dangerous dog breeds.

        1.4.1.1.5. Pro:
        Bans on dogs or specific dog breeds exemplifies speciesism [105], wherein human beings believe that they have the right to make decisions about the existence of other species.

      1.4.1.2. Pro:
      Dog breeds [106] that are likely to lead to conflicts with other people, such as neighbors, should be avoided. Instead, indoor dog breads [107], which have limited impact on those outside the family, should be preferred.

        1.4.1.2.1. Pro:
        Barking is a common source of annoyance and conflict with neighbors [108]. For this reason, dogs that do not bark [109] (e.g., basenji [110]) should be preferred over those with a reputation for barking excessively [111].

        1.4.1.2.2. Con:
        Conflict with neighbors is a common problem [112] that can be attributed to any number of factors. The appropriate solution, therefore, is to create more tolerant neighborhoods and promote peaceful conflict resolution rather than ban all potential sources of annoyance and conflict.

      1.4.1.3. Con:
      Different jurisdictions ban different dog breeds [113]. This implies that there is no consensus over which dog breeds are problematic enough to be banned from all cities.

      1.4.1.4. Pro:
      Many dog breeds [114] have been banned from cities due to occurrences of reported aggression.

        1.4.1.4.1. Pro:
        Many aggressive dog breeds are also difficult to train [115], especially without professional assistance. Therefore, banning such dog breeds may be the only effective way to prevent their aggressive behaviors.

        1.4.1.4.2. Pro:
        Dog breeds associated with aggression are also often mutilated to look more aggressive. Banning such breeds would put an end to such practices.

          1.4.1.4.2.1. Pro:
          Ear cropping and tail docking [116] are common procedures through which aggressive dog breeds (e.g., Doberman Pinscher) are subjected to enhance their perceived aggressiveness. This is a cruel practice that should be put to an end.

          1.4.1.4.2.2. Con:
          There is no reason why a dog breed needs to be banned to ban a cruel practice inflicted upon the respective breed. It would suffice to simply ban ear cropping and tail docking.

          1.4.1.4.2.3. Pro:
          Tail docking actually ends up making dogs more aggressive. Specifically, dogs express their emotions by wagging their tails; tail docking deprives them of such an outlet, leading to increased aggressiveness [117].

        1.4.1.4.3. Con:
        Mounting evidence shows that breed is a poor indicator [118] of aggressive behavior, which is learned rather than innate. Consequently, many breed-specific bans [119] are being lifted.

    1.4.2. Pro:
    Owners can extinguish problematic behaviors in their dogs through training, thereby making a blanket ban unnecessary.

      1.4.2.1. Con:
      Some problematic dog behaviors are innate and, therefore, cannot be eliminated even with training (e.g., barking [120]). Therefore, all dogs should be banned.

      1.4.2.2. Con:
      Dog training may only reduce rather than eliminate problematic behaviors, some of which can be spontaneous [121] or idiopathic [122]. For this reason, it would be more effective to ban all dogs rather than train them to be well-behaved.

      1.4.2.3. Pro:
      Dog training should begin as early as possible [123]. This will ensure that desired behaviors are solidified and problematic behaviors are eliminated by the time puppies reach adulthood.

      1.4.2.4. Pro:
      Dogs are highly trainable, the only exceptions being those with specific disorders [124]. To opt to ban dogs rather than train them would be tantamount to wasting their unique potential [125] to listen, understand, and respond to human beings.

      1.4.2.5. Pro:
      Ongoing training [126] is an excellent means by which owners can strengthen their bond [127] with their dog. In addition to fostering their emotional bond, such experiences enhance the effectiveness of training.

      1.4.2.6. Con:
      Owners cannot be trusted to train their dogs to be well-behaved.

        1.4.2.6.1. Pro:
        Training a dog requires skill, discipline, and commitment [128]; not all dog owners can be trusted to invest time, money, and energy in training their dogs.

          1.4.2.6.1.1. Pro:
          Dog owners who lack the ability to train their dogs should ideally seek professional help, yet only one in four dogs [129] receives professional training.

          1.4.2.6.1.2. Con:
          By this logic, bad parenting behaviors [130] warrant a ban on having children, yet this is not the case. Instead, the appropriate solution is to promote appropriate parenting techniques; the same principle should apply for dog training.

        1.4.2.6.2. Pro:
        People often over-estimate [131] how well-trained their dogs are, compared to how others perceive them. This can cause owners to take undue risks around others.

        1.4.2.6.3. Con:
        Instead of implementing a ban, the government can invest the same resources into instituting mandatory training classes for all dogs and dog owners as well as follow-up check-ins to improve dog behaviors.

    1.4.3. Con:
    Just because dog behaviors are perceived as "problematic" does not mean that they are truly problematic. Instead, this exemplifies the problem of viewing animal behaviors from a human-centric lens.

      1.4.3.1. Pro:
      Dogs are distressed by loud noises like fireworks, because they perceive them as threats [132]. To counteract against this, many pet owners choose to sedate their pets [133], thereby silencing their very natural and adaptive response to perceived threats.

      1.4.3.2. Con:
      In the wild, perceptions of problematic behavior are enough to trigger an animal [134] to attack others, including human beings. In comparison, human beings are more empathetic and considerate, making cities a better dwelling place for dogs.

      1.4.3.3. Pro:
      Dog owners are frequently embarrassed by their dogs trying to "hump" their legs or other objects. Contrary to popular belief, this is not a sexual act [135] but a means why which dogs try to get attention, release pent up energy, or play with their owners.

  1.5. Pro:
  Due to their aggressive nature, dogs are dangerous.

    1.5.1. Pro:
    Aggressive dog behaviors can cost owners financially [136], as they render them liable to civil and criminal lawsuits filed by the victim of a bite or attack.

      1.5.1.1. Pro:
      Dog bites cost insurance companies more than 1 billion USD due to liability claims (p. 1 [137]). This figure is likely to be higher when the costs incurred by those without insurance are included.

      1.5.1.2. Con:
      Liability for damage [138] and injury are not specific to pet ownership. Parents are also responsible for such harms caused by their children, yet this is not cited as a reason to ban children from cities.

    1.5.2. Pro:
    Dogs bite, disfigure, and even kill [139] people.

      1.5.2.1. Con:
      Fatal dog attacks are very rare. In the US, for instance, only 30 to 50 people [140] die from dog bites every year, most of which are likely attributable to other infections such as rabies.

      1.5.2.2. Con:
      The risk of injury or death posed by dogs is significantly lower [141] than that posed by other amenities like cars [142], which are not banned in cities.

      1.5.2.3. Pro:
      There have been instances of dogs mauling, killing, and eating [143] the flesh of their dead owners with whom they shared a strong and affectionate relationship; dog experts often have no explanation for why this happens.

      1.5.2.4. Pro:
      The high prevalence of dog attacks significantly strains an already overstretched healthcare infrastructure.

        1.5.2.4.1. Pro:
        According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, approximately 4.5 million dog bites [144] occur in the US each year.

        1.5.2.4.2. Con:
        Dog-inflicted injuries account for a very minuscule percentage (0.3% to 1.1% [145]) of all visits to the emergency department.

        1.5.2.4.3. Con:
        Most dog bites do not warrant a visit to a healthcare facility.

          1.5.2.4.3.1. Pro:
          Pet dogs are often required to be vaccinated [146], and most dog attacks cause no injury [147]. Therefore, most dog bites can be easily treated at home [148], in the same way that any other wound would be managed.

          1.5.2.4.3.2. Con:
          The average person may not be knowledgeable about the relevant facts to determine whether a visit to a healthcare facility is necessary. Therefore, to err on the side of caution, they must always seek professional help.

            1.5.2.4.3.2.1. Pro:
            Dog bites necessitate following a strict protocol, which includes catching, isolating, and monitoring the dog that has bitten a person (p. 1 [149]). This requires the intervention of government officials.

            1.5.2.4.3.2.2. Con:
            "Facts" can exacerbate anxieties in the public. For example, it is a fact that rabies is spread not just through bites, but also scratches, licks, and aerosol droplets; awareness of such facts could increase unnecessary visits to healthcare centers (p. 1 [150]).

        1.5.2.4.4. Pro:
        The number of people who seek treatment for dog bites each year is only a little shy of a million [151].

        1.5.2.4.5. Pro:
        The healthcare cost of treating dog bites that require emergency care is around one million USD per year (p. 1 [152]).

      1.5.2.5. Con:
      Dog bites and attacks can be prevented through human intervention (p. 1 [153]).

        1.5.2.5.1. Pro:
        Aggressive dogs can be made to wear muzzles [154], when in the presence of other people.

        1.5.2.5.2. Pro:
        Biting and attacking are learned behaviors; therefore, dogs can be trained to unlearn these behaviors [155], such as by teaching them to lie down or stay on command.

        1.5.2.5.3. Con:
        Expert advice on preventing dog bites and attacks - such as through reading dog behaviors accurately and avoiding triggering situations - have been proven to be ineffective [156].

        1.5.2.5.4. Pro:
        The risk and prevalence of dog bites and attacks can be reduced by preventing dog suppliers [157] from providing aggressive biting-prone dogs to those looking for a puppy or dog.

        1.5.2.5.5. Con:
        Dog personalities often match owner personalities. Therefore, disagreeable owners [158] of aggressive dogs are less likely to perceive such behavior as problematic and intervene.

    1.5.3. Con:
    Aggression is not inherent to canine nature but a product of their environments.

      1.5.3.1. Pro:
      Aggression in dogs is often a product of training methods that employ punishment and aversive methods [159]. This can be reduced by promoting "science-based, force-free positive training methods."

      1.5.3.2. Pro:
      Dogs often become aggressive in response to triggers [160]. By identifying and avoiding these triggers, dog owners can reduce aggressive behaviors in their dogs.

        1.5.3.2.1. Pro:
        The Yellow Dog Project [161] aims to reduce aggressive dog-human interactions; specifically, a yellow ribbon, collar, or leash is used to inform others that the dog tends to be anxious around strangers and should preferably be left alone.

        1.5.3.2.2. Con:
        Identifying and eliminating all possible triggers is impractical. Often, owners are agnostic to what triggers aggressive behavior [162] in their dogs, as evidenced in the case of Bethany Lynn Stephens, who was mauled to death by her dogs.

      1.5.3.3. Con:
      Environment and genetics do not operate in isolation. Dogs have been specifically bred to inherit qualities [163] that are beneficial to human beings, including aggressiveness.

    1.5.4. Con:
    Aggression in dogs does not necessarily make them dangerous to human beings. Instead, they can make cities safer for human beings.

      1.5.4.1. Pro:
      Houses with dogs are less likely to be robbed [164] than those without dogs. Barking not only alerts their owners of suspicious activity but also threatens potential intruders, who often target the most easily accessible houses.

      1.5.4.2. Pro:
      Guard dogs, protection dogs, and police dogs are only a few categories of dogs that work hard [165] to keep city dwellers safe from potential criminals.

        1.5.4.2.1. Pro:
        Police dogs are protected under the Federal Law Enforcement Animal Protection Act [166], as they are recognized to serve communities like human officers.

        1.5.4.2.2. Con:
        The effectiveness of guard dogs is often exaggerated [167]. Burglars self-reportedly know how to silence a dog, such as by being "good with dogs" or using tranquilizers.

      1.5.4.3. Con:
      Training dogs as security dogs is an extremely expensive undertaking [168]. Given their short lifespan, it may be more prudent to invest the same amount of money in longer-lasting alternatives, such as technological solutions (e.g., cameras, alarms, weapons).

        1.5.4.3.1. Con:
        Cameras, alarms, and weapons serve distinct purposes, namely, monitoring, altering, and attacking intruders. Dogs perform all of these functions.

        1.5.4.3.2. Pro:
        The average dog lives for only around a decade [169] and costs around 3000 USD per year [170], excluding the cost of training. In contrast, CCTV cameras last the same duration for only a fraction of the cost [171].

      1.5.4.4. Con:
      Dogs cannot discern between which people are actual threats, and those who are not.

    1.5.5. Con:
    Like human beings, dogs have different personalities [172]. That some dogs have an aggressive trait does not warrant a ban on all dogs.

  1.6. Con:
  Banning dogs would be unenforceable.

    1.6.1. Pro:
    Dog ownership has a positive impact on the economy. Therefore, governments have few economic incentives to enforce such a ban.

      1.6.1.1. Pro:
      Dog ownership restrictions influence where people choose to live. Therefore, dog bans may increase migration to rural areas and limit migration to urban areas.

        1.6.1.1.1. Con:
        The migration of ardent dog lovers to rural areas may be counterbalanced by vociferous dog haters moving to urban areas. Therefore, the net impact on population and economics may not be significant.

        1.6.1.1.2. Pro:
        Dog owners commonly look for dog-friendly accommodation [173], when looking for dwelling places. A ban on dog ownership in cities would coerce such individuals to move to rural areas.

      1.6.1.2. Con:
      Dog food production [174] increases greenhouse emissions. These are bad for the economy, especially because the net environmental impact is not offset by any consequent economic benefits (e.g., goods or services).

      1.6.1.3. Con:
      The positive impact of pet ownership on the economy is not specific to dogs. The same economic benefits can be reaped regardless of which pet is owned.

        1.6.1.3.1. Pro:
        In the US, the pet industry yields a net economic impact of 222 billion USD [175]. Banning dogs would likely cause owners to own other animals; therefore, this figure is unlikely to remain unchanged.

        1.6.1.3.2. Con:
        Dogs are the most popular pets [176] worldwide. Many individuals who like only dogs as pets would likely not replace their dogs with other pets, leading to a significant reduction in the economic output of the pet industry.

      1.6.1.4. Pro:
      Dogs are wildly popular on social media; consequently, dog influencers [177] earn tens of thousands of US dollars for a single post. This is revenue that would be lost, if a dog ban were to be implemented.

    1.6.2. Pro:
    Dog ownership is a personal and private decision. Therefore, a ban would be an unnecessary infringement on individual liberties.

      1.6.2.1. Pro:
      Dogs live on private properties. As such, any restrictions on dog ownership within such properties should be imposed only by the land owner or similar authority.

      1.6.2.2. Con:
      Dog ownership is not merely a private undertaking because the noise created by barking dogs affects everyone in the surrounding areas.

        1.6.2.2.1. Con:
        -> See 1.4.1.2.1.

        1.6.2.2.2. Pro:
        Dog barks can keep people up at night [178]. Consequently, sleep deprivation or poor sleep can impact their health and next-day functioning [179] at home, school, or work.

        1.6.2.2.3. Con:
        Excessive dog barking is not normal [180], but often symptomatic of underlying problems such as separation anxiety [181]. This can be reduced through training [182].

        1.6.2.2.4. Pro:
        Dogs are a particularly problematic source of noise pollution [183].

          1.6.2.2.4.1. Con:
          In cities, there are many sources of noise pollution [184] ranging from traffic to home appliances to crying infants to construction work. Dogs are nowhere as noisy.

            1.6.2.2.4.1.1. Pro:
            Many of the common sources of noise pollution are artificial human-made products (e.g., home appliances). Dog barks cannot be categorized along with these, as they are the sounds of nature, not unlike birds singing.

            1.6.2.2.4.1.2. Con:
            Many sources of loud noise such as car horns [185] and loudspeakers [186] are banned or restricted in many parts of the world.

          1.6.2.2.4.2. Pro:
          Dogs have evolved to bark in such a way that the sounds they emit draw human attention [187]. Consequently, dog barking is more annoying than other noises and can make attention-focusing particularly challenging.

          1.6.2.2.4.3. Con:
          Excessive dog barking is already prohibited in many cities, states, and countries, so noise pollution caused by dogs is already restricted by law [188].

        1.6.2.2.5. Pro:
        Dog barks [189] often trigger other dogs to start barking [190]. The cacophonous sound of many dogs barking together can be troubling and even intimidating.

      1.6.2.3. Pro:
      From a biological perspective, human-dog relationships are comparable to parent-child relationships [191]. Denying city dwellers to the opportunity to own dogs is, therefore, tantamount to denying them the right to have children.

        1.6.2.3.1. Con:
        Dogs and human children are not comparable, in that they neither have the fundamental rights (e.g., right to movement [192]) as human beings nor can fulfill the responsibilities that come with such rights (e.g., paying tax, voting obligations).

        1.6.2.3.2. Pro:
        Millennials are choosing pets such as dogs over babies [193]. As many millennials are opting to never have children [194], a ban on dogs would be an infringement of their right to parenthood.

    1.6.3. Pro:
    Banning dog ownership in cities would require that all pet dogs be accommodated elsewhere. There is no such suitable accommodation.

      1.6.3.1. Con:
      People may continue to discreetly own dogs in their homes. Due to privacy restrictions, no one may find out or be able to prove that they own a dog.

        1.6.3.1.1. Pro:
        Dogs can be trained to use the bathroom [195] or specific products [196] that allow them to relieve themselves indoors, to reduce their chances of being seen by others outside.

        1.6.3.1.2. Pro:
        Bans do not prevent people from owning dogs. Instead, they only motivate owners to try hiding them [197].

          1.6.3.1.2.1. Pro:
          When owners try to hide the fact that they own dogs, they may take their dogs on fewer walks and veterinary visits, all of which are detrimental to their well-being [198].

          1.6.3.1.2.2. Con:
          A ban on dog ownership would effectively ban dog breeding, dog sale, dog adoption, and all products and services catering to dogs (e.g., vaccination centers). This would make it impossible to own and raise a dog.

          1.6.3.1.2.3. Con:
          Keeping dogs hidden at all times, by not taking them outside or not allowing them to bark, would be a violation of their rights [199] and a form of animal cruelty. Owners who commit such acts violate two laws: dog bans and animal cruelty prohibitions.

        1.6.3.1.3. Con:
        Search warrants [200] empower law enforcement officials to search the private residences of those suspected of violating the law, irrespective of owner consent. The same warrants can be issued for violations of dog bans.

      1.6.3.2. Con:
      A ban on dog ownership in cities does not have to be enforced on dogs that are currently owned. Instead, it could be enforced to prohibit new ownership.

      1.6.3.3. Pro:
      There are about half a billion pet dogs [201] worldwide. A ban on dog ownership would necessitate the mass transportation and accommodation or mass killing of these dogs, both of which are problematic.

    1.6.4. Con:
    The practical limitations of enforcing a ban have never precluded the legislature from instituting a ban based on theoretical considerations.

    1.6.5. Con:
    Dogs have already been banned from many countries [202], thereby proving that such a ban is enforceable.

      1.6.5.1. Pro:
      Dogs are typically banned in Muslim-majority countries, such as Saudi Arabia [203], Iran [204], and Malaysia [205], because they are considered "impure."

      1.6.5.2. Con:
      The countries that typically ban dogs are also often the countries that fare poorly on human rights indices [206]. Thus, such bans may be enforceable only in such countries, not in modern democratic nations.

    1.6.6. Con:
    Dogs are often banned from many public spaces such as restaurants. Therefore, it would be possible to enforce a similar ban on a larger scale.

      1.6.6.1. Con:
      There is a difference between limiting access to certain urban spaces and banning the existence of an entire species in cities. This is akin to prohibiting children from certain spaces and banning children as a whole, the latter being untenable.

      1.6.6.2. Con:
      Dog owners, trainers, and activists are challenging bans [207] that prohibit dogs from accessing public spaces on the grounds that they are often a treasured member [208] of the families that own them.

      1.6.6.3. Pro:
      Dogs shed hair [209], which can be a health hazard [210] in public spaces. For example, in restaurants, dog fur and danger can contaminate food during the production or consumption process.

      1.6.6.4. Pro:
      Allowing dogs in public spaces would mean that many dogs are present in a given space at the same time. These dogs may want to play or fight with each other, which can be disruptive to other people.
