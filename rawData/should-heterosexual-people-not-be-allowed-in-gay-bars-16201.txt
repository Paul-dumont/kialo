Discussion Name:
Should heterosexual people not be allowed in gay bars?

1. Thesis:
Heterosexual people should not be allowed in gay bars.

  1.1. Con:
  Denying entry on the basis of sexual orientation would be discriminatory.

    1.1.1. Con:
    Minority groups have a right to ensure that their culture continues to exist. It is wrong and misleading to describe this behavior, when practiced by the LGBTQ+ community, as 'discrimination.'

      1.1.1.1. Pro:
      Reverse discrimination is justified [1], because it is intended to remedy past discrimination against minority groups by denying privileges to dominant majorities.

      1.1.1.2. Con:
      Creating a space exclusively for a minority group reverses the distribution of power such that minorities are now dominant. Thus, it is possible to discriminate against straight people under these circumstances.

    1.1.2. Pro:
    Denying straight-passing people access to gay bars would require assuming their sexual orientation or identity. This could bring more harmful [2] consequences [3] than alleged benefits to the LGBTQ+ community.

      1.1.2.1. Pro:
      Bisexuals often feel erased [4] by the LGTQ+ community; the enforcement of "no heterosexual people" could make this worse, as bisexuals with opposite-gender partners could be turned away from gay bars.

      1.1.2.2. Pro:
      It is important to ensure that closeted queer people are able to interact with their culture; exclusionary bans in gay bars would not allow this.

        1.1.2.2.1. Pro:
        LGBTQ+ individuals who are not socially out may be forced to justify why they attend LGBTQ+ exclusive bars. If these spaces are for everyone, they can feel welcomed and safe.

          1.1.2.2.1.1. Pro:
          Closeted LGBTQ+ individuals could have to justify their presence not only to the bar staff but also friends/family in gay-only clubs. Being able to experience the community will help those who are not ready to out themselves yet.

          1.1.2.2.1.2. Pro:
          If straight people are not allowed into gay bars, then closeted LGBTQ+ people from highly homophobic families will not be able to feign ignorance or curiosity, if found out.

          1.1.2.2.1.3. Con:
          The LGBTQ+ [5] community includes those who are "questioning" their sexuality, so gay bars can ensure that those not yet out feel welcome.

        1.1.2.2.2. Pro:
        Closeted [6] LGBTQ+ people often find support from the community when they attend gay bars and other queer spaces; this may enable them to come out.

        1.1.2.2.3. Con:
        Straight people accompanying LGBTQ+ people to gay bars would presumably be supportive, should their closeted friend come out. If an exclusive door policy was enforced, they could visit alone, as coming out to gain entrance to a gay bar carries no risk.

      1.1.2.3. Con:
      Sexual orientation does not have to be assumed. It might be sufficient to hang a sign that says "LGBTQ+ people only" and expect most patrons to comply.

        1.1.2.3.1. Pro:
        Homophobic people are unlikely to visit gay bars, and allies are likely to respect their policies. Thus, a vast majority of patrons in such spaces are likely to be LGBTQ+.

        1.1.2.3.2. Con:
        The defiant few who do not comply with an explicitly stated LGBTQ+-only policy may be the very people such a policy seeks to exclude.

      1.1.2.4. Con:
      Bars generally hire bouncers [7] to judge who to let in. Making assumptions about sexual orientation or gender identity is no different to other commonly used metrics.

        1.1.2.4.1. Con:
        Asexuals or demisexuals may feel excluded [8] from gay clubs if they are asked to justify their sexual orientation as it compounds the assumption that you have sex to be part of the LGBTQ+ community.

        1.1.2.4.2. Con:
        Transgender [9], non-binary, or intersex individuals may feel uncomfortable being vetted by bouncers to enter a gay bar, as they might have to justify themselves, or have their gender assumed.

        1.1.2.4.3. Pro:
        Gay bars can hire bouncers with strong gaydars [10] to restrict access to cisheterosexuals while still allowing queer patrons into gay bars.

      1.1.2.5. Con:
      The marginal harm to some excluded members of the LGBTQ+ people is far outweighed by the benefits of having a true safe space with no straight people.

    1.1.3. Pro:
    In some countries, privately owned businesses [11] like gay bars can refuse [12] service on the basis of conduct, but not on the basis of protected characteristics. Taking such action opens them up to lawsuits and other repercussions.

      1.1.3.1. Pro:
      In the UK [13] , Australia [14], and Canada [15], it is illegal to deny someone service based on their sexual orientation when providing goods, facilities and services. A bar that caters only to LGBTQ+ people would, therefore, be illegal.

      1.1.3.2. Con:
      In some European countries, legislation against discrimination based on sexual orientation does not extend to access to goods and services (p. 1 [16]).

    1.1.4. Pro:
    The LGBTQ+ community advocates against discrimination based on sexual orientation, so denying straight people access to a public space contradicts this principle.

      1.1.4.1. Con:
      Having some gay-only bars is not the same as the entire movement alienating straight people. The movement can still be inclusive yet have some exclusive safe spaces.

      1.1.4.2. Con:
      While a ban on heterosexuals would be classed as discrimination if all sexualities were equal, in full context, a ban would provide equity [17] by countering a larger systemic inequality against the LGBTQ+ community.

      1.1.4.3. Pro:
      To achieve acceptance of LGBTQ+ people in mainstream culture, both cultures have to blend together. Banning straight people from gay bars to preserve queer culture is thus a regressive step away from acceptance.

    1.1.5. Con:
    LGBTQ+ bar owners should feel empowered to decide who they admit, so that they can take into account the specific needs of their customers and the community they serve.

      1.1.5.1. Pro:
      Bars catering specifically to gay women are on the decline [18]. This often forces them to go to other gay bars on "lesbian night [19]" to meet women, which would not be possible if the bar owners were forced to let in everyone.

      1.1.5.2. Pro:
      All venues, including gay bars, should be legally allowed to discriminate against whomever they like. Once these policies are communicated, communities can decide whether or not to patronize these places.

        1.1.5.2.1. Con:
        As heterosexual communities have more power [20] in society, allowing them to discriminate against LGBTQ+ people is likely to be harmful.

        1.1.5.2.2. Pro:
        By allowing consumers to choose the bar they patronise on the basis of more information [21], the market can better meet people's needs and encourage competition to meet them.

      1.1.5.3. Con:
      Bars don't have a right to know the sexual orientation of their patrons.

  1.2. Pro:
  As long as being LGBTQ+ is not as commonly [22] accepted as being cisgender and heterosexual, safe and autonomous spaces are needed [23].

    1.2.1. Con:
    In the US, even the most conservative states have spaces and events exclusively [24] for the LGBTQ+ community. Given that clear advancements in acceptance have occurred, gay bars are no longer necessary.

      1.2.1.1. Pro:
      Gay bars are decreasing in number because of the increased acceptance [25] of queerness. As society becomes more tolerant, many young gay people do not feel the need to congregate.

        1.2.1.1.1. Pro:
        Gay culture was built [26] on the tension between sexual self-expression and social repression. As that tension has eased, it’s no surprise that gay bars have shuttered.

        1.2.1.1.2. Con:
        While white, cisgender gay men may feel safe in straight bars, society is still not accepting of those belonging to more marginalized groups [27].

      1.2.1.2. Pro:
      The vast majority of cohabiting gay men and lesbians now live in neighbourhoods where gay and straight people increasingly live side by side [28], demonstrating the reduced need for LGBTQ+ exclusive spaces.

      1.2.1.3. Con:
      Gay bars can exist solely because the community wants an exclusive space to congregate and interact.

        1.2.1.3.1. Con:
        Men's clubs [29] have existed for a very long time, yet, in recent times, society seems to be demanding their demise as they exclude women. The idea that clubs can be established based on one's gender identity is a double standard.

          1.2.1.3.1.1. Pro:
          Women-only spaces exist and are deemed necessary [30] by many. Creating protected spaces for some groups of people but not others is discriminatory.

          1.2.1.3.1.2. Con:
          There is a difference between oppressors creating exclusive spaces to dehumanize their victims (e.g., locker-room talk [31]), and victims creating safe spaces where they can recover from their dehumanizing oppression.

        1.2.1.3.2. Pro:
        Other exclusive spaces are not questioned for existing. For example, places of worship remain uncritiqued though they exclude people of other faiths or certain demographic groups.

      1.2.1.4. Con:
      In the US, many major cities [32] have only one lesbian bar, many of which are shutting down, or at risk of closure.

      1.2.1.5. Con:
      Conservative American states reporting that they have LGBTQ+ spaces and events [33] does not prove that exclusive spaces are now unnecessary. Their marginal increase in visibility does not undermine the general homophobic tenor [34] of their geography.

    1.2.2. Pro:
    Compared to heterosexual and cisgender people, LGBTQ+ people have fewer safe spaces, with that number rapidly declining [35]. Removing gay bars might make them feel as though their identities are being erased [36].

      1.2.2.1. Con:
      In the US, only 5.6% of adults [37] identify as LGBTQ+. Therefore, the small number of LGBTQ+ spaces relative to general spaces reflects this disparity.

      1.2.2.2. Pro:
      LGBTQ+ individuals from states [38] with homophobic or transphobic laws or norms have a greater need for places where they can socialize, as they are unable to interact with each other as freely as heterosexual individuals.

      1.2.2.3. Pro:
      There are certain conversations that are difficult for LGBTQ+ individuals [39] to have if straight people are present. These include, but are not limited to: sharing stories about coming out, discussing how to provide community support, and discussing experiences of homophobia.

        1.2.2.3.1. Pro:
        LGBTQ+ individuals may self-censor when talking about important queer topics [40] in front of straight people to avoid offending them [41].

        1.2.2.3.2. Pro:
        Straight people who overhear important LGBTQ+ exclusive conversations often feel the need to interject and hijack conversations by "straightsplaining [42]".

        1.2.2.3.3. Con:
        It would be beneficial for straight people to hear these stories to broaden their worldview and foster empathy for the struggles that LGBTQ+ people go through.

      1.2.2.4. Con:
      Treating gay bars as sacrosanct spaces for queer empowerment oversells [43] the depth of connection people make there.

      1.2.2.5. Pro:
      Living in a cisheterosexual society can force many people to conform to gender norms they feel are oppressive; gay bars offer a space to reject [44] these, which can be liberating.

    1.2.3. Pro:
    Gay bars are important spaces to find potential partners, especially given the sometimes lethal harm [45] that LGBTQ+ people are subjected to after hitting on straight people.

      1.2.3.1. Con:
      Gay men are more likely to find romantic and sexual partners [46] through dating apps than gay bars; diluting the crowd of gay bars with straight patrons thus has little effect on people's dating success.

        1.2.3.1.1. Pro:
        A survey of 537 single gay and bisexual men [47] found that first generation dating apps have far outpaced bars as the most promising way to find a hookup.

        1.2.3.1.2. Con:
        While dating apps may help people find romantic and sexual partners, LGBTQ+ friendly spaces (like gay bars) provide a non-judgemental and friendly place to physically meet them and spend time together.

        1.2.3.1.3. Con:
        Online LGBTQ+ spaces are increasingly being infiltrated by heterosexual people. For example, many straight people use Grindr, a hook-up app designed for gay men, to make friends [48].

        1.2.3.1.4. Pro:
        The decline [49] in gay bars is in part due to the popularity of dating apps. Once a person finds someone through a dating app, they can meet them at an agreed-upon location, rendering gay bars obsolete.

      1.2.3.2. Con:
      In the beginning, there were few dating apps for lesbians, non-binary people, and other non-mlm [50] communities. As this is no longer the case [51], there is little need for gay bars.

      1.2.3.3. Pro:
      Gay bars empower LGBTQ+ people to pursue romantic relationships because they can assume attendees share their sexuality, unlike other bars. It would thus make it harder for LGBTQ+ people to find partners if straight people patron gay bars.

      1.2.3.4. Pro:
      Straight women often react poorly [52] when hit on in gay bars by sapphic women; such disgust plays into harmful stereotypes of predatory lesbianism [53].

    1.2.4. Con:
    Using a bar as a platform to empower a community often leads to exclusion; it is better for LGBTQ+ activism if gay bars are simply treated as bars.

      1.2.4.1. Con:
      Gay bars have a long tradition [54] of serving as meeting places for those seeking to achieve social and political change. Abandoning this history allows LGBTQ+ people to forget their radical history and grow complacent about protecting their rights.

      1.2.4.2. Pro:
      LGBTQ+ individuals who struggle [55] with alcohol abuse should not have to meet in a bar to feel empowered and assist their community's political advancement.

        1.2.4.2.1. Pro:
        Studies [56] have found that LGBTQ+ individuals abuse alcohol at higher rates than heterosexual individuals. This could be exacerbated by making gay bars central sites for organising community support and activism.

        1.2.4.2.2. Con:
        There are increasing numbers of "queer cafes [57]" for LGBTQ+ individuals to meet without drinking.

        1.2.4.2.3. Con:
        Events like "Queers Without Beers [58]" increasingly provide safe spaces for LGBTQ+ individuals without the expectation of having to drink alcohol.

      1.2.4.3. Pro:
      Elderly LGBTQ+ individuals or families with children may not have the time, resources, or interest in going to a gay bar to participate in activism.

        1.2.4.3.1. Pro:
        According to one study [59], 8 percent of all high school students in America report being LGBTQ+. They cannot attend gay bars because they are underage.

        1.2.4.3.2. Con:
        Gay bars do not have to empower all members of the LGBTQ+ community; even helping a small portion of the community can be useful.

      1.2.4.4. Con:
      Gay bars are not [60] the only places [61] that the LGBTQ+ community can meet in. Disempowerment would not be a concern, if these spaces were to close or become more accepting of straight people.

        1.2.4.4.1. Pro:
        Many cities have their own LGBTQ+ sports leagues [62], book stores [63], and film festivals [64], in addition to many more LGBTQ+ friendly places to meet.

        1.2.4.4.2. Con:
        Gay bars represent all LGBTQ+ spaces, so all issues affecting the former generalize to the latter (e.g., exclusion, safety). The closure [65] or de-segregation of gay bars may, therefore, set the precedent for similar change in all LGBTQ+ exclusive spaces.

  1.3. Pro:
  Gay bars that are popular among straight clientele often become uninviting to LGBTQ+ individuals, jeopardising their ability to express themselves.

    1.3.1. Pro:
    Straight people tend to behave badly [66] in gay bars which deters LGBTQ+ patrons from those places and harms the community.

      1.3.1.1. Pro:
      Drag queens and gay men often complain that straight women crash [67] LGBTQ+ scenes and act like inconsiderate cultural tourists.

        1.3.1.1.1. Pro:
        Bachelorette/hen parties [68] can often leer at gay men making out in clubs, making them highly uncomfortable.

        1.3.1.1.2. Con:
        Young women, some of whom are straight, are often major fans [69] of drag queens. Presuming they will be inappropriate at shows is reductive, and can deny gay bars a vital source of income.

      1.3.1.2. Con:
      Like straight people, LGBTQ+ individuals also misbehave and make others uncomfortable in gay bars, for example by being racist [70] or misogynistic [71].

        1.3.1.2.1. Pro:
        Even within the LGBTQ+ community, many lesbian women experience misogyny [72] in male-dominated spaces.

        1.3.1.2.2. Con:
        The fact that the LGBTQ+ community has issues does not negate the necessity of having autonomous spaces free of discrimination.

        1.3.1.2.3. Con:
        BIPOC queer spaces, like gay bars [73] and sex clubs [74], are increasingly common. In these instances, racist members of the community can be more easily excluded so that others can thrive.

      1.3.1.3. Pro:
      Straight men have been known to come to gay bars to harass [75] women.

        1.3.1.3.1. Pro:
        Straight men often treat sapphic women as curiosities [76] or entertainment [77], making lesbian bars unsafe for women.

        1.3.1.3.2. Con:
        This is not a phenomenon only seen in gay bars. Men also harass women [78] in straight bars, yet are still allowed into those venues.

        1.3.1.3.3. Pro:
        Allowing straight men in gay bars where they then harass [79] women encourages the view that women's sexuality is always for the pleasure of men, regardless of their partner preference (p. 8 [80]).

        1.3.1.3.4. Con:
        People in gay bars can call out harassment [81] or ask bouncers [82] to intervene. Such actions would create a healthier and more caring space than relying on exclusionary policies.

      1.3.1.4. Con:
      Only those misbehaving [83] should be asked to leave or banned. Excluding all straight people is not fair and could scare away potential allies.

      1.3.1.5. Pro:
      Straight patrons can make drag performers uncomfortable [84] by manhandling and making uncomfortable disclosures to them.

    1.3.2. Con:
    At a time when LGBTQ+ spaces are declining due to economic factors and changes in hook-up culture [85], the patronage of straight people can help to preserve these spaces.

      1.3.2.1. Pro:
      To minimise [86] harassment and complaints, gay bars historically opened in lower-income areas. As these places gentrify, they price out gay bars. Straight patronage can mitigate this.

        1.3.2.1.1. Pro:
        Historically, gay bars had to be discreet, so they were established [87] in scruffier parts of cities. As cities become wealthier, gay bars are squeezed out by rent increases.

        1.3.2.1.2. Con:
        Straight people do not need to be patrons to help keep a gay bar afloat. Instead, they could donate to such spaces to ensure they remain operational without their patronage.

      1.3.2.2. Pro:
      Many heterosexual and LGBTQ+ people are friends. Thus, both groups should be welcomed in gay bars to ensure their long-term economic viability.

        1.3.2.2.1. Pro:
        The Peel, a gay club which has the legal right to refuse entry to heterosexual people and lesbian women, has angered gay patrons as they feel that the actions of the club are discriminatory towards straight [88] and lesbian women.

        1.3.2.2.2. Pro:
        Gay bars cater to a much smaller [89] consumer base than a normal bar, and bars that are 'gay only' or 'lesbian only' cater to a small section of a minority group. This limits available profits.

        1.3.2.2.3. Con:
        When people want to socialise in mixed straight and LGBTQ+ friend groups, they can visit straight bars.

          1.3.2.2.3.1. Con:
          LGBTQ+ people may not feel safe being themselves in certain locations, and thus prefer to bring their heterosexual friends into bars they know are safe.

          1.3.2.2.3.2. Pro:
          Straight friends, regardless of their allyship, may have homophobic friends, family, or superiors. To accommodate their fear of being perceived as gay, LGBTQ+ people should meet their straight friends in straight bars.

      1.3.2.3. Con:
      If LGBTQ+ spaces are preserved by de-queering, their benefits for the community are lost.

        1.3.2.3.1. Con:
        Many gay bars have historical significance; any way of preserving [90] these spaces is better than none.

        1.3.2.3.2. Pro:
        Merely paying lipservice to the history of a gay bar does not preserve its ability to support the community; instead, it often serves as pinkwashing [91] as bars profit from using from the community whilst contributing little (p. 618 [92]).

      1.3.2.4. Con:
      New inclusive community spaces [93] that have more functions than just being a bar are increasingly common. This expansion can bring in more patrons, without jeopardising the bars' LGBTQ+ connections.

        1.3.2.4.1. Pro:
        LGBTQ+ bookshops [94] often host events to support the community. Merging these with gay bars, like other book bars [95], could maintain their history by ensuring their economic success.

        1.3.2.4.2. Con:
        Investing in multi-functional LGBTQ+ spaces could stop gay bars from shutting down [96]. However, there is no evidence that this will work; in contrast, widening the pool of clientele to include straight people is risk-free as it requires no additional investment.

      1.3.2.5. Pro:
      The commodification of queer identity and lifestyles can be incredibly useful [97] for normalising and empowering the LGBTQ+ community.

    1.3.3. Con:
    A selective door policy [98] may be sufficient to deter people from gay bars. Those who are dedicated could still attend with little hassle and bouncers could still have the option to turn away people if the straight-to-gay ratio becomes too skewed towards the former group.

      1.3.3.1. Pro:
      Patrons of gay bars who do not wish to associate with any straight customers are free to move on to other bars where straight customers are few in number or non-existent.

      1.3.3.2. Con:
      A selective entry policy in service of maintaining a good heterosexual-to-queer ratio in gay bars would still technically amount to not allowing cisheterosexual people into the bar.

    1.3.4. Pro:
    Many LGBTQ+ people will "act straight [99]" so they are not attacked [100] for being flamboyant [101]; gay bars are critical safe spaces for expressing authentic behaviours without hatred.

      1.3.4.1. Con:
      Not all LGBTQ+ people are flamboyant, and not all flamboyant people [102] are LGBTQ+. By including straight people who may like being flamboyant, gay bars can debunk the stereotypical association between this trait and being LGBTQ+.

      1.3.4.2. Pro:
      LGBTQ+ people who appear flamboyant in public have been verbally abused, chased, and shot dead by homophobes [103]. Gay bars allow LGBTQ+ people to express flamboyance, even if only for a short time period.

        1.3.4.2.1. Pro:
        LGBTQ+ people who are chased, followed, or stalked by homophobes could seek refuge in gay-only bars. Allowing straight people into gay bars would defeat this purpose, making them more vulnerable.

        1.3.4.2.2. Con:
        Flamboyance is often expressed by clothing or appearance [104]. Flamboyant LGBTQ+ people, therefore, still risk [105] harassment, abuse, or attacks on their way to a gay bar.

      1.3.4.3. Pro:
      In some LGBTQ+ people, flamboyance may be so ingrained that it cannot always be masked. These people should not have to act straight to avoid abuse, attacks, or death.

      1.3.4.4. Con:
      Straight people are not solely responsible for homophobic attacks on flamboyant gay boys and men. Indeed, non-flamboyant gay men have been known to invalidate their peers' experiences [106].

  1.4. Con:
  Including people of all genders and sexualities in gay bars would help further advocacy for LGBTQ+ causes.

    1.4.1. Pro:
    Segregation is counter-intuitive to the LGBTQ+ community's efforts to better integrate and be accepted by heterosexual society.

      1.4.1.1. Con:
      Self-segregation is a way for LGBTQ+ individuals to ensure they are safe [107]. It is wrong to expect LGBTQ+ individuals to place their safety at risk.

        1.4.1.1.1. Con:
        Self-segregation can make it easier for extremists to target the LGBTQ+ community, as demonstrated by the fact that LGBTQ+ bars are sometimes subjected to violent attacks [108].

        1.4.1.1.2. Pro:
        Many LGBTQ+ individuals face discrimination [109], harassment, and violence [110] in their daily lives, so they should be able to self-segregate into exclusive safe spaces.

          1.4.1.1.2.1. Pro:
          Many workplaces in the USA are hostile to LGBTQ+ people; 11-28% have experienced discrimination when it comes to promotions, and 1 in 10 have reportedly been fired for their sexuality (pp. 7-8 [111]).

            1.4.1.1.2.1.1. Pro:
            47% of transgender people in the USA report that they were fired or denied a job or promotion because of their gender identity (p. 11 [112]).

            1.4.1.1.2.1.2. Con:
            94% of American Fortune 500 companies include gender identity in their non-discrimination [113] policies.

          1.4.1.1.2.2. Pro:
          Homophobia is rife in schools; 75% of LGBTQ+ students report frequently hearing the word “gay” used negatively and nearly 60% reportedly experience sexual harassment (pp. 39-40 [114]).

          1.4.1.1.2.3. Con:
          Permitting cisgender heterosexual persons to enter LGBTQ+ spaces does not mean these spaces are no longer safe.

          1.4.1.1.2.4. Con:
          Patrons of gay bars often experience [115] violence [116] outside of the premises, rather than inside. Normalising that straight people attend such spaces may make homophobes more reticent to commit such attacks.

      1.4.1.2. Pro:
      Many heterosexual people are outspoken allies [117] of the gay community and can benefit the LGBTQ+ community with their support.

        1.4.1.2.1. Con:
        It is legitimate to be a heterosexual ally, while also recognizing the benefits of having a safe and exclusive space for LGBTQ+ people to meet and socialize.

        1.4.1.2.2. Con:
        LGBTQ+ individuals regularly interact with straight individuals in schools, places of work and in other social venues. It is in these spaces that advocacy can occur.

          1.4.1.2.2.1. Con:
          Many LGBTQ+ people may not be comfortable discussing their sexuality freely in schools and work (p. 40 [118]), resulting in straight people being unaware of the community's issues and how to interact appropriately with them.

          1.4.1.2.2.2. Pro:
          Individuals tend to go to bars to socialise and enjoy themselves, rather than to engage in meaningful and constructive dialogue about the various ways in which they may be discriminating against others. Gay bars are not the right place for such conversations.

        1.4.1.2.3. Pro:
        Excluding straight people from queer conversations inhibits their ability to provide support for important causes.

          1.4.1.2.3.1. Pro:
          Not all LGBTQ+ individuals can access the community. Thus, it is important that straight people are educated so that they can provide appropriate advice and support to marginalised queer people.

          1.4.1.2.3.2. Con:
          Excluding heterosexual people from gay bars does not mean that they would be excluded from all conversations pertaining to LGBTQ+ people and the community; awareness can be created through multiple forums.

        1.4.1.2.4. Pro:
        Many LGBTQ+ bars function as sites from which protest and activism [119] are organised. It is useful to include cisgender heterosexual people as they often have more sociopolitical power.

          1.4.1.2.4.1. Pro:
          When gay bars promote interactions between powerful straight people and LGBTQ+ individuals, they help create opportunities for the community.

          1.4.1.2.4.2. Con:
          Cisgender heterosexual people could be selectively allowed into gay bars on specific days when the community convenes for the purposes of activism. There is no need to generalize this across the board.

          1.4.1.2.4.3. Con:
          Cisgender heterosexual people may take undue credit [120] for their role in LGBTQ+ victories, so they should not be directly included in protests and activism.

      1.4.1.3. Pro:
      When gay bars discriminate against straight people, they embolden other business owners to discriminate against the LGBTQ+ community by creating [121] an us vs. them mentality [122], which slows down acceptance.

        1.4.1.3.1. Pro:
        As the LGBTQ+ community is already [123] discriminated against, there is no need to provide homophobes with another reason to do so.

        1.4.1.3.2. Con:
        Homophobic us vs. them mentalities predate exclusionary gay bar policies; the impetus to facilitate LGBTQ+ acceptance is on cisheterosexual people, who are more likely to be accepted in their day-to-day life.

    1.4.2. Con:
    There is no guarantee that LGBTQ+ rights will advance, rather than regress in the future. Therefore it is important that LGBTQ+ bars are maintained to protect these rights and people's ease of access to safe spaces.

      1.4.2.1. Pro:
      LGBTQ+ rights have been set back [124] in the past, as politicians leveraged homophobia to gain power [125].

      1.4.2.2. Pro:
      Assuming that progressive issues are settled leaves them vulnerable [126] to attack; keeping gay bars open means that LGBTQ+ people can continue to agitate for change and react quickly any possible attacks.

      1.4.2.3. Con:
      If LGBTQ+ rights are revoked, the most impactful site for political campaign is unlikely to be gay bars. They would instead take place at parliament or marches.

        1.4.2.3.1. Pro:
