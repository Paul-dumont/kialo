Discussion Name:
Dating the New Testament Bible Scrolls

1. Thesis:
The evidence available for dating the New Testament writings indicates an early authorship.

  1.1. Pro:
  All of Paul's letters would have been circulating prior to his death [1] between 62 and 64 AD.

    1.1.1. Con:
    This would only apply to the genuine Pauline epistles, and not the pseudepigraphical epistles. [2]

    1.1.2. Pro:
    The epistles credited directly to Paul are said to have been written even prior to the gospels [3].

    1.1.3. Pro:
    Paul was most active in his missionary work between 50 and 58 AD [4], so that is likely when he wrote the seven epistles that are credited to him.

  1.2. Con:
  The gospel of Matthew used the gospel of Mark as a source, which was written between 66 and 74 AD [5], so it must have been written some time later.

    1.2.1. Pro:
    Many passages in Matthew and Mark [6] are similar.

    1.2.2. Pro:
    The gospel of Matthew shows evidence of how much antagonism (pp. 1-2 [7]) there was between the Christians and the Jews which would have taken time to develop.

    1.2.3. Pro:
    The gospel of Matthew shows evidence of a much more developed ecclesiology than Mark [8], which would have taken time to develop.

  1.3. Pro:
  The Gospels were written before the fall of Herod's Temple in 70 AD [9].

    1.3.1. Pro:
    Such a terrifying and monumental event for 1st century Jews would have been evident in the writings, if the event had already occured.

      1.3.1.1. Con:
      If the gospel authors wanted to make them seem older than they actually were, they would have tried to exclude events that would clearly date them.

        1.3.1.1.1. Pro:
        The gospels show evidence of nodding to the reader in a self-aware way, leaving some things implicit, as in the case of the parenthetical "let the reader understand" (Mark 13:14, Matt 24:15).

      1.3.1.2. Pro:
      Three of the four gospels contain predictions about the destruction of Jerusalem [10], so the authors would have been incentivized to document the fulfillment of these prophecies.

        1.3.1.2.1. Con:
        The gospels contain ex eventu prophecies of the destruction of Jerusalem which date them to later than that event.

          1.3.1.2.1.1. Pro:
          In the parable of the wedding banquet (Matt 22:1-14 [11]), the enraged king sends his troops to burn the city of those who declined his invitation to his son's wedding banquet (22:7). This is very plausibly a direct allusion to the burning and destruction of Jerusalem in 70CE, framed as a punishment on the Jews for rejecting Jesus.

          1.3.1.2.1.2. Pro:
          In Matt 24:15 [12], Jesus says, "So when you see the desolating sacrilege standing in the holy place, as was spoken of by the prophet Daniel (let the reader understand)." This is a clear allusion to the destruction of the temple in 70CE.

    1.3.2. Con:
    The gospels show a development of theology and Christology that would have taken more time to develop than the 40 years between the crucifixion and the destruction of Jerusalem.

      1.3.2.1. Pro:
      The Christology of the early Jerusalem Church--whose intellectual successors would be called the Ebionites or Nazarenes [13]--was adoptionism, in which Jesus is a human adopted by YHWH at his baptism or resurrection, but not before, and never coequal with God the Father.

  1.4. Con:
  The early dating is contingent on who is attributed with authorship.

    1.4.1. Con:
    As the gospels were all originally written anonymously [14] with the traditional attributions only added later, the traditional attributions cannot be used to justify an early dating.

    1.4.2. Pro:
    The authors of the gospels claim to be eyewitnesses [15], so they were Jesus' contemporaries, so they likely died a few decades after the death of Jesus in AD 33.

      1.4.2.1. Pro:
      Matthew was martyred in AD 60 [16], so his gospel had to have been written prior to this date.

      1.4.2.2. Pro:
      Mark was martyred in 68 AD [17].

      1.4.2.3. Con:
      John the Apostle died only in AD 100 [18], so this would imply a late authorship.

      1.4.2.4. Con:
      St. Luke died between 84 and 100 AD [19], a time period that followed the destruction of the temple.
