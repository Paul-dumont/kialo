Discussion Name:
Should the US Pay Reparations for Slavery?

1. Thesis:
The US should pay reparations for slavery.

  1.1. Pro:
  The US has a collective moral responsibility [1] to pay reparations to those harmed by the legacy of slavery.

    1.1.1. Pro:
    There exist significant domestic and international precedents for paying reparations for other past grievances. Therefore, the US must also pay for slavery.

      1.1.1.1. Pro:
      The US has historically given similar compensation or special treatment to other mistreated groups, like Japanese Americans [2] and Native Americans. Not paying for slavery implies that it was not as bad as those other crimes.

        1.1.1.1.1. Pro:
        Some slave owners, like those in the District of Columbia [3], received compensation for economic harms incurred due to the abolition of slavery. The slaves themselves, however, did not receive anything.

          1.1.1.1.1.1. Con:
          Compensated emancipation [4] was one of the policy methods used by the US to hasten the end of slavery. This tatic was devised to quell the worries of those who opposed abolition, not to reward slave owners.

        1.1.1.1.2. Con:
        Native Americans were reimbursed for land, the rights to which were often determined via treaties [5]. Thus, there is a legal foundation for reparations, whereas in the case of slavery, there is not.

        1.1.1.1.3. Pro:
        Under the 1988 Civil Liberties Act, the US government apologized for Japanese American internment during World War II and provided reparations [6] of USD$20,000 to each survivor.

        1.1.1.1.4. Pro:
        Native American tribes have received compensation [7] for lands they were forced to cede to the US government in various treaties.

          1.1.1.1.4.1. Con:
          The fact that legislation was made in the past does not mean [8] that it was justified. Otherwise, the fact that slavery was previously legal would provide justification for the institution of slavery.

          1.1.1.1.4.2. Pro:
          The reparations received by Native American tribes were significant. For example, USD$963 million [9] and 44 million acres of land were given to Alaskan Natives.

        1.1.1.1.5. Con:
        The Indian Claims Commission failed to deliver meaningful legal or financial resitution to Native American tribes (p. 215 [10]).

      1.1.1.2. Con:
      In most past cases, compensation payments by the US went to living victims of injustices. Reparations would be paid to descendants of victims who were not directly affected.

        1.1.1.2.1. Con:
        Slavery reparation may more closely resemble the Black Lung benefit program [11], were payments were made to widows for harm inflicted by the government to individuals.

        1.1.1.2.2. Con:
        The living relatives of serious crime victims are often given additional compensation for harms [12] that affect them, including trauma or other mental health issues [13].

        1.1.1.2.3. Pro:
        It would be immensely difficult to prove that monetary damage extends to someone several generations later, even in civil litigation.

          1.1.1.2.3.1. Con:
          There are legal precedents [14] that have helped descendants of victims reclaim art looted by Nazis.

          1.1.1.2.3.2. Pro:
          The existing legal frameworks provide a right to reparation and restitution for those wronged [15] by a specific wrongdoer.

            1.1.1.2.3.2.1. Con:
            In such cases, the state is typically understood to be the wrongdoer. There is sufficient evidence to support the claim that Black Americans have been financially damaged through structural inequalities [16] created by government policies.

        1.1.1.2.4. Pro:
        These reparations were paid to the victims of internment, not to their descendants or all ethnic Japanese in the United States. There are no longer any remaining direct victims of slavery; accordingly, this case does not make for a valid comparison.

      1.1.1.3. Con:
      The US has never paid reparations to the victimized populations in its colonies [17] or in countries that it has invaded or interfered in.

        1.1.1.3.1. Con:
        Reparations for slavery are meant to foster reconciliation among American citizens. Reparations to non-Americans are a different category, as these people are not part of American society.

        1.1.1.3.2. Pro:
        The application of US legal frameworks for compensating civilian populations victimized by its colonialism and warfare is inconsistent. The frequent failure to compensate states and civilians indicates that there is a lack of political will to do so.

          1.1.1.3.2.1. Con:
          The US has a customary obligation to compensate the families of civilians harmed in lawful combat, known as 'solatia'. There are several examples of this being successfully applied, such as in the Balkans (p. 3 [18]).

          1.1.1.3.2.2. Pro:
          The Foreign Claims Act [19] often ascribes arbitrary value to human life and suffering. As such, the US can be seen as both ambivalent and uncaring in its assessment of how its actions impact the lives of victims.

        1.1.1.3.3. Pro:
        In the case of Vietnam, the US refused to compensate the state and its civilians, despite comitting multiple atrocities. In fact, it demanded Vietnam to pay back over USD$140 million of debt incurred by the former Republic of Vietnam [20].

      1.1.1.4. Pro:
      Existing legal and moral frameworks imply that the US has a legal responsibility to pay reparations.

        1.1.1.4.1. Pro:
        In the 18th century [21], former slaves successfully sued for compensation. Therefore, there is some legal precedent to hold companies accountable for holding slaves.

          1.1.1.4.1.1. Con:
          The US legal system is generally opposed [22] to holding specific companies responsible for historical injustices.

          1.1.1.4.1.2. Pro:
          One such proposal could allow private business to make elective reparations to satisfy this responsibility (p. 1291 [23]). In turn, this may leverage support for reparations from otherwise disinclined [24] demographics.

        1.1.1.4.2. Con:
        Following this logic, individuals should be responsible for crimes that their family members commit, even if they are not in any way involved.

          1.1.1.4.2.1. Con:
          Just as people's children inherit their property and debts, so too do they inherit the debts owed to them.

          1.1.1.4.2.2. Pro:
          If a man commits a crime but somehow manages to avoid serving time in prison, the jail sentence can't be imposed on his children after he dies.

          1.1.1.4.2.3. Con:
          People today would not pay reparations because they are directly 'responsible' for the crimes of slavery, it is because they have benefitted from an oppressive political system. This logic holds no bearing on criminal responsibility.

        1.1.1.4.3. Con:
        Prior to the 13th Amendment [25], slavery was not illegal [26] in the US. Thus, there is no legal foundation for compensating the descendants of slaves for the actions of their ancestors.

          1.1.1.4.3.1. Con:
          The reason why descendants of slaves are owed reparations is precisely because slavery was legal, and therefore endorsed by the government.

          1.1.1.4.3.2. Con:
          Japanese internment camps were legal [27] at the time they were used; yet, the United States still compensated affected Japanese Americans.

          1.1.1.4.3.3. Pro:
          Ex post facto law is incompatible [28] with the US Constitution. Any attempt to retroactively criminalize actions that were legal when committed would undermine the integrity of the US legal system.

      1.1.1.5. Pro:
      Other rich countries have paid reparations for similar crimes.

        1.1.1.5.1. Pro:
        Australia [29] has started paying reparations for members of the 'Stolen Generation [30]'.

        1.1.1.5.2. Pro:
        Japan has paid hundreds of millions of dollars in reparations [31] for its colonial crimes, including compensation for women from other Asian countries who were exploited as sex slaves [32] before and during World War II.

        1.1.1.5.3. Pro:
        In 2013, New Zealand agreed to pay reparations [33] for its treatment of the Maori people some 150 years earlier.

        1.1.1.5.4. Pro:
        The Canadian government has created a USD$2 billion fund to compensate [34] Indigenous communities for the loss of their language, culture, and history as a result of residential school programs.

        1.1.1.5.5. Con:
        Plenty of countries who have committed atrocities (i.e. Britain [35] and Belgium [36]) refuse to pay reparations.

        1.1.1.5.6. Con:
        Forcing the US to act based on international precedent would constitute a violation of its national sovereignty. As such, the US alone should decide whether reparations are justified.

          1.1.1.5.6.1. Con:
          The US is bound by international law [37] to recognize reparations as an appropriate means of redress for the historical human rights abuses incurred by its practice of slavery.

    1.1.2. Con:
    The US did not start the global slave trade [38] and, therefore, has no individual moral obligation to rectify its harms.

      1.1.2.1. Con:
      Based on this logic, the perpetrators of all kinds of other injustices would be free from responsibility as their ideologies and tactics all have historical precedents.

      1.1.2.2. Pro:
      It was not long after gaining independence that the US took steps to leave the slave trade with the Slave Trade Act of 1794 [39].

        1.1.2.2.1. Con:
        A large proportion of America's slaves were born into slavery. Between 1808 and 1860, after the abolition of the trans-Atlantic slave trade, the population of slaves increased [40] domestically from 1.1 to 3.9 million.

        1.1.2.2.2. Con:
        However, the US perpetuated the system long after [41] most other nations had ended the practice.

          1.1.2.2.2.1. Pro:
          Most northern states abolished slavery at an earlier date [42] than the South. This shows that it was possible for the US to deal with and oppose the practice.

            1.1.2.2.2.1.1. Con:
            In the years preceding the Civil War, the North became markedly more industrialized [43] and urbanized than the South. Thus, slavery died out in the North because it no longer benefited northern society.

          1.1.2.2.2.2. Pro:
          In fact, the slave population [44] in the US skyrocketed in the 19th century, while other powers were in the process of abolishing slavery.

          1.1.2.2.2.3. Con:
          The system became much more concentrated in the 19th century, with the percentage [45] of slave-holding households decreasing from 36% to 25% between 1836 and 1860.

      1.1.2.3. Pro:
      If it had not been for local tribal and cultural warfare [46], many people would not have been sold into slavery.

      1.1.2.4. Con:
      Slavery was one of the cornerstones of America's rise to become the biggest economy in the world.

        1.1.2.4.1. Con:
        Slavery being one of the cornerstones has been criticized for ignoring [47] economic realities and a lack of quantitative substantiation. As such, it exaggerates the economic benefits of slavery.

          1.1.2.4.1.1. Con:
          Although exact estimates vary, most scholars estimate [48] the total value of slave labor to be well into the trillions of dollars.

          1.1.2.4.1.2. Pro:
          Slavery stifled [49] innovation, diversification, and trade networks.

          1.1.2.4.1.3. Pro:
          While slavery-driven industries have been estimated [50] to have contributed about 1 to 1.5% of the US' GDP at its high point, much of this growth did not trickle down to most Americans. Rather, it enriched the most privledged.

          1.1.2.4.1.4. Con:
          At least 15 major US corporations [51], among them Lehman Brothers and AIG, have directly profited from slavery. These companies have become some of the biggest in the world.

        1.1.2.4.2. Pro:
        The North continued to facilitate and profit [52] from slavery in the South, even after its own decision to abolish slavery.

        1.1.2.4.3. Pro:
        The development of the South [53] — and the United States as a whole — was heavily driven by the use of slave labor.

          1.1.2.4.3.1. Pro:
          At the time of the Civil War, southern plantations produced most of the world's cotton [54]. Cotton production was vital [55] to the southern United States' growth.

            1.1.2.4.3.1.1. Pro:
            As the price for a slave was between £16 and £45 [56] (in current value) until the mid-18th century, there was a cheap supply of labor that allowed for wide-scale labor-intensive cash crop production.

          1.1.2.4.3.2. Pro:
          Slavery was used in public works, such as railroads [57] and waterways. Creating this public infrastructure —required for long-term growth— would not have been possible as extensively without slaves.

            1.1.2.4.3.2.1. Pro:
            The federal government used the labor of slaves, for example, to construct the White House [58] and the Capitol [59].

          1.1.2.4.3.3. Pro:
          Cheap labor allowed southern industries to compete against more efficient ones in the North and Britain. Without it, companies like the Tredegar Iron Company would have faced tougher competition and not grown as much (p. 18 [60]).

          1.1.2.4.3.4. Con:
          The economic and political power of the US stems from its growth as an international power, the result of a process of state-building that was not primarily spurred by slavery.

            1.1.2.4.3.4.1. Pro:
            The development of the US was primarily the result of the growing centralization of the American state [61] and the beginning of the era of American expansion and imperialism after the Civil War.

            1.1.2.4.3.4.2. Pro:
            The US' international power and control over global supply chains was the result of the two World Wars [62], where American territory faced no damage in contrast to the devastation inflicted upon the great European powers.

            1.1.2.4.3.4.3. Con:
            This state-building project were enabled by the immense economic growth produced by slavery, which created wealth in the South that rivaled the European superpowers [63].

          1.1.2.4.3.5. Con:
          The incredible post-war economic growth in the US was the result of industrialization [64]. The system of slavery likely inhibited economic growth [65] in the lead up by preventing southern investment in industry.

      1.1.2.5. Pro:
      The US inherited the practice of slavery from European nations. If a duty to pay reparation exists, then it should fall on those European states.

        1.1.2.5.1. Con:
        The fact that the US did not start the transatlantic slave trade does diminish its culpability for significantly participating in and perpetuating it.

        1.1.2.5.2. Con:
        Americans who had plantations did not always inherit [66] them from European masters. In many cases, they were passed down through naturalized American families, like Thomas Jefferson's plantation.

          1.1.2.5.2.1. Con:
          Thomas Jefferson's father, Peter Jefferson, was of European descent [67]. Therefore, the plantation is necessarily linked to some earlier European slave owner.

        1.1.2.5.3. Pro:
        Only 6% [68] of slaves taken from Africa were destined for British North America. The majority were taken to the Caribbean and South America.

        1.1.2.5.4. Pro:
        In contrast to pre-imperial forms of slavery, it was Portugal [69] that began the system of trading, selling, and shipping off slaves.

        1.1.2.5.5. Pro:
        The slave trade came to the US through the British [70], when they controlled the colonies.

    1.1.3. Con:
    The US has already taken actions that deal sufficiently with its history with slavery.

      1.1.3.1. Con:
      America continues to downplay the role that slavery played in its history today.

        1.1.3.1.1. Pro:
        The Antebellum period - the period between American Independence and the Civil War, particularly associated with Southern plantations - continues to be glorified [71] in the media and entertainment industry.

        1.1.3.1.2. Con:
        The United States supported the migration of free slaves to the African continent, as well as the founding of Liberia [72] as a colony to provide them a new homeland.

          1.1.3.1.2.1. Pro:
          The formation of the state of Liberia allowed former slaves a state of their own and self-determination [73].

          1.1.3.1.2.2. Con:
          The colonization of Liberia [74] was an attempt to rid America of freed slaves by placing them in an especially inhospitable area.

        1.1.3.1.3. Pro:
        America has over twenty [75] dedicated Holocaust museums (more than Israel, Germany, and Poland combined), yet only a handful [76] of museums are dedicated to the issue of slavery.

        1.1.3.1.4. Con:
        In 2008, the US federal government apologised [77] for the harm caused to Black Americans by slavery.

        1.1.3.1.5. Con:
        For most of the history [78] of the United States, the international slave trade has been illegal [79].

      1.1.3.2. Pro:
      The United States already bore a huge cost [80] in abolishing slavery through the Civil War.

        1.1.3.2.1. Con:
        The cost of the Civil War was nowhere near the economic benefits produced by slavery. Studies of the direct and indirect costs of the war estimate a cost around USD$21 billion (pp. 304, 308, 317 [81]).

        1.1.3.2.2. Con:
        The vast majority of Americans stood on the side of those fighting to abolish slavery. Excluding slaves, Union states had about three times [82] the population of Confederate states.

        1.1.3.2.3. Pro:
        More Americans died [83] in the Civil War than in any other war that the United States has participated in.

          1.1.3.2.3.1. Con:
          Fewer people died in the US Civil War than from slavery. In the Civil War, around 600,000 [84] died while there are estimates that put the death toll of slaves at more than one million [85].

          1.1.3.2.3.2. Pro:
          More than 200,000 fewer Americans died in World War II, the second-most [86] deadly conflict for America.

        1.1.3.2.4. Con:
        The Emancipation Proclamation was a strategic move [87], aimed at making it impossible for the British or the French to back the South, rather than a conscious effort to help slaves.

          1.1.3.2.4.1. Con:
          While slavery may not have been the immediate cause of the war, it was at the core of the disagreement [88] between representatives of the South and North in Congress.

          1.1.3.2.4.2. Con:
          Even if the Emancipation Proclamation was a strategic move, it fit in with the Republican Party's long-standing opposition [89] to slavery.

          1.1.3.2.4.3. Pro:
          As such, the abolition of slavery represented a material benefit to the North.

      1.1.3.3. Pro:
      Many white people helped slaves escape. Their descendants do not carry guilt.

        1.1.3.3.1. Pro:
        Many Quakers [90] played a role in the abolition of slavery. Their descendants are not likely to carry guilt.

        1.1.3.3.2. Pro:
        Abolitionist John Brown [91] was a noted 'conductor' of the Underground Railroad and established an organization called the League of Gileadites, helping runaway slaves escape to Canada.

        1.1.3.3.3. Con:
        The fact that some Germans, such as Oskar Schindler [92] or John Rabe [93], saved thousands of innocent civilians during World War II does not absolve the collective guilt of Germany for the Holocaust.

          1.1.3.3.3.1. Pro:
          These individuals were exceptions; the overwhelming majority of those who could have resisted decided not to.

          1.1.3.3.3.2. Con:
          It nonetheless demonstrates that support for historical crimes is never absolute. It is therefore unfair to collectively punish or ascribe guilt to everyone and their descendants.

      1.1.3.4. Con:
      The US government rescinded [94] its promise to redistribute plantation land to former slaves, denying them the opportunity to own property.

    1.1.4. Pro:
    The US caused tremendous suffering to slaves. States who commit crimes against humanity carry a responsibility to attempt to rectify those harms.

      1.1.4.1. Pro:
      Slaves were denied [95] the opportunity for education, apprenticeships, property, and assembly, in order to reduce the risk of rebellion.

      1.1.4.2. Pro:
      Slaves were often subjected to inhumane living and working conditions [96].

        1.1.4.2.1. Pro:
        Slaves working on plantations were often required to work [97] 15 to 16 hours per day.

          1.1.4.2.1.1. Con:
          Historically, these working hours are not extraordinary. Factory workers during the Industrial Revolution in Britain also worked 12 to 14 hours a day [98] or more.

        1.1.4.2.2. Pro:
        The squalid conditions in which slaves were housed contributed to dire health issues, often due to the spread of severe infections and diseases [99].

        1.1.4.2.3. Con:
        The living conditions of domestic slaves were generally much more humane [100] than those afforded to slaves who worked the plantations.

      1.1.4.3. Con:
      The conditions of slavery were better in North America than elsewhere, especially in other parts of the Americas.

        1.1.4.3.1. Pro:
        Corporal punishment was much more widespread in the British West Indies (BWI) than in the South of the US (p. 12 [101]).

        1.1.4.3.2. Pro:
        The death to fertility rate [102] in other parts of the Americas was so abysmal that additional slaves needed to be brought in from Africa to sustain the local population. In contrast, the US slave population grew without resorting to supplementation.

        1.1.4.3.3. Con:
        The fact that practices elsewhere were more egregious does not make the treatment of slaves in the United States any less cruel or worthy of redress.

      1.1.4.4. Pro:
      Slave owners inflicted immense physical and sexual [103] violence on slaves.

      1.1.4.5. Con:
      States cannot always redress the wrongs of the past, even in cases where there were mass violations of human rights. The sole moral obligation of the US is to ensure that this never happens again.

    1.1.5. Con:
    Neither states nor nations can have moral responsibilities.

      1.1.5.1. Pro:
      Many people, including the significant white working class during slavery, played no significant part in the crimes committed by elites. As such, moral responsibility for those crimes does not lie with them.

        1.1.5.1.1. Pro:
        Not all US citizens owned slaves or supported slavery while the insitution was still legal. America as a whole, therefore, cannot be at fault.

          1.1.5.1.1.1. Pro:
          Plantation owners owned more slaves and gained more significant financial advantages from slavery than poor Southerners did. In North Carolina, slave-owning families accounted for only 27% of all families (p.14 [104]) .

          1.1.5.1.1.2. Con:
          This majority of US citizens profited passively from slavery and did not actively oppose it, thereby sanctioning its existence.

            1.1.5.1.1.2.1. Con:
            Some non-enslaved Americans were indirectly harmed by slavery, since they were unable to compete against farms and enterprises [105] which unfairly benefited from slave labor.

              1.1.5.1.1.2.1.1. Con:
              Small farmers often rented slaves, still contributing to the system of slavery (p. 32 [106]).

            1.1.5.1.1.2.2. Pro:
            Despite the abolition of slavery in the North, northern states still benefited [107] from southern slavery.

          1.1.5.1.1.3. Con:
          In 1860 [108], almost one-third of all free households owned slaves. That number is likely higher in some states.

          1.1.5.1.1.4. Pro:
          Throughout the history of slavery in the US, the South accounted for the vast majority [109] of all slaves held.

        1.1.5.1.2. Con:
        States are responsible for the actions of individuals too. This counts as collective responsibility.

          1.1.5.1.2.1. Con:
          States are not liable for the actions of individual citizens who act outside of the established social contract [110].

          1.1.5.1.2.2. Pro:
          States can regulate the harmful actions of individuals through the criminal justice system [111]. If many individuals continue to commit crimes, the state is also at fault.

            1.1.5.1.2.2.1. Pro:
            Instead, the American state used its enforcement capacities to aid and sustain [112] the practice of slavery.

        1.1.5.1.3. Con:
        Many white people accept [113] that their privileged position was accrued through racial inequality. This suggests that a portion of the US is feeling a collective guilt.

      1.1.5.2. Con:
      A prerequisite for collective guilt is a strong identity link to the perpetrator population (p. 252 [114]).

        1.1.5.2.1. Con:
        Americans often fail to identify as a collective due to the prevalence of individualism [115] in their moral ecology. As such, a truly collective guilt cannot take hold.

        1.1.5.2.2. Pro:
        Americans identify strongly with the Founding Fathers who owned slaves (p. 1 [116]).

        1.1.5.2.3. Con:
        Individuals who resisted historical crimes, like abolitionist John Brown [117], have become lionized in their cultures, demonstrating that people associate their country with those who resisted.

        1.1.5.2.4. Pro:
        Such strong identity links imply that a country's culture has refused to understand and condemn the actions of the oppressors and continue to base their society around the same principles [118].

  1.2. Con:
  Paying reparations would cause economic harms to all Americans.

    1.2.1. Con:
    Reparations would economically empower the Black community by improving their access to high-quality education, housing, and healthcare.

      1.2.1.1. Con:
      The money that would be used to fund reparations can be better spent on systemic changes which would improve the lives of Black Americans to an even greater extent.

        1.2.1.1.1. Pro:
        The adoption of criminal justice [119] reforms may provide a better form of redress for past wrongs to Black Americans than reparations.

          1.2.1.1.1.1. Pro:
          Black Americans are disproportionately affected by police discrimination and more likely be killed [120] by police.

          1.2.1.1.1.2. Con:
          The use of excessive force by police predominantly occurs in Black neighbourhoods which experience high rates of actual poverty, income inequality, and segregation [121].

          1.2.1.1.1.3. Pro:
          Race-neutral reforms [122], like welfare and criminal justice reform, would prevent the impression of an unfair distribution of benefits, which might further deepen the rifts in American society.

        1.2.1.1.2. Pro:
        A functioning and generous social safety net [123] and other redistributive policies [124] will be more important to disadvantaged minorities than a one-off payment of reparations.

          1.2.1.1.2.1. Pro:
          Black Americans are the most likely [125] racial group in the US to be living in poverty. Therefore, more general welfare programs such as housing assistance and food stamps disproportionally help them.

          1.2.1.1.2.2. Pro:
          Schools districts with a majority Black American student population are generally underfunded [126] by state and local authorities. Funding these schools would alleviate economic disparities.

            1.2.1.1.2.2.1. Pro:
            Educational attainment is a strong driver [127] of socio-economic mobility. Improving education could, therefore, empower disenfranchised communities to transcend their inherited class [128].

            1.2.1.1.2.2.2. Con:
            Increasing funding for schools which are largely segregated [129] by race may exacerbate racial inequality, rather than solving it.

          1.2.1.1.2.3. Pro:
          President Biden's initiatives to reduce overall poverty are explicitly designed to combat racial disparities [130] in income. These render reparations unnecessary.

          1.2.1.1.2.4. Con:
          Since the US is a capitalist [131] state, it is unlikely to authorize a widescale redistribution of wealth.

            1.2.1.1.2.4.1. Con:
            Capitalist welfare provisions, such as those based on individual merit, are still more likely to provide proportionate financial relief to Black Americans as they are more likely to live in poverty [132] than others.

            1.2.1.1.2.4.2. Pro:
            Financial inequality is an essential [133] feature of free market capitalism [134].

            1.2.1.1.2.4.3. Pro:
            The equitable redistribution of wealth is a socialist [135] principle. Given the unpopularity [136] of socialism among the US public, it is unlikely that this policy will have popular support.

            1.2.1.1.2.4.4. Con:
            The US is not a strictly capitalist state. In addition to its mixed economy [137], programs such as Medicaid [138] demonstrate its willingess to use social welfare in order to prevent rampant inequality.

          1.2.1.1.2.5. Con:
          Social programs designed to financially benefit disadvantaged communities have often excluded Black Americans.

            1.2.1.1.2.5.1. Pro:
            The American movement for maternalist reform was intended for white mothers only (p. 2 [139]) . Black single mothers, on the other hand, were simply excluded.

            1.2.1.1.2.5.2. Con:
            The American Rescue Plan (ARP) has reduced [140] poverty rates amongst Black children by around 30%.

            1.2.1.1.2.5.3. Pro:
            Black Americans were barred from the benefits of the 1944 G.I. Bill (pp. 1-2 [141]), further widening the economic gap between Black and white Americans.

            1.2.1.1.2.5.4. Pro:
            The primary beneficiaries of the New Deal [142] were white Americans [143].

        1.2.1.1.3. Con:
        Broadly speaking, systemic change is important but extremely difficult to implement. Furthermore, systemic changes are not mutually exclusive of reparations.

          1.2.1.1.3.1. Pro:
          Recalibrating the code of laws in any social system would require the removal and reformulation of thousands of pieces of legislation.

          1.2.1.1.3.2. Con:
