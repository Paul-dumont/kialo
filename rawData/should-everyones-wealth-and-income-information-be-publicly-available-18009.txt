Discussion Name:
Should everyone's wealth and income information be publicly available?

1. Thesis:
Everyone's wealth and income information should be publicly available.

  1.1. Con:
  The public knowledge of everyone's income information will have a negative effect on the wider economy.

    1.1.1. Con:
    Transparency [1] improves market function. By providing additional information, financial registries can equip markets with this transparency.

      1.1.1.1. Pro:
      Transparency is very important to investors, who require information to make informed, rational decisions [2]. By providing that information universally, financial registries improve access for a range of investors.

        1.1.1.1.1. Con:
        While smaller liquidity traders prefer transparency, large-scale institutional investors prefer less - it is thus not clear that increased liquidity will lead to increased investment (p. 125 [3]).

      1.1.1.2. Pro:
      Consumers value transparency highly [4] and are therefore more likely to participate in a transparent market.

        1.1.1.2.1. Con:
        Consumers may prefer a more transparent market, but consumer preference is not necessarily a driver of market efficiency.

      1.1.1.3. Con:
      Market function depends on the transparency of corporations and other institutions; increased transparency with respect to personal wealth does not necessarily impact that.

    1.1.2. Con:
    Wealth transparency would enable taxation of wealth, rather than income, increasing revenues for the government.

      1.1.2.1. Pro:
      Increased revenue from taxed wealth would aid the redistribution of wealth from the richest to the poorest individuals.

        1.1.2.1.1. Pro:
        Taxation is one of the most direct [5] mechanisms available to redistribute wealth more equitably in the short term.

        1.1.2.1.2. Pro:
        Taxation can fund a social safety net, which is a very effective way of reducing wealth inequality (p. 2 [6]).

      1.1.2.2. Pro:
      The taxation of wealth would easily offset any government cost of managing and enforcing the disclosure of this information by private citizens.

      1.1.2.3. Con:
      Increased taxation can often result in capital flight, as wealthy people legally protect their assets. This can be harmful as it can reduce consumption and investment within an economy.

        1.1.2.3.1. Con:
        A good deal of capital flight (particularly capital flight for the purpose of tax avoidance) is illegal [7].

        1.1.2.3.2. Con:
        Tax treaties [8] that tax the flow of capital between countries can ensure that it is not profitable to move wealth simply for the sake of tax avoidance.

        1.1.2.3.3. Con:
        The vast majority of middle class and moderately wealthy people do not have large enough assets to make wealth movement worthwhile. They would, therefore, still pay a higher level of taxation.

        1.1.2.3.4. Pro:
        The Panama Papers [9] demonstrated that wealthy people tend to move their money abroad to avoid paying tax on it.

        1.1.2.3.5. Pro:
        The theory of the Laffer Curve [10] states that if tax rates rise too much then the actual level of income collected by governments falls due to the fact that people move their money abroad (or engage in other practices) to minimise their tax paid.

          1.1.2.3.5.1. Con:
          The Laffer Curve model depends on regression analysis that mixes federal and state levels, even though the model was supposed to explain state tax rates (p. 2 [11]).

          1.1.2.3.5.2. Con:
          The Laffer Curve has not been supported by fiscal policy since its formulation: large tax cuts such as the Bush tax cuts have not lead to increased revenue (p. 2 [12]).

        1.1.2.3.6. Pro:
        The economies of Argentina and India [13] suffered due to capital flight.

    1.1.3. Pro:
    The public knowledge of everyone's income information can play a disruptive economic role by increasing pressure to redistribute wealth.

      1.1.3.1. Con:
      The relative share that different proportions of the economy have, as well as the general extent of wealth and income inequality [14], are already fairly well known. There is thus no reason to think there would be a major shift in the public’s demand for wealth redistribution.

        1.1.3.1.1. Con:
        Being able to connect individual names, faces, and identities to the broad social phenomena that we see personalizes the data in a way that aggregate information does not. It is reasonable to think that renewed resentment may come with this personalization.

          1.1.3.1.1.1. Con:
          Deriving information about a whole group from properties of individual members commits the fallacy of composition [15].

        1.1.3.1.2. Pro:
        Increased pressure to redistribute wealth does not mean it will necessarily lead to rapid reform. Despite increased pressure to target the richest in society [16], radical reforms in wealth redistribution remain uncommon.

          1.1.3.1.2.1. Con:
          This particular analysis is based on information in the Forbes list, which presents wealth in an overwhelmingly positive light. A more critical presentation of wealth is more likely to lead to demands for change.

          1.1.3.1.2.2. Pro:
          Despite the massive wealth [17] of Amazon CEO Jeff Bezos, Amazon has been continuing the widely publicized [18] mistreatment of their workers [19].

      1.1.3.2. Con:
      Americans already want [20] a much more equal distribution of wealth than currently exists. Such pressure is therefore already present.

      1.1.3.3. Con:
      The pressure to redistribute wealth should not necessarily be assumed to be a bad thing.

        1.1.3.3.1. Pro:
        Income and wealth transparency could create great social pressure on the richest, with fear of embarrassment leading to increased charitable contributions and benefits for all of society.

          1.1.3.3.1.1. Pro:
          There is already growing pressure [21] on the wealthy to increase charitable giving. Greater public knowledge of individuals' income and assets would likely exacerbate this.

          1.1.3.3.1.2. Pro:
          Some wealthy individuals are trying to lead by example [22] in showing how charitable contributions can help those less fortunate than themselves.

          1.1.3.3.1.3. Con:
          The immensely unequal distribution of global wealth is well-documented [23], and yet this embarrassment effect has not occurred.

            1.1.3.3.1.3.1. Con:
            The publication of information about the overall distribution of wealth is very different from the naming and shaming of individuals, which is more likely to produce a response.

              1.1.3.3.1.3.1.1. Pro:
              Celebrities such as Jimmy Carr [24] have publicly apologised and made donations in response to revelations that they avoided tax, even though they broke no laws.

                1.1.3.3.1.3.1.1.1. Con:
                Other individuals outed for the same behaviour, such as Lewis Hamilton [25], have not made donations or otherwise redistributed their wealth.

        1.1.3.3.2. Pro:
        Redistributing wealth, or at least addressing unethical income disparities, would be beneficial to society as a whole.

          1.1.3.3.2.1. Pro:
          Understanding the large disparity that exists between incomes would make people more supportive of policies that would act as a safety net [26] to benefit all.

            1.1.3.3.2.1.1. Pro:
            Insofar as people greatly underestimate the extent of wealth and income inequality [27], they are likely to underestimate both the need for social spending on behalf of the poor and the resources available through taxation to provide those needs. Greater financial transparency facilitates both realizations.

            1.1.3.3.2.1.2. Con:
            It is naive to assume that everyone has the same moral compass and that they'll choose to act on information in the same way.

            1.1.3.3.2.1.3. Pro:
            Finland and Norway do better [28] than most of Europe when it comes to income inequality, indicating that this policy [29] might be beneficial.

          1.1.3.3.2.2. Con:
          According to public choice theory [30], which argues that economic self-interest is the driving force of politics, redistribution tends to benefit those with the power to set spending priorities more than it helps those in need.

            1.1.3.3.2.2.1. Con:
            Public choice theory tends to lead to undersupport for public goods that provide broad benefit. This suggests that public choice theory is not a good lens through which to evaluate public policy (pp. 1145-1148 [31]).

            1.1.3.3.2.2.2. Con:
            Political choice involves comparing incommensurable goods. For example, the proposed policy involves balancing concerns about privacy, fairness, property rights, and equality among others. Public choice theory tends to do a poor job of modelling choices between incommensurable goods (p. 1145 [32]).

            1.1.3.3.2.2.3. Pro:
            Public choice theory can provide a powerful explanatory model for tax policy (p. 361 [33]). It, therefore, makes sense to incorporate public choice theory into evaluating proposed changes that would affect tax policy, such as a financial registry.

          1.1.3.3.2.3. Pro:
          Wealth inequality, which is on the rise, is an issue of major public concern [34].

            1.1.3.3.2.3.1. Pro:
            Economic inequality has a range of negative social consequences [35].

              1.1.3.3.2.3.1.1. Pro:
              Economic inequality is linked to poorer health [36] outcomes, including higher rates of infant mortality.

              1.1.3.3.2.3.1.2. Pro:
              Economic inequality increases criminality [37].

              1.1.3.3.2.3.1.3. Con:
              Negative social consequences will likely still exist in part since economic inequality is difficult to eradicate [38] entirely.

              1.1.3.3.2.3.1.4. Con:
              It is argued that inequality can stimulate market growth and thus actually increase equality in the longer term (p. 7 [39]).

            1.1.3.3.2.3.2. Con:
            Public concern is often rooted in self-interest [40]. Simply because a policy is in the interests of some members of the public does not necessarily make it a good one.

      1.1.3.4. Pro:
      The redistribution of wealth can be regarded as a disruptive economic force since it interferes with the natural market's self-regulation [41] by the invisible hand [42] of the market.

        1.1.3.4.1. Con:
        The idea of the invisible hand is a fictional construct describing ideal market conditions. It does not reflect real world conditions. [43]

      1.1.3.5. Pro:
      A certain degree of wealth inequality can be beneficial, as wealth amongst the highest earners will likely produce a trickle-down effect [44].

        1.1.3.5.1. Con:
        The validity of trickle-down economics is widely disputed [45].

        1.1.3.5.2. Pro:
        It is more important [46] to reduce poverty than wealth inequality. A wealthy upper class can play a role in reducing poverty, because they have excess money to spend on goods and services, the purchase of which reduces overall poverty.

        1.1.3.5.3. Pro:
        -> See 1.1.2.3.5.

        1.1.3.5.4. Con:
        It would be better if the income was taxed and the state used it than to rely on the trickle-down effect of wealth inequality.

          1.1.3.5.4.1. Pro:
          A private investor will likely invest in profitable endeavours rather than ones which are socially useful (e.g. building apartments and charging a high rent rather than building social housing).

          1.1.3.5.4.2. Pro:
          Comparatively, the state has a duty of care to its citizens so will likely engage in more socially useful projects.

          1.1.3.5.4.3. Con:
          States are regularly massively wasteful [47] with taxpayer money.

  1.2. Con:
  Increased knowledge of others' financial status is likely to lead to an increase in social unrest.

    1.2.1. Con:
    However intuitively plausible it may seem, it is actually quite difficult to connect [48] income inequality to social unrest.

    1.2.2. Pro:
    Increased financial transparency can help to form class consciousness, which is a major driver of social unrest.

      1.2.2.1. Pro:
      A study [49] of unrest in Latin America found that citizens who saw themselves as part of an increasingly strong middle class were more likely to act against perceived inequalities compared to the social elite.

        1.2.2.1.1. Con:
        The same study [50] found that income inequality in and of itself was not the principal driver of social unrest.

      1.2.2.2. Pro:
      According to classical conceptions [51] of class consciousness, class consciousness depends upon members of a class having a clear sense of their shared material situation in opposition to other classes. Income and wealth transparency can facilitate this knowledge.

      1.2.2.3. Con:
      There is no necessary connection between class consciousness and the publication of financial details.

        1.2.2.3.1. Pro:
        Class consciousness has to do with an understanding of group dynamics and relationships. But income and wealth registries provide information about individuals, not groups.

    1.2.3. Pro:
    Differences in socioeconomic status are already major drivers [52] of social stratification. Making those differences easy to identify runs the risk of further exacerbating this stratification.

      1.2.3.1. Pro:
      Adolescent measures of health are closely tied [53] to perceptions of socioeconomic status. Increased transparency about relative socioeconomic status can be expected to exacerbate the inequity this entails.

      1.2.3.2. Con:
      In order to be a major driver of social stratification, lots of people would have to take the time to look up the incomes of other people, which can be time-intensive. Most people will have neither the time nor interest to do so.

      1.2.3.3. Pro:
      When two people know the disparity between their wealth, it can pose serious challenges [54] to their friendship.

        1.2.3.3.1. Pro:
        Even without explicit information about one another's income or wealth, there are many barriers [55] to interclass friendships. Consequently, people's friends already tend [56] to come from one's own socioeconomic group.

      1.2.3.4. Con:
      Given that that social stratification on the basis of socioeconomics is already so pronounced, with people already making judgements on the basis of where someone lives, the clothes they wear and so on, it is unlikely that the proposed policy could have too pronounced an effect.

        1.2.3.4.1. Con:
        While it is true that people already make judgements based on the perception of wealth, knowing exactly how wealthy someone is will only serve to aggravate and intensify this. This is especially likely to be true if such wealth turns out to be much greater than previously thought.

      1.2.3.5. Pro:
      There is stigma against people who are long-term unemployed [57]. If income is public, people can see if they are unemployed and that their main source of income is social welfare.

        1.2.3.5.1. Pro:
        The stigma of having to apply for government support programmes when unemployed can impact a person's self-confidence [58].

        1.2.3.5.2. Pro:
        Many people believe that if someone has failed to find a job for over a year that they are lazy [59]. They ignore the other factors affecting unemployment such as stigma from employers or a lack of jobs.

          1.2.3.5.2.1. Pro:
          Managers [60] tend to hire someone who is currently employed but with no relevant experience over someone who has been unemployed for a long time, even if that person has superior credentials.

        1.2.3.5.3. Con:
        The creation of a culture of greater financial transparency by this policy could help to defuse the stigma surrounding long-term unemployment by encouraging frank and open conversations about financial issues.

          1.2.3.5.3.1. Pro:
          A reduction in the levels of stigma and secrecy surrounding private financial matters might make it easier for long-term unemployed or otherwise struggling individuals to seek help.

    1.2.4. Con:
    Since their actual wealth would be visible to the public, people would not feel the need to 'compete' with those around them by buying material things to make themselves appear wealthier.

      1.2.4.1. Pro:
      Given that overconsumption is one of the main drivers [61] of climate change, reducing conspicuous consumption is a valuable goal.

      1.2.4.2. Pro:
      Societal pressures due to that visibility would cause people to save more and spend less, leading to more fiscally responsible activity.

      1.2.4.3. Con:
      Decreasing the incentive to buy material things would not necessarily be a positive thing, as industries producing "material things" still provide employment as well as enhancing the economy.

      1.2.4.4. Con:
      To strangers or upon first introduction, a person's wealth would not be immediately known, and therefore people would still buy expensive things.

    1.2.5. Pro:
    Coworker relations will likely become more toxic, as they struggle to justify the pay disparity by comparing day-to-day activity.

      1.2.5.1. Con:
      When coworkers know one another's salaries it does not usually lead to a toxic situation.

        1.2.5.1.1. Pro:
        Shared knowledge of salaries gives workers' power [62] as they have more information (and therefore leverage) in wage negotiations.

        1.2.5.1.2. Pro:
        When coworkers know one another's salaries, they can negotiate together. Indeed, it tends [63] to make struggles to end wage inequalities more effective.

      1.2.5.2. Pro:
      Performance can be difficult to measure and isn’t observable to everyone. The knowledge of each other's individual pay, therefore, can trigger social comparison [64].

        1.2.5.2.1. Pro:
        Employees are not always aware of the complex decisions that determine [65] how someone is paid.

      1.2.5.3. Con:
      Pay disparity should be justified. This will enable coworkers to know exactly why a colleague is being paid more or less than them. This should reduce resentment at what might be seen as unfair benefits another may be getting.

  1.3. Pro:
  This policy would make it much harder to carry out criminal or unethical actions.

    1.3.1. Con:
    Public knowledge of individuals' net worth would likely lead to those who were elderly or vulnerable being targeted by criminals who view them as soft targets.

      1.3.1.1. Pro:
      For example, an employee working as a carer [66] for a vulnerable or elderly individual may take advantage of their condition, trust, or generosity.

        1.3.1.1.1. Pro:
        A care worker [67] in Hertfordshire, UK, was found guilty of stealing over £100,000 from elderly victims in her care.

        1.3.1.1.2. Pro:
        A live-in [68] carer stole over £2000 from an elderly man in his care in Berkshire, UK.

        1.3.1.1.3. Con:
        Caretakers are already well positioned to have a sense of their client's relative level of prosperity.

      1.3.1.2. Con:
      There are already unique forms of crime [69] specifically directed towards the elderly on account of the traits they possess as a social group.

        1.3.1.2.1. Pro:
        Elderly people find it harder to report crime and are more likely to not detect things [70] such as discrepancies in their account statements.

        1.3.1.2.2. Pro:
        5.4% of older adults experience [71] some form of fraud or scam each year since criminals already believe the elderly to be a more susceptible class.

        1.3.1.2.3. Con:
        Even if there are already specific forms of crimes directed at the elderly, making it easier to exploit elderly people is not a good thing.

      1.3.1.3. Con:
      Older people are already generally associated with wealth as they are perceived as having lived their lives, paid off their debts, and free of the financial commitments of the younger generation.

        1.3.1.3.1. Pro:
        The baby boomer generation is already seen as wealthy [72] and privileged.

        1.3.1.3.2. Con:
        Just because someone is not living hand-to-mouth does not mean that they are wealthy. It is likely that most people understand this.

    1.3.2. Pro:
    Increased wealth and income transparency would help prevent political and governmental corruption.

      1.3.2.1. Pro:
      Politicians could not hide or lie about their financial interests when running for office or when making policy decisions.

        1.3.2.1.1. Pro:
        There is substantial evidence that Donald Trump has exaggerated [73] his personal wealth in order to convince the American public that he is a good businessman. If all wealth and income information were publicly available, this would have been difficult if not impossible to do.

          1.3.2.1.1.1. Con:
          Trump is an exception to most political norms. His actions are not reflective of a typical politician.

            1.3.2.1.1.1.1. Con:
            Even if Trump’s actions aren’t reflective of typical politicians, his conduct normalizes these actions and shows that they are possible for future political leaders.

            1.3.2.1.1.1.2. Pro:
            Even with his exaggerations taken into account, Trump is likely considerably wealthier [74] than any other president in the history of the United States. His propensity to secrecy (and probable misrepresentation of his wealth) is reflective of his public persona as a flashy real estate mogul more than as a politician.

              1.3.2.1.1.1.2.1. Con:
              While Trump may have been an anomaly among billionaires in 2016 when he won the presidency, he was not the first billionaire to make a credible run [75] for the presidency, and several [76] others followed suit in 2020. His actions are relevant to understanding their actions.

            1.3.2.1.1.1.3. Pro:
            Trump was already famous as a television personality and celebrity reality television character [77] prior to running for the presidency. There is nothing atypical about that style of celebrity concealing their wealth.

        1.3.2.1.2. Pro:
        Politicians can currently refuse [78] to release their tax returns and other financial information, making it difficult to account for their finances and therefore to know whether their financial interests are influencing their actions.

          1.3.2.1.2.1. Pro:
          Secret financial interests can help politicians get away with insider trading [79], whereby their political actions serve to enrich themselves.

          1.3.2.1.2.2. Pro:
          Financial secrecy may facilitate lobbying [80] behind closed doors, giving wealthy donors disproportionate political influence.

            1.3.2.1.2.2.1. Pro:
            It is currently very difficult to track money spent by lobbyists on elections. A public financial registry would, however, allow for indirect tracking because it would force individuals to disclose where their wealth is going.

              1.3.2.1.2.2.1.1. Pro:
              Because spending on political campaigns (a key part of lobbying) has been defined [81] by the United States Supreme Court as a protected form of speech, which has opened the way for virtually limitless dark money [82] to flow into political campaigns, it is almost impossible to track political spending directly.

            1.3.2.1.2.2.2. Con:
            There already exists dedicated legislation for the regulation [83] of lobbying.

              1.3.2.1.2.2.2.1. Con:
              Because they focus narrowly [84] on political donations above a certain amount, current lobbying regulations fail to make the financial interests of politicians transparent to the same extent as would be achieved by this policy.

            1.3.2.1.2.2.3. Con:
            Lobbying is an important and necessary part of the legislative process.

              1.3.2.1.2.2.3.1. Con:
              Even if some form of lobbying may be necessary, it does not follow that the ease in which wealthy donors can use lobbying to influence politicians for their benefit is desirable. It is this aspect of lobbying that is facilitated by financial secrecy.

              1.3.2.1.2.2.3.2. Pro:
              Lobbying helps [85] to provide important information to politicians and to assist them in comparing the various interests of their constituents.

                1.3.2.1.2.2.3.2.1. Con:
                The informational and educational work that lobbyists do can just as easily be done in an open, transparent way as in a secretive way.

            1.3.2.1.2.2.4. Con:
            In the US, lobbying is often done by funding super PACs [86], and these donations are given to organisations rather than individuals so they would not be disclosed.

        1.3.2.1.3. Con:
        Even without wealth and income transparency, politicians' financial interests can still be known or speculated as often information about political donations is also available to the public [87].

          1.3.2.1.3.1. Con:
          While information about political donations is available to the public, other forms of income and wealth are not. This still allows politicians to hide financial information that is potentially pertinent to the public.

        1.3.2.1.4. Pro:
        Journalists and the media would have increased access to information that can help them check the financial interests of politicians.

      1.3.2.2. Pro:
      Close monitoring of income and assets will make it more difficult for state funds to shift into private hands [88] without being detected.

      1.3.2.3. Pro:
      Scrutiny of party donors would help identify where politicians have vested interests in outside companies or institutions, which may skew political outcomes [89].

      1.3.2.4. Con:
      In developing nations where corruption [90] is a major problem, governments are unlikely to enforce such a policy since those individuals whose wealth has been accumulated illegally often occupy positions of power.

        1.3.2.4.1. Pro:
        Where leaders have great personal stakes in their countries' economies, such as in autocratic states and harsh military or oligarchical regimes, making wealth information public may come with grave political risks.

          1.3.2.4.1.1. Pro:
          Autocratic leaders with strong stakes in their countries’ economies will have a strong incentive to make sure such public disclosures do not happen.

          1.3.2.4.1.2. Pro:
          The Prime Minister of Pakistan was forced to resign and was later imprisoned after courts ruled [91] that information contained in the Panama papers [92] showed that he had deliberately under-represented his wealth and was therefore unfit for office.

          1.3.2.4.1.3. Con:
          There are a number of beneficial policies which autocratic leaders would oppose. Just because illiberal states could not implement this policy does not make it a bad policy.

            1.3.2.4.1.3.1. Pro:
            For example, many illiberal states restrict freedom of speech and freedom of the press to keep the regime stable (p. 2 [93]). This does not make these things inherently bad.

      1.3.2.5. Con:
      Transparency may not always be ideal. Not all information that goes through governmental bodies is for the public to scrutinize and critique.

        1.3.2.5.1. Con:
        Transparency may not be ideal for information related to national security, where enemies of the state may use the information in a negative way. But this has little relationship to transparency with respect to the personal income of political figures and government employees.

        1.3.2.5.2. Pro:
        Government actions that are covert or sensitive in nature rely on a freedom to not be answerable to the broad public. Having wealth and income information public makes it more difficult to keep this sensitive information out of public knowledge.

          1.3.2.5.2.1. Con:
          The United States federal government [94] and all state governments [95] already publish employee salaries. Clearly, they already have mechanisms in place to conceal the exact nature of spending on covert projects.

          1.3.2.5.2.2. Pro:
          Since payroll is a large part of every operation, publication of government employee salaries can give a good indication of where covert spending might be taking place.

          1.3.2.5.2.3. Con:
          Governments will still be able to keep information covert by claiming this spending is for different things and that these employees work in other areas.

            1.3.2.5.2.3.1. Pro:
            The government already regularly places covert employees in other agencies or attached to other projects. Concealing spending would be no different.

    1.3.3. Pro:
    Terrorist and criminal financing often involves bank transfers, due to the large sums of money involved (p. 16 [96]). Publicly declared income can highlight certain irregular incomes such as these.

      1.3.3.1. Pro:
      By monitoring the movement of funds, wealth registries would facilitate tracing the source of financing for terrorism and organized crime. As these financers generally have a strong interest in not being identified, increased scrutiny is likely to have a strong deterrent effect on providing that backing.

        1.3.3.1.1. Pro:
        The Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) has identified monitoring financial activity as one of the chief ways to track and deter financiers of terrorism (p. 11 [97]).

      1.3.3.2. Pro:
      Anti-money laundering [98] regulations already use monitoring and reporting as a technique for fighting terrorism and organized crime. Expanding these monitoring and reporting requirements would further aid in this fight.

        1.3.3.2.1. Pro:
        One of the most effective [99] ways to combat money laundering is to scrutinize the records of where money is supposed to have come [100] from. Wealth registries will make this much easier for law enforcement to do.

        1.3.3.2.2. Con:
        Money laundering [101] is a well-known yet frequently practised technique used by criminals to make illicit income appear legitimate. Making incomes visible would drive up money laundering techniques and businesses.

      1.3.3.3. Con:
      Criminal and terrorist organizations will still be able to use untraceable forms of banking, such as cash or shell corporation [102] payments, to avoid this scrutiny [103].

        1.3.3.3.1. Con:
        Property registries [104] would likely make it much more difficult for the very wealthy to rely on mechanisms such as shell companies to hide their financial activities.

        1.3.3.3.2. Pro:
        Transactions could be paid to others through a trust or using a limited liability company to hide the origin of the payment [105].

      1.3.3.4. Pro:
      When the declaration of financial information is obligatory, it becomes harder to access profits from illegal goods or services since it is evident when spending habits don't match individuals' declared income. This makes it difficult for criminals to operate.

        1.3.3.4.1. Pro:
        Tax havens (jurisdictions where it is easy to hide money) are an essential part of how criminal organizations maintain banking secrecy and shield themselves [106] from judicial activity. Wealth and income registries would eliminate the ability to use tax havens to maintain banking secrecy.

        1.3.3.4.2. Pro:
        The reason why organized criminals are often ultimately brought down by tax evasion charges [107] is that there is an obvious discrepancy between their consumption and their reported, licit earnings. Making earnings more transparent would further faciliate this mechanism.

        1.3.3.4.3. Con:
        Many people who are paid under the table do so without any criminal intent but simply because those are the only jobs they can find. Such a policy would disproportionately target and harm them.

          1.3.3.4.3.1. Pro:
          The shadow economy [108] in the US is estimated to be about ten percent of the size of the US economy overall. It thus represents a huge part of a society’s overall economic activity.

            1.3.3.4.3.1.1. Con:
            A wealth and income registry would not eliminate this economic activity. Rather, it would help to bring the shadow economy out of the shadows.

          1.3.3.4.3.2. Con:

        1.6.1.1.4. Pro:
        People can make approximations on someone's income based on their spending patterns (e.g. where they live, where they shop etc.).

          1.6.1.1.4.1. Con:
          Even if credit card and bank information can be used to approximate someone's incomes, that could be done only by the very small subset of people with access to this information, not society as a whole.

        1.6.1.1.5. Con:
        The proportion of the public who work at institutions which provide them with access to personal financial data is extremely small.

      1.6.1.2. Con:
      There are regulations [225] that manage how companies and financial agencies use the personal information they may possess. These regulations allow us to enjoy as much financial privacy as possible. A wealth registry would effectively eliminate these regulations.

        1.6.1.2.1. Con:
        Companies frequently violate [226] privacy regulations already.

          1.6.1.2.1.1. Con:
          Laws should not be made based on actions which violate laws. Just because financial privacy is not always respected, does not mean that it should not exist.

    1.6.2. Pro:
    Information privacy [227], including the collection and dissemination of personal financial data, is recognized as an important fundamental human right across the world.

      1.6.2.1. Pro:
      The UN Declaration of Human Rights, among other international and regional treaties, recognizes a right to privacy [228] as key to protecting human dignity and other values such as freedom of association and freedom of speech.

        1.6.2.1.1. Pro:
        Exposing individuals' wealth or income in the US would be a violation of the Financial Privacy Act [229], 1978.

          1.6.2.1.1.1. Con:
          Even if a wealth registry isn't possible by current law, it can easily be facilitated by enacting new laws.

      1.6.2.2. Pro:
      Protecting financial privacy is key to protecting a person's freedom of association and freedom of speech, because revealing their financial data would implicitly disclose the person's relationships and actions.

      1.6.2.3. Con:
      Any individual's right to privacy must be balanced [230] against the interests of the state in protecting the general social welfare.

      1.6.2.4. Con:
      The right to information privacy is not absolute, and there are many legal exceptions recognized.

        1.6.2.4.1. Pro:
        -> See 1.6.2.3.

        1.6.2.4.2. Pro:
        Few rights are absolute and many are often violated by the state, including at times the right to life (e.g. the death penalty, police use of firearms). Considering that the right to life is more important than the right to privacy, then if life is not an absolute right, privacy should not be one.

        1.6.2.4.3. Pro:
        An individual's right to privacy [231] is already revoked if a crime is committed. It's not a crime to generate wealth, but if that wealth is ill-gotten and the beneficiary is found to have committed a crime, then information pertaining to the crime becomes public knowledge.

    1.6.3. Con:
    While the publication of one's financial information may be a gross invasion of privacy for most people, that concern is less relevant to the wealthy individuals who have consistently hidden money and, in doing so, evaded paying into the public purse and thereby harmed society.

      1.6.3.1. Pro:
      -> See 1.3.4.4.1.1.

      1.6.3.2. Pro:
      -> See 1.3.4.4.1.2.

      1.6.3.3. Pro:
      -> See 1.3.4.1.

      1.6.3.4. Pro:
      -> See 1.3.4.4.1.

      1.6.3.5. Con:
      Rights [232] are generalIy taken to apply irrespective of consequentialist considerations. If there is a right to privacy, it should be similarly universalizable.

    1.6.4. Pro:
    There are numerous legitimate reasons why a person might not want others to know the amount or source of their income.

      1.6.4.1. Pro:
      There are many reasons why people who have excess wealth would not want this to be public knowledge.

        1.6.4.1.1. Con:
        The interests of the excessively wealthy do not necessarily line up with the social good. There is no reason, therefore, to think that the interest of the excessively wealthy to keep knowledge of their wealth out of the public should be honored.

        1.6.4.1.2. Pro:
        Charities, fundraisers, and those seeking donors to private causes often target [233] individuals with high net worth.

          1.6.4.1.2.1. Pro:
          Making wealth information publicly available makes it easier for groups seeking donors to identify them. This ease of access could lead to individuals feeling targeted or harassed from multiple directions (p. 36 [234]).

          1.6.4.1.2.2. Con:
          There is nothing wrong with worthwhile causes reaching out to individuals who are well positioned to support those causes.

        1.6.4.1.3. Pro:
        Individuals with a high net worth may wish to keep this fact private for security reasons [235], reducing their risk of kidnapping or robbery.

        1.6.4.1.4. Pro:
        Individuals with sudden financial windfalls, such as lottery winners [236], may find themselves targeted by scammers or unscrupulous family members.

          1.6.4.1.4.1. Con:
          The winners of large lotteries make up only a tiny fraction of society. Their interests should therefore be regarded as far outliers and not reflective of broader social interest.

          1.6.4.1.4.2. Con:
          It is already the case that lottery winners find themselves targeted, as many places have laws [237] preventing lottery winners from remaining anonymous.

            1.6.4.1.4.2.1. Con:
            The existence of some current bad privacy laws should not be used to justify more. Many other countries do [238] currently allow lottery winners to remain anonymous.

      1.6.4.2. Pro:
      Some forms of income and wealth carry social stigmas.

        1.6.4.2.1. Pro:
        There is a certain amount of social stigma [239] towards those who rely on the welfare system. Those benefitting from it may wish to keep this information private.

          1.6.4.2.1.1. Pro:
          -> See 1.2.3.5.

          1.6.4.2.1.2. Pro:
          Someone receiving a state allowance for medical reasons (e.g. a disability allowance) may want to keep their medical conditions private.

            1.6.4.2.1.2.1. Pro:
            Those with invisible disabilities [240] are often harassed or accused of 'scrounging' off the state. Making their status as welfare recipients public is likely to exacerbate this.

        1.6.4.2.2. Con:
        Working to eliminate biased social opinions would be a better remedy to the problem of stigmatisation than not utilizing an otherwise useful tool such as a wealth registry at all.

        1.6.4.2.3. Con:
        -> See 1.5.3.1.

        1.6.4.2.4. Pro:
        Certain forms of work, such as sex work [241] or adult entertainment [242], carry a social stigma. Individuals who work in these sectors have a right to anonymity.

      1.6.4.3. Con:
      The guide of government activity should not be what specific individuals want, whether those desires are legitimate or not. Rather, it should be what is in the best interest of society.

      1.6.4.4. Pro:
      Some individuals carry out investigative work [243] anonymously in order to protect their identities and preserve their access to sources of information.

      1.6.4.5. Pro:
      Being able to easily inquire the amount of wealth of everyone would exacerbate gender inequality in dating as well as economic inequality (pp. 101, 102 [244]): men with low or less wealth/income than others may not want [245] potential partners to know their wealth beyond [246] the already widely established wealth indicators and uses [247] regarding males [248].

      1.6.4.6. Con:
      In Norway [249], the source of people's incomes is not revealed, just their wealth, income, and taxes paid. Such a model could be used generally.

        1.6.4.6.1. Con:
        Knowing the amount and extent of someone’s wealth or income can provide indications of other features about their financial information, even if that information is not explicitly disclosed.
