Discussion Name:
Is school bad for your mental health?

1. Thesis:
School is bad for your mental health.

  1.1. Con:
  The causes of poor mental health are unrelated to schools.

    1.1.1. Con:
    Homeschooling has been shown to yield better mental health outcomes for students, suggesting that traditional schooling does play a role in causing or exacerbating mental health problems.

      1.1.1.1. Pro:
      Children who are homeschooled [1] are not required to engage in regular testing or homework, which could help them feel far less stress.

      1.1.1.2. Pro:
      Studies have shown that homeschooled students are at a reduced risk of developing mental health issues like anxiety and depression.

        1.1.1.2.1. Pro:
        A study [2] found that traditionally-schooled children experienced higher levels of depression and some types of emotional and behavioural problems than homeschooled children.

        1.1.1.2.2. Pro:
        A UK study conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic found that children with Special Educational Needs and Disabilities [3] who had attended school before the pandemic felt less anxiety and stress after switching to homeschooling.

        1.1.1.2.3. Con:
        During the COVID-19 pandemic, the mental health of adolescents in Austria declined considerably with the forced closure of traditional schools and the increased prevalence of homeschooling (pp. 3, 12 [4]).

          1.1.1.2.3.1. Con:
          The homeschooling situation that many students had to deal with during an unforeseen and unprecedented pandemic is not representative of homeschooling in normal times.

      1.1.1.3. Pro:
      Homeschooling can open routines up to becoming more flexible [5]; this form of bespoke scheduling might suit the individual student more optimally.

      1.1.1.4. Con:
      Children who are homeschooled may not have the same access to socialization [6] opportunities, which could result in them lacking vital social skills or support systems in the future.

        1.1.1.4.1. Con:
        Socialization is not an absolute good, and can lead to negative outcomes. Homeschooling helps children avoid [7] bullying and peer pressure.

        1.1.1.4.2. Con:
        There is little difference [8] between the social competencies of home-schooled and traditionally-schooled children.

        1.1.1.4.3. Pro:
        School usually brings lots of children in the same age group together under the instruction of a professional who is specially trained in child development. This develops and improves children's social skills.

          1.1.1.4.3.1. Pro:
          Those with poor social skills [9] have been shown to have higher levels of stress and loneliness. Therefore, developing social skills can help protect people's mental health.

          1.1.1.4.3.2. Pro:
          The school environment provides unique opportunities for children to learn and practise social skills - such as sharing, using eye contact, and resolving conflicts - under the supervision of a trained facilitator (p. 2 [10]).

          1.1.1.4.3.3. Con:
          Children are not taught social skills by other children of their age, but by their primary caregivers [11] at home.

            1.1.1.4.3.3.1. Con:
            While children may not learn social skills for the first time from peer interactions, such interactions provide vital social practice, and therefore consolidate what children have already learned at home from their primary caregivers.

      1.1.1.5. Con:
      The relationship [12] between a parent and their child is often a barrier to effective homeschooling, and can make homeschooling a very negative, stressful experience for children.

      1.1.1.6. Con:
      Children who are homeschooled may be more sedentary and less physically active [13] than traditionally-schooled children, which could have a negative impact on the former's mental health.

        1.1.1.6.1. Pro:
        Home-schooled children are less likely to play sports [14] than traditionally-schooled children.

          1.1.1.6.1.1. Pro:
          Specifically participating in sports as a child or adolescent is associated with [15] improved self-esteem and confidence, and reduced depressive symptoms.

          1.1.1.6.1.2. Con:
          This could be remedied by removing restrictions that prevent homeschooled students from participating in traditional school sports activities.

            1.1.1.6.1.2.1. Pro:
            In the US, homeschooled students are not [16] allowed to participate in traditional school sports activities in 10 states, and must complete lengthy administrative processes to do so in a further 20.

        1.1.1.6.2. Pro:
        Research [17] has shown that regular physical activity can increase self-esteem and decrease depression and anxiety in children.

        1.1.1.6.3. Con:
        Many of the past studies on physical activity and fitness levels in homeschooled and traditionally-schooled children have produced contradictory [18] results.

        1.1.1.6.4. Con:
        Researchers note that this could be remedied [19] by introducing health promotion strategies that encourage physical activity for homeschooled children.

    1.1.2. Pro:
    If schools were the cause of mental health difficulties, most people would be expected to have poor mental health, since most people attend or have attended school. In reality, far fewer people have poor mental health than those who attend school.

      1.1.2.1. Con:
      It is possible for someone to have poor mental health while attending school and recover from it later on in life.

      1.1.2.2. Con:
      Mental illness will become a reality for almost half [20] of all US adults at some point in their lives, a 2019 report noted.

      1.1.2.3. Pro:
      Poor mental health only affects around 25% [21] of the world's population, whereas 68% [22] of children complete primary school globally.

    1.1.3. Pro:
    There are other much more significant influences over young people's mental health than their schooling. It is these, and not school, that cause poor mental health.

      1.1.3.1. Con:
      The fact that other factors may be more significant does not negate the impact that schools have on young people's mental health.

      1.1.3.2. Pro:
      The rise of social media has fuelled information overload [23] and changes in interpersonal interaction [24], both of which are linked to increased incidence of mental health issues.

        1.1.3.2.1. Con:
        Some educators have begun weaving social media [25] into their teaching methods.

        1.1.3.2.2. Pro:
        The use of social media has been linked to depression and anxiety [26].

    1.1.4. Con:
    50% [27] of all lifetime mental illness begins by age 14. This may indicate that school is a common trigger of mental health problems.

      1.1.4.1. Con:
      The correlation between the two does not signify that one caused the other; mental illnesses are caused [28] by many factors that are unrelated to school, including genetics, viruses or infectious diseases, substance abuse, and traumatic experiences.

    1.1.5. Con:
    Teachers are much more stressed [29] than the rest of the workforce [30], suggesting that schools are definitely an aggravating factor in the mental health outcomes of those who spend a lot of time in them.

      1.1.5.1. Con:
      While at school, teachers and students have completely different experiences and expectations; school worsening teachers' mental health does not necessarily mean that schools are equally bad for students' mental health.

        1.1.5.1.1. Pro:
        Whereas teachers have several responsibilities [31] including preparing lesson plans and educating dozens of students, organizing departmental affairs, caring for students needs, and managing extra-curricular activities, students are only responsible for their own learning.

          1.1.5.1.1.1. Con:
          In addition [32] to their school performance, students are often responsible for competing for scholarships and awards, applying for college, working a job, and stabilizing [33] their home lives.

      1.1.5.2. Pro:
      Some studies [34] have found that the principal cause of stress among teachers is the management of students' mental health problems. Thus, if most teachers are highly stressed, this suggests that their students frequently have mental health difficulties, too.

      1.1.5.3. Pro:
      Educational charities have stated that stress has a negative impact [35] on an educator's ability to teach effectively, and creates a high-stress environment for the students they teach.

  1.2. Con:
  The education provided by schools equips students with skills and knowledge that set them up for better mental health in adult life.

    1.2.1. Pro:
    Schools teach students many transferable skills that help them succeed in their academic studies, careers, and personal life, indirectly bringing them immediate and longer-term mental health benefits.

      1.2.1.1. Pro:
      School teaches students critical thinking skills, which may prove to be highly useful later on in their careers and when resolving personal issues.

        1.2.1.1.1. Con:
        Schools often fail [36] to teach critical thinking skills.

        1.2.1.1.2. Pro:
        The development of critical thinking skills could help tackle depression [37], which is often fuelled by illogical, irrational thinking styles.

        1.2.1.1.3. Pro:
        Critical thinking skills make students more likely to innovate and problem solve, giving them a competitive advantage [38] in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) career fields.

      1.2.1.2. Pro:
      School attendance requires students to prioritise and organise [39] their workload, teaching them valuable time management [40] skills.

        1.2.1.2.1. Pro:
        Good time management [41] can help relieve stress.

        1.2.1.2.2. Con:
        Research suggests that children become better at time management if they are given less structure [42] to begin with, suggesting that school attendance may actually be inhibitory for the development of time management skills.

          1.2.1.2.2.1. Con:
          While the schoolday itself is structured, school attendance necessarily uses up a lot of free time, requiring students to independently structure their time outside of school so as to successfully manage homework, extra-curricular activities, and relationships.

      1.2.1.3. Pro:
      -> See 1.1.1.4.3.

      1.2.1.4. Con:
      In the US, schools do not [43] sufficiently prepare students for the pressures that come with university studies or careers, which can indirectly increase the risk of mental health problems.

        1.2.1.4.1. Con:
        While there are issues with public schools in the US, there are many private schools [44] available that specialize in preparing their students for success in college and their careers.

          1.2.1.4.1.1. Pro:
          The National Association of Independent Schools (NAIS) and Gallup report [45] indicates that students attending independent private schools outperform students from public schools in college.

            1.2.1.4.1.1.1. Con:
            Empirical evidence [46] indicates that students attending private schools do not outperform students attending public schools once differences in income is controlled for, indicating that private schools do not prepare students any better than public schools.

        1.2.1.4.2. Pro:
        A Norwegian study [47] of current and former university students found that those who had already dropped out reported a larger number of current mental health problems - and less access to support - than those who were still pursuing their university studies.

        1.2.1.4.3. Pro:
        Being unprepared for the pressures of a career may lead to unemployment, and studies indicate that unemployment significantly increases [48] the incidence of poor mental health.

    1.2.2. Con:
    Schools neglect many important aspects of the real world that are critical for students to develop into competent adults.

      1.2.2.1. Pro:
      Schools do not teach students critical skills required for everyday life [49] such as cooking, cleaning, sewing, or carrying out simple household repairs.

        1.2.2.1.1. Con:
        These life skills can be more appropriately taught at home, so it is unnecessary to use resources to teach students these skills at school.

        1.2.2.1.2. Pro:
        Lacking these life skills can lead to lower self-esteem and higher anxiety among students (p. 1821 [50]).

        1.2.2.1.3. Pro:
        In the UK, schools are increasingly unlikely to give students tactile general knowledge [51] in areas such as sewing or stitching, which is important for developing manual dexterity and solving practical problems.

      1.2.2.2. Con:
      School curricula can be expanded to include these aspects of the real world in addition to the current skills and subjects taught.

        1.2.2.2.1. Pro:
        Students sometimes study subjects or take electives [52] that have little relevance for them later in life. Integrating life skills into these classes, or replacing the classes altogether, could make school more relevant for students.

          1.2.2.2.1.1. Con:
          While some elective courses may seem unrelated to student success, evidence indicates that students enrolled in music classes [53] score better on major standardized tests.

          1.2.2.2.1.2. Pro:
          Very few students will be going into fields where calculus or geometry [54] are necessary tools of the trade; the vast majority will not need this information as adults.

        1.2.2.2.2. Con:
        Given that there are only a fixed number of schooling hours, it would be difficult to expand curricula to include more life skills without either sacrificing entire current subjects or the current depth of each subject.

          1.2.2.2.2.1. Con:
          The number of schooling hours varies [55] significantly around the world, suggesting that this is not a universally fixed number and can be changed.

      1.2.2.3. Pro:
      Schools fail to teach students how to navigate the workforce [56].

      1.2.2.4. Con:
      In practice, many schools do teach important practical and psychological life skills that students would not necessarily learn otherwise.

        1.2.2.4.1. Con:
        In the UK, religious education [57] is compulsory; it is debatable whether or not this subject matter could be considered an important life skill.

        1.2.2.4.2. Pro:
        In the UK, it is compulsory [58] for schools to have some teaching time dedicated to relationships, sex education, and health education, all of which provide vital knowledge for adult life.

      1.2.2.5. Pro:
      Schools do not teach students the knowledge and skills required to be financially literate, leaving them more likely [59] to have poor mental health as a result.

        1.2.2.5.1. Pro:
        In a large-scale international study by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), the majority [60] of 15-year-olds did not perform highly in financial literacy tests.

        1.2.2.5.2. Pro:
        Schools do not teach students [61] vital financial literacy skills, such as how to save, invest, and negotiate a salary.

        1.2.2.5.3. Pro:
        Schools neglect to teach students how to do their taxes [62], knowledge required of all adults.

        1.2.2.5.4. Con:
        As of 2020, 45 US states [63] included personal finance education in their state curricula.

          1.2.2.5.4.1. Con:
          The United States is just one country in the world; most other countries do not highlight financial literacy at school.

            1.2.2.5.4.1.1. Pro:
            According to an international report, only one-third [64] of the world's population over the age of 15 have good financial knowledge, suggesting that most of the world's schoolchildren are not taught financial literacy.

          1.2.2.5.4.2. Con:
          While the state curricula may allow it, it is not mandatory [65], thus not adequately assuring these classes may be taught or even being taught adequately.

    1.2.3. Pro:
    Some schools specifically run classes to introduce students to both the concept of mental health and practices they can adopt to maintain good mental health.

      1.2.3.1. Pro:
      Some schools teach their students about mindfulness [66] practices.

      1.2.3.2. Con:
      Most schools have yet to pursue comprehensive mental health initiatives (p. 7 [67]).

    1.2.4. Con:
    Most of the learning that sets students up for good mental health outcomes in adulthood is done while at university, not school.

      1.2.4.1. Pro:
      Most of the positive mental health effects associated with education are gained at the university level [68].

      1.2.4.2. Con:
      University students tend to have poorer [69] mental health than their non-student peers, suggesting that university does not facilitate good mental health outcomes overall.

      1.2.4.3. Pro:
      University, unlike school, teaches students to be self-motivated [70], a trait which has been linked to better mental health outcomes [71].

      1.2.4.4. Con:
      As school-level qualifications are usually a prerequisite [72] for attending university, school can be said to enable the mental health benefits gained while at university.

  1.3. Con:
  Schools often play a vital role in both identifying and mitigating child safeguarding concerns, which could otherwise have a disastrous effect on a child's mental health.

    1.3.1. Pro:
    Many schools have dedicated specialists in pastoral care [73] who are trained to identify and treat physical and emotional problems in students.

      1.3.1.1. Con:
      Pastoral care in schools tends to support students only [74] once they are in the midst of a crisis; therefore, this type of care is not always effective in mitigating budding or everyday mental health concerns.

    1.3.2. Pro:
    Schools offer vital practical support to many children living in poverty, whose physical and thus mental health might greatly suffer otherwise.

      1.3.2.1. Con:
      The existence of localised and temporary treatments for the symptoms of poverty can distract [75] from the systemic reasons behind such poverty, meaning governments feel less pressure to implement institutional changes to permanently resolve its root causes.

        1.3.2.1.1. Con:
        While increasing institutional complacency is an inherent risk in all local and charitable interventions to help impoverished people, such interventions are still needed as stopgaps [76]: larger-scale policies typically take a long time [77] to receive approval and then roll out.

      1.3.2.2. Pro:
      Many countries have programs which provide students with free or heavily subsidized school meals (p. iii [78]). If the children benefiting from these did not attend school, they would likely not eat.

        1.3.2.2.1. Pro:
        Malnutrition [79] can drive significant dips in academic achievement, physical health problems, and anxiety in children, all of which can increase the risk [80] of developing serious mental health issues in adulthood [81].

        1.3.2.2.2. Con:
        Foodbanks [82] can help provide for impoverished children who would otherwise not be fed.

          1.3.2.2.2.1. Pro:
          Foodbanks in the US feed more than 14 million [83] children per year.

          1.3.2.2.2.2. Con:
          Studies indicate that users of foodbanks often experience stigma and shame [84] surrounding their reliance on this service.

        1.3.2.2.3. Pro:
        When the COVID-19 pandemic stopped attendance of school around the world, thousands of children [85] were left without food. This demonstrates the crucial role schools play in food assistance for impoverished children.

        1.3.2.2.4. Pro:
        In the summer vacation periods when students do not attend school, the majority [86] of students who would usually rely on free school meals go hungry. This indicates that without attending school, impoverished children would be significantly more malnourished.

      1.3.2.3. Pro:
      Some students live in structurally dilapidated homes or degenerate neighbourhoods. In such instances, school provides a safe space and, crucially, an impetus for students to leave these unsafe spaces for a large portion of each week.

        1.3.2.3.1. Con:
        In the absence of school, sports clubs [87] and other activity groups [88] run by trained and trusted adults could provide safe spaces for vulnerable youth and identify specific safeguarding concerns.

        1.3.2.3.2. Pro:
        In 2015, millions [89] of schoolchildren in the US were living in neighbourhoods that parents believed were unsafe.

        1.3.2.3.3. Pro:
        Children living in hazardous, poor-quality housing are more likely [90] to have emotional and behavioural problems.

      1.3.2.4. Con:
      There are other institutions that can identify and support children in poverty.

        1.3.2.4.1. Con:
        There is no stigma involved in attending school, whereas there could be for the availing of other services.

          1.3.2.4.1.1. Con:
          The stigma of accessing these other services could [91] and should be dismantled.

          1.3.2.4.1.2. Pro:
          Stigma [92] can make someone's mental health issues worse, or it could stop them from seeking the help they need through mental health services.

          1.3.2.4.1.3. Pro:
          -> See 1.3.2.2.2.2.

        1.3.2.4.2. Pro:
        The primary aim of many international [93] and local [94] charities is to end child poverty by providing support for those in need.

        1.3.2.4.3. Pro:
        In some countries, state-funded social services [95] may provide assistance to families in poverty.

          1.3.2.4.3.1. Pro:
          In the UK, state-funded services such as the National Health Service [96] (NHS) often provide support to vulnerable young people and children in need.

          1.3.2.4.3.2. Con:
          According to UNICEF, two-thirds [97] of children worldwide are not assisted by social protection services.

      1.3.2.5. Pro:
      In the US, Community Schools [98] offer a wide range of practical services for impoverished families and their children, such as counselling, healthcare, food donations, childcare signposting, and extra tuition.

    1.3.3. Con:
    There are government and charity organisations whose sole purpose is to protect children from abuse and neglect. Schools are not necessary to this end.

      1.3.3.1. Con:
      If the end goal is to reduce child safeguarding violations, then the more available avenues to provide the most effective coverage, the better.

      1.3.3.2. Con:
      Teachers play a key role in recognizing and reporting child abuse (p. 8 [99]).

        1.3.3.2.1. Pro:
        There are many stories [100] of teachers being the first or only adults in a child's life to recognise [101] and report [102] their abuse.

          1.3.3.2.1.1. Pro:
          Teachers represent a very significant proportion [103] of all those who report child abuse to higher authorities and support services; child abuse has gone more unreported [104] during the COVID-19 pandemic because teachers have not been as able to have regular in-person contact [105] with vulnerable students.

        1.3.3.2.2. Pro:
        There are a number of school jurisdictions around the world that require mandatory reporting [106] of child abuse.

          1.3.3.2.2.1. Con:
          Despite mandatory reporting requirements, most school districts focus on the original definition of severe abuse, ignoring the minor and moderate cases of abuse [107] and neglect.

          1.3.3.2.2.2. Con:
          Mandatory reporting leads to over reporting, which leads to unsubstantiated claims and investigations which can tear families apart unnecessarily, causing further psychological damage [108] to the young children.

          1.3.3.2.2.3. Pro:
          Mandatory reporting increases the number of people paying attention to the warning signs [109] of child abuse, an overall net positive for child welfare.

          1.3.3.2.2.4. Pro:
          Legislature in the US makes schools liable [110] if they fail to prevent sexual harassment between students.

        1.3.3.2.3. Con:
        Some teachers [111] are too inexperienced or underconfident to recognise and report abuse.

          1.3.3.2.3.1. Con:
          School staff can be better trained in identifying the signs of teacher-to-student abuse so that they feel more confident in promptly reporting abusive colleagues.

          1.3.3.2.3.2. Pro:
          In a UK survey, almost 80% [112] of teachers expressed a desire for more resources and training in handling child abuse allegations, suggesting they currently feel underconfident in their ability to respond effectively.

          1.3.3.2.3.3. Pro:
          Training is unlikely to fix the barriers to reporting abuse, because student victims are seldom taken seriously when they first speak up.

            1.3.3.2.3.3.1. Con:
            Some countries, like the UK [113], have launched helplines to support potential victims of sexual abuse in schools and ensure any allegations are taken seriously.

            1.3.3.2.3.3.2. Con:
            Initiatives to improve responses to student complaints of abuse committed by teachers could tackle ageism (p. 6 [114]), which is likely a common [115] barrier to professionals believing and validating students who come forward with abuse allegations.

            1.3.3.2.3.3.3. Pro:
            Teachers and schools [116] frequently [117] disregard [118] student reports of peer-on-peer abuse. It therefore seems likely that they also ignore student complaints of teacher-to-student abuse.

            1.3.3.2.3.3.4. Pro:
            In some cases where a teacher is the perpetrator of child abuse, other teachers who are aware of the abuse [119] choose not to report [120] it. In such instances, teachers perpetuate the abuse [121] carried out by other teachers.

        1.3.3.2.4. Pro:
        As trusted adults with whom students have frequent and prolonged contact outside of the home, teachers have a unique opportunity [122] to identify and report at-home abuse.

          1.3.3.2.4.1. Con:
          -> See 1.3.2.3.1.

          1.3.3.2.4.2. Pro:
          Some students [123] express that they are likely to tell teachers about non-academic problems because they trust them and feel they are approachable adult role models.

      1.3.3.3. Pro:
      Child protective services [124] respond to cases of child neglect and abuse and are responsible for ensuring the safety of children.

        1.3.3.3.1. Con:
        Oftentimes child protective services overstep their boundaries [125] and take children away from homes without sufficient hard evidence.

    1.3.4. Con:
    Sometimes, it is precisely when attending school that a child is not safeguarded and has poor mental health outcomes as a result.

      1.3.4.1. Con:
      The vast majority of perpetrators of physical and sexual abuse against a child are family members [126]. This suggests that abuse occurs more commonly at home, not at school.

        1.3.4.1.1. Pro:
        To this end, school can help separate victims from the perpetrators of their abuse for at least one-third of the day.

      1.3.4.2. Pro:
      In and around schools across the globe, 246 million [127] children per year experience gender-based violence, which is known to have terrible mental health effects [128].

      1.3.4.3. Pro:
      Children that are abused [129] by their teachers [130] can develop emotional, cognitive, and behavioral issues.

        1.3.4.3.1. Con:
        One study in Greece [131] demonstrated that it is possible for students to develop resilience after emotional abuse by teachers.

        1.3.4.3.2. Con:
        The abuse of students can be successfully mitigated [132] through better vetting, training, and monitoring of school staff.

          1.3.4.3.2.1. Pro:
          Child safety vetting can be expanded to include not only criminal histories, but also 'soft information [133]', which can help conclude whether a person is likely to be a threat to children or not.

          1.3.4.3.2.2. Con:
          Training and regulations to help mitigate the possibility of teachers abusing students are limited, as there is little desire [134] to rectify this issue and change the status quo.

        1.3.4.3.3. Pro:
        Students who are bullied by teachers often struggle [135] to form positive relationships with other educators, negatively affecting both their present and future academic progress and mental well-being.

        1.3.4.3.4. Pro:
        There are many cases of teachers emotionally and physically abusing students around the world.

          1.3.4.3.4.1. Pro:
          According to a study in South Korea, over 30% [136] of students reported having suffered physical or emotional maltreatment by a teacher at least once in the previous year.

          1.3.4.3.4.2. Pro:
          In South Africa, differently-abled students often face physical violence [137] at the hands of their teachers.

          1.3.4.3.4.3. Con:
          Claims that teachers abuse students are exaggerated and inaccurate.

            1.3.4.3.4.3.1. Pro:
            Many governments and school boards have "no touch [138]" policies that teachers are expected to follow, specifically to minimise the risk of physical abuse committed by teachers.

              1.3.4.3.4.3.1.1. Con:
              Some psychologists say that a complete lack [139] of physical touch by teachers is in itself abusive, as it harms the healthy brain development of students.

              1.3.4.3.4.3.1.2. Con:
              As of 2021, 64 countries [140] are yet to fully prohibit corporal punishment in schools.

                1.3.4.3.4.3.1.2.1. Pro:
                Studies indicate that children subject to corporal punishment at school have lower self-esteem and academic attainment (p. 13 [141]).

                1.3.4.3.4.3.1.2.2. Con:
                Societal acceptance of corporal punishment overall is declining [142] and support for corporal punishment at school is especially low (pp. 14-15 [143]), suggesting that it will become a decreasingly prevalent issue.

                1.3.4.3.4.3.1.2.3. Con:
                Children are often beaten [144] and humiliated in Lebanon by staff members, despite a decades-old ban on corporal punishment in schools.

            1.3.4.3.4.3.2. Pro:
            Figures reported by a UK teachers' union indicate that less than 2% [145] of sexual and physical abuse claims made against teachers by students are true.

              1.3.4.3.4.3.2.1. Con:
              As the source of this statistic is a teacher's union, it likely involves some form of bias in having absolved teachers from being sources of abuse.

            1.3.4.3.4.3.3. Con:
            Studies estimate that 4.5 million [146] current school students in the US have suffered sexual abuse at the hands of a teacher.

            1.3.4.3.4.3.4. Con:
            In an anonymous survey released in 2006, 45% [147] of the teachers who responded admitted to having bullied a student.

        1.3.4.3.5. Pro:
        Children who witness bullying perpetrated by teachers may feel encouraged [148] to become bullies themselves.

      1.3.4.4. Pro:
      At school, children are often involved in bullying, which has incredibly harmful mental health effects.

        1.3.4.4.1. Pro:
        A UNICEF survey of over one million children from around the world revealed that one-third [149] of 13- to 15-year-olds experience bullying at school.

        1.3.4.4.2. Pro:
        Experiences of school bullying can lead to anxiety, depression, and poor self-esteem [150] in victims.

        1.3.4.4.3. Con:
